Transformers

From seq2seq and attention to the transformer, BERT, GPT and fine-tuning: what every part does, why it is there, and what breaks without it.

Diagrams, worked maths and practice questions · follows Sequence models

In short

  1. Seq2seq squeezed the whole source sentence into one fixed vector. Attention let the decoder look back at every encoder state. The transformer dropped recurrence and kept only attention.
  2. Each token reads every other token directly, \(\text{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_k})\,V\). Training runs in parallel and any two tokens are one step apart, at a cost that grows as \(n^2\).
  3. Each block adds two things to a residual stream: attention, which moves information between tokens, and an MLP, which processes it within each token.
  4. BERT (encoder-only) is pretrained to fill in masked tokens and suits classification and tagging. GPT (decoder-only) is pretrained to predict the next token under a causal mask and suits generation.
  5. To adapt a pretrained model: a frozen backbone with a linear probe when labelled data is small, fine-tuning when there's more data or a domain shift, LoRA when memory is tight or many tasks share one base.

01From LSTMs to transformers

The field moved from LSTMs to transformers in three steps, each fixing the last one's bottleneck.

1. Seq2seq: encode, then decode (Sutskever, Vinyals and Le, 2014)

An LSTM encoder reads the source sentence. Its last state is handed to an LSTM decoder, which writes the output one token at a time, each output becoming the next input:

\[ \begin{aligned} h_j &= \text{LSTM}_{\text{enc}}(x_j, h_{j-1}) \\ c &= h_T \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} s_0 &= c \\ s_t &= \text{LSTM}_{\text{dec}}(y_{t-1}, s_{t-1}) \\ p(y_t \mid y_{<t}, x) &= \text{softmax}(W s_t) \\ L &= -\sum_t \log p(y_t \mid y_{<t}, x) \end{aligned} \]

2. Attention: let the decoder look back (Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio, 2014)

Keep every encoder state. At each decoder step, score each one against the decoder's current state, softmax the scores into weights, and read their weighted sum, the context:

\[ \begin{aligned} e_{tj} &= v^\top \tanh(W s_{t-1} + U h_j) && \text{additive (Bahdanau)} \\ e_{tj} &= s_{t-1}^\top h_j && \text{dot product (Luong's version)} \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \alpha_{tj} &= \frac{\exp(e_{tj})}{\sum_k \exp(e_{tk})} \\ c_t &= \sum_j \alpha_{tj} h_j \\ s_t &= \text{LSTM}_{\text{dec}}([y_{t-1};\, c_t],\ s_{t-1}) \end{aligned} \]

One attention step, in code

# enc: (T_x, H) every encoder state;  s: (H,) decoder state
scores  = v @ tanh(W @ s[:, None] + U @ enc.T)    # (T_x,)  additive score per source word
alpha   = softmax(scores)                         # (T_x,)  sums to 1
context = alpha @ enc                             # (H,)    weighted average of encoder states
s       = lstm_dec(concat([embed(y_prev), context]), s)
y_hat   = softmax(W_out @ s + b_out)              # next-word probabilities

3. The transformer: attention without recurrence (Vaswani et al., 2017)

If attention gives the direct path, drop the recurrence altogether: let every position attend to every other position, in every layer, all at once (“Attention Is All You Need”). BERT (encoder-only) and GPT (decoder-only) followed in 2018, and modern LLMs descend from GPT.

LSTM / GRUTransformer
How positions are processedOne after anotherAll at once, in parallel
Path from token 1 to token \(n\)\(n - 1\) steps1 step
Memory of the pastOne fixed-size stateEvery past token, kept
Cost per layer for \(n\) tokens\(n\) sequential steps of about \(H^2\)About \(n^2 d\) for attention, plus \(n d^2\) for the MLP
One more token at inferenceConstant work: update the stateGrows with \(n\): attend to every past token

The last row is why recurrent models still win for streaming with constant memory, and why recent state-space models such as Mamba try to get both: a fixed-size, RNN-like state with parallel training.

02The transformer, part by part

Each token starts as an embedding (a learned vector per token) plus a position signal. A stack of identical blocks then updates every token's vector, and each block does two things: attention moves information between tokens, and a small MLP processes it within each token.

Self-attention: a soft lookup

\[ \begin{aligned} Q &= XW_Q \\ K &= XW_K \\ V &= XW_V \\ \text{Attention}(Q, K, V) &= \text{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\Big) V \end{aligned} \]
  • Every token makes three vectors: a query (what am I looking for?), a key (what do I contain, as an advertisement?) and a value (what do I hand over if picked?).
  • Score = query · key. Row \(i\) of \(QK^\top\) says how relevant each token \(j\) is to token \(i\). The softmax turns each row into weights that are positive and add up to 1.
  • Output = weighted average of values. Token \(i\)'s new vector is a mix of every token's value, weighted by relevance. All tokens do this at once, as two matrix multiplies, which is why it trains in parallel.
  • Why three separate projections? What a token looks for and what it advertises differ (a verb looks for its subject; the subject doesn't look for the verb the same way), so the scores can be asymmetric. And what gets copied (the value) needn't be what was matched on (the key).

Why divide by \(\sqrt{d_k}\)

A dot product of two random \(d_k\)-dimensional vectors with independent, zero-mean, unit-variance entries has variance \(d_k\): it's a sum of \(d_k\) independent products, each with variance 1, and variances add. With \(d_k = 64\) the scores spread over tens, the softmax goes nearly one-hot, and its gradient goes to almost zero: the same saturation problem as a tanh stuck at ±1. Dividing by \(\sqrt{d_k}\), the standard deviation, brings the variance back to about 1. Dividing by \(d_k\) would overshoot, shrinking the scores toward 0 so every row would start out nearly uniform.

Multi-head attention

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{head}_i &= \text{Attention}\big(XW_Q^{(i)},\, XW_K^{(i)},\, XW_V^{(i)}\big) \\ \text{MultiHead}(X) &= [\text{head}_1; \dots; \text{head}_h]\, W_O \end{aligned} \]

Instead of one attention over the full width \(d\), run \(h\) heads in parallel, each with its own \(W_Q, W_K, W_V\) of width \(d/h\), then concatenate their outputs and mix them with \(W_O\). One softmax can only focus on one pattern per token, since its output is a single weighted average, and averaging two different relations blurs both; several heads can track several (a syntactic link, a coreference, a neighbouring word) at once. It costs about the same as one full-width head, because \(h\) heads of width \(d/h\) add up to the same \(d \times d\) projections and the same \(n^2 d\) of score work.

Where word order comes from

Attention treats its input as a set: shuffle the tokens and the outputs shuffle the same way. So position has to be added.

  • Sinusoidal (the original paper): fixed sine and cosine vectors of different frequencies added to the embeddings. They work for any length and change smoothly with position.
  • Learned (BERT, GPT-2): one trained vector per position. Simple, but capped at the longest length seen in training.
  • RoPE (most modern LLMs, e.g. Llama): rotate each query and key by an angle proportional to its position, so \(q_i \cdot k_j\) depends only on the offset \(i - j\). Relative position is built into the score itself.
\[ \begin{aligned} PE_{(pos,\,2i)} &= \sin\!\big(pos / 10000^{2i/d}\big) \\ PE_{(pos,\,2i+1)} &= \cos\!\big(pos / 10000^{2i/d}\big) \end{aligned} \]

Each dimension pair is a sine wave of a different wavelength, so every position gets a unique pattern, and a fixed offset is a fixed rotation of it. That makes relative positions easy to read off.

The block

\[ \begin{aligned} x &\leftarrow x + \text{MultiHead}(\text{LN}(x)) \\ x &\leftarrow x + \text{FFN}(\text{LN}(x)) \\ \text{FFN}(x) &= W_2\,\text{GELU}(W_1 x) \end{aligned} \]
  • The FFN is a two-layer MLP applied to each token separately, usually 4× wider than \(d\). Attention gathers; the FFN thinks. Most of a transformer's parameters, and much of its stored knowledge, live here.
  • Residual connections make each sublayer add to the stream instead of replacing it, so the gradient has a straight path through every layer. It's the same trick as the LSTM's \(c_t = c_{t-1} + \dots\), applied across depth instead of time.
  • LayerNorm, not BatchNorm. LayerNorm normalizes each token's vector on its own, so it doesn't care about batch size, sequence length or padding, and it behaves the same in training and at inference. BatchNorm would average across a batch of padded, variable-length sequences, so each token's output would depend on what else is in the batch, and the running averages it uses at inference may not match the sequences seen then.
  • Pre-LN vs post-LN. The original transformer put LayerNorm after each residual add (post-LN) and needed a learning-rate warmup to train. Putting it inside the branch (pre-LN, above) keeps the residual path clean and trains more stably, so most modern models use pre-LN.

The transformer in pseudocode

def attention(Q, K, V, mask=None):          # Q: (n, d_k)  K: (m, d_k)  V: (m, d_v)
    scores = Q @ K.T / sqrt(d_k)            # (n, m): how relevant each key is to each query
    if mask is not None:
        scores = where(mask, scores, -inf)  # blocked pairs get weight 0
    A = softmax(scores, axis=-1)            # each row sums to 1
    return A @ V                            # (n, d_v): weighted mix of values

def multi_head(X, mask=None):               # X: (n, d)
    Q, K, V = X @ W_Q, X @ W_K, X @ W_V     # each (n, d)
    Q, K, V = split_heads(Q, K, V, h)       # each (h, n, d/h)
    heads = [attention(Q[i], K[i], V[i], mask) for i in range(h)]
    return concat(heads, axis=-1) @ W_O     # (n, d)

def block(X, mask=None):                    # one pre-LN transformer block
    X = X + multi_head(layer_norm(X), mask)          # mix information BETWEEN tokens
    X = X + gelu(layer_norm(X) @ W_1) @ W_2          # MLP WITHIN each token: d -> 4d -> d
    return X                                # same shape in and out: (n, d)

def gpt(tokens):                            # decoder-only, e.g. GPT
    n = len(tokens)
    X = E[tokens] + P[:n]                   # token + position embeddings: (n, d)
    causal = tril(ones(n, n))               # token i may see tokens j <= i
    for _ in range(L):
        X = block(X, mask=causal)
    return layer_norm(X) @ E.T              # logits (n, V); output weights tied to E

def generate(prompt, steps, temperature=1.0):
    tokens = list(prompt)
    for _ in range(steps):
        logits = gpt(tokens)[-1]            # only the last position predicts the next token
        tokens.append(sample(softmax(logits / temperature)))
    return tokens
# KV cache: store each layer's K and V for past tokens; a new token computes only its own
# q, k, v and attends to the cache, so each step costs O(n * d) instead of re-running everything.
# BERT = the same blocks with NO causal mask (every token sees every token).

Masks

Before the softmax, blocked scores are set to \(-\infty\), so they get weight 0. A padding mask blocks pad tokens. A causal mask blocks the future: token \(i\) may only attend to tokens \(j \le i\). That's what lets a decoder train on every position of a sequence at once without peeking at the answer.

Three shapes

\[ \text{CrossAttn}(S, H) = \text{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{(S W_Q)(H W_K)^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\Big)\, H W_V \qquad S = \text{decoder states},\ H = \text{encoder outputs} \]

What it costs

  • Attention builds an \(n \times n\) score matrix per head: about \(n^2 d\) time and \(n^2\) memory per layer. The FFN costs about \(n d^2\). Short sequences are dominated by the FFN, long ones by attention.
  • Training processes all positions at once. Generation is still one token at a time; the KV cache stores every past token's keys and values, so each new token's attention costs about \(n d\) per layer instead of recomputing everything (its own projections and FFN add about \(d^2\)).
  • Parameters per block: \(4d^2\) for \(W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O\), plus \(8d^2\) for the FFN with a 4× hidden width, so about \(12 d^2\).

Decoding: greedy, beam search, sampling

A decoder outputs a probability for every possible next token; something still has to pick one.

  • Greedy: take the most likely token at each step. Fast, but short-sighted: the best whole sequence may start with a token that wasn't the single best first step, and greedy can't go back.
  • Beam search: keep the \(w\) best partial sequences by total log-probability (\(w\) is the beam width). Each step, extend each by every token and keep the best \(w\) again, at about \(w\) times the cost of greedy. Log-probabilities are negative and add up, so each extra token lowers the score and the search is biased toward ending early; dividing by a length penalty such as \(t^\alpha\) corrects for that.
  • Sampling: draw from the distribution. A temperature \(\tau\) divides the logits first (below 1 sharpens, above 1 flattens); top-\(k\) keeps only the \(k\) likeliest tokens; top-\(p\) (nucleus) keeps the smallest set whose probabilities add up to at least \(p\).

Beam search suits tasks with one right answer: translation, summarization, speech recognition. For open-ended text the single most probable continuation is often bland and repetitive (Holtzman et al., 2019), so chat models sample instead.

03Transformer what-ifs

The “take a piece out, what happens?” questions. Each answer follows from what the piece is for.

ChangeWhat happens
Drop the \(\sqrt{d_k}\)Scores grow with \(d_k\), the softmax saturates to near one-hot, gradients through it vanish, and training gets unstable.
Drop positional encodingsThe model sees a bag of tokens: “dog bites man” = “man bites dog”. (A causal decoder can still infer some order from how much it is allowed to see.)
Drop residual connectionsEvery gradient has to pass through every sublayer, so deep stacks train badly, much like a plain deep network without skip connections.
BatchNorm instead of LayerNormStatistics get mixed across padded sequences and examples, so outputs depend on the rest of the batch; they're noisy with small batches; and the running averages used at inference may not match the sequences seen then. In a causal decoder, statistics over positions would also leak the future.
Drop the causal mask in a decoderIn training each position can see the token it is supposed to predict, so the loss collapses. At generation the future isn't there, so it fails.
One head instead of many (same width)Each token gets one attention pattern instead of several, so it can't track several relations at once. Quality drops; cost is about the same.
Drop the FFNEach layer can only take weighted averages of value vectors, with no per-token computation. Capacity drops sharply.
Drop the softmax (linear attention)Weights are no longer positive and normalized. With kernel tricks it can run in \(O(n)\), but quality is usually lower.
Tie queries and keys (\(W_Q = W_K\))Scores become symmetric, and each token tends to score itself highest, so it over-attends to itself.
Replace attention with an LSTM layerBack to sequential processing: you lose the parallel training and the one-step path between tokens.

04Pretrained language models: BERT, GPT and fine-tuning

The modern recipe: pretrain a transformer on a huge unlabelled corpus with a self-supervised objective, then adapt it to a small labelled task. The two classic objectives produce the two classic shapes.

\[ \begin{aligned} L_{\text{BERT}} &= -\sum_{i \in \mathcal{M}} \log p\big(x_i \mid x_{\setminus \mathcal{M}}\big) \\ L_{\text{GPT}} &= -\sum_t \log p(x_t \mid x_{<t}) \\ p(x) &= \prod_t p(x_t \mid x_{<t}) \end{aligned} \]

Here \(\mathcal{M}\) is the set of masked positions and \(x_{\setminus \mathcal{M}}\) is everything else. BERT learns from about 15% of the tokens per sentence, while GPT learns from every one.

  • How small a linear probe is: a linear head on a frozen 1024-d encoder for 61 classes is \(1024 \times 61 + 61 = 62{,}525\) parameters. That's the entire trainable model; the encoder, often hundreds of millions of parameters, stays frozen.

BERT: fill in the blanks (encoder-only)

  • Masked language modelling. Pick 15% of the tokens. Of those, replace 80% with [MASK], 10% with a random token, and leave 10% unchanged. Predict the original token at those positions only, with cross-entropy over the vocabulary. Why 15%: fewer gives little training signal per sequence, and more leaves too little context to predict from. It's a choice rather than a tuned optimum; later work found about 40% works as well or better for large models (Wettig et al., 2023).
  • Why 80/10/10? It fixes two problems you'd get if every chosen position showed [MASK]:
    • A train/test mismatch. The loss would only ever ask about [MASK] positions, so the model would have no reason to build careful vectors for the ordinary words it sees. But BERT is mostly used as an encoder for classification, tagging, QA and embeddings, and there the input never contains [MASK]: every token is a real word. (You can put [MASK] in at inference for fill-in-the-blank; that is the one exception.)
    • The random 10% (“the cat sat on the banana”, predict “mat”) means no visible word can be blindly trusted, since any might be a planted fake. The model has to check every token against its context.
    • The unchanged 10% (the true word shown, still predicted) stops the opposite habit of ignoring what it sees: the visible word is usually right, so use it, but verify it. These positions also look exactly like real inputs.
    • Because the random and unchanged cases look like ordinary words, the model can't tell which tokens are being tested, so it keeps a good contextual vector for all of them. Overall that's 12% of tokens masked, 1.5% randomized and 1.5% left as-is. The exact split was chosen by ablation in the BERT paper.
  • Input format: [CLS] tokens [SEP], with token, position and segment embeddings added together. The final [CLS] vector feeds classification heads, and each token's final vector feeds tagging heads.
  • Next-sentence prediction was a second objective in the original BERT. RoBERTa dropped it, trained longer and re-drew the mask every epoch (“dynamic masking”), and did better.
  • Why bidirectional suits understanding: each token sees both sides, which is what tagging and classification need. It can't generate text left to right naturally.
  • Domain BERTs: the same recipe on other “languages”. ProtBert is BERT trained with masked LM on protein sequences, with amino acids as tokens.

BERT's masking, in code

def mlm_example(tokens):
    labels = [IGNORE] * len(tokens)            # no loss at unmasked positions
    for i in sample_positions(tokens, frac=0.15):
        labels[i] = tokens[i]                  # predict the original token here
        r = random()
        if r < 0.8:   tokens[i] = MASK_ID      # 80%: [MASK]
        elif r < 0.9: tokens[i] = random_token()   # 10%: a random token
        # else:       10%: leave it unchanged
    return tokens, labels

logits = bert(tokens)                          # (n, V), bidirectional: no causal mask
loss = cross_entropy(logits, labels, ignore_index=IGNORE)

Training and using BERT, end to end

1. Pretraining (self-supervised). Corrupt the input \(x\) into \(\tilde x\) by choosing 15% of positions \(\mathcal M\) and applying 80/10/10. Run the encoder, and at each chosen position predict the original token:

\[ L_{\text{MLM}} = -\frac{1}{|\mathcal M|}\sum_{i \in \mathcal M} \log p_\theta(x_i \mid \tilde x) \]
\[ p_\theta(\cdot \mid \tilde x) = \text{softmax}\big(E\, g(h_i) + b\big) \in \mathbb{R}^{V} \]

Here \(h_i\) is the encoder's final vector at position \(i\), \(g\) is a small dense + GELU + LayerNorm head, and \(E\) is the token-embedding matrix reused as the output layer (one logit per vocabulary word). The original BERT added next-sentence prediction, a binary cross-entropy on the [CLS] vector, so \(L = L_{\text{MLM}} + L_{\text{NSP}}\); RoBERTa later dropped it.

# ---- 1. pretraining: masked LM (self-supervised, huge unlabelled corpus) ----
for batch in corpus_batches:                      # token ids (B, n), e.g. n = 512
    inputs, labels = mlm_example(batch)           # 15% chosen; 80/10/10; labels = IGNORE elsewhere
    H = bert_encoder(inputs)                      # (B, n, d): no causal mask, every token sees all
    logits = gelu_ln(H @ W_t) @ E.T + b           # (B, n, V): MLM head, output tied to embeddings E
    loss = cross_entropy(logits, labels, ignore_index=IGNORE)   # only the chosen positions count
    # original BERT also: loss += cross_entropy(W_nsp @ H[:, 0], is_next)   # next-sentence prediction
    loss.backward(); clip_grad_norm(1.0); adamw.step(); warmup_then_linear_decay.step(); zero_grad()

2. Fine-tuning (supervised, per task). Add a small head and train everything briefly, with a small learning rate (about 2e-5 to 5e-5) for 2–4 epochs:

\[ L_{\text{task}} = -\log \text{softmax}\big(W h_{\text{[CLS]}} + b\big)_{y} \]
# ---- 2. fine-tuning: add a small head, train everything briefly (small lr, 2-4 epochs) ----
head = Linear(d, num_classes)                     # new, randomly initialized
for tokens, y in labelled_batches:                # "[CLS] text [SEP]", class labels
    H = bert_encoder(tokens)                      # (B, n, d), starting from the pretrained weights
    logits = head(H[:, 0])                        # H[:, 0] is the [CLS] vector: (B, num_classes)
    loss = cross_entropy(logits, y)
    loss.backward(); adamw.step(lr=2e-5); zero_grad()
# frozen-backbone variant: no grad for bert_encoder, train only `head` (a linear probe)

# ---- 3. inference: one forward pass, then read off what the task needs ----
H = bert_encoder(tokenize(text))                  # (n, d)
label    = argmax(head(H[0]))                     # classification: the [CLS] vector
tags     = [argmax(tag_head(h)) for h in H]       # NER / tagging: one label per token
best_fill = argmax(mlm_head(H[mask_pos]))         # fill-in-the-blank: input contained [MASK]
start, end = argmax(H @ w_start), argmax(H @ w_end)   # extractive QA: answer span in the passage
embedding = H.mean(axis=0)                        # search / similarity: compare by cosine

For contrast, GPT's training is simpler: shift the sequence by one and predict the next token at every position.

# ---- GPT for contrast: next-token prediction, a loss at EVERY position ----
for batch in corpus_batches:                      # (B, n + 1) token ids
    inputs, targets = batch[:, :-1], batch[:, 1:] # predict token t+1 from tokens 1..t
    logits = gpt(inputs)                          # (B, n, V), causal mask inside
    loss = cross_entropy(logits, targets)         # every position is a training example
    loss.backward(); adamw.step(); zero_grad()
# inference: generate() samples one token at a time, feeding each back in (with a KV cache)

GPT: predict the next token (decoder-only)

  • A causal mask, and a loss at every position: predict token \(t+1\) from tokens \(1 \dots t\). Every position is a training example, so it's very data-efficient.
  • It generates naturally, one token at a time. Scaled up, it gives modern LLMs, which can do new tasks from a few examples in the prompt (in-context learning). TabFM applies the same idea to tables.

Tokenization

  • Words: any word outside the vocabulary becomes an unknown token. Characters: nothing is unknown, but sequences get long. Subwords sit in between: frequent words stay whole, and rare words split into pieces.
  • BPE (GPT) starts from characters (or bytes) and repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair. WordPiece (BERT) is similar but picks the merge that most increases the likelihood of the corpus. SentencePiece runs on raw text and can learn a BPE or unigram vocabulary.
  • Domain tokens: in biology, amino acids or codons (3-nucleotide units) are natural tokens.

Worth knowing

Why can a frozen backbone with a linear head beat a model trained from scratch, or a much bigger fine-tuned one?

The pretrained embeddings already encode the structure, learned from far more data than the labelled set. With little labelled data, training millions of parameters overfits, while a linear head has few parameters, so low variance. A model trained from scratch has to learn the representation and the task from the same small dataset.

LoRA

\[ W' = W + \frac{\alpha}{r} B A, \qquad B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r},\ A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k},\ r \ll \min(d, k) \]
  • Freeze \(W\) and train only the low-rank pair \(B A\), often under 1% of the parameters: \(r(d + k)\) instead of \(dk\). \(B\) starts at 0, so training starts from the unchanged model; \(A\) starts random, so \(B\)'s gradient isn't zero.
  • The \(\alpha / r\) factor keeps the update's size roughly steady when you change \(r\), so the learning rate needn't be retuned.
  • It works because the weight change needed for a new task tends to have low intrinsic rank. After training, merge \(BA\) into \(W\): no extra inference cost.
  • It saves memory because frozen weights need no gradients and no optimizer state, which is most of what fine-tuning stores.
  • QLoRA keeps the frozen base in 4-bit precision and trains LoRA on top, which fits big models on one GPU.

LoRA, in code

class LoRALinear:
    def __init__(self, W, r=8, alpha=16):      # W: (d_out, d_in), frozen
        self.W = W
        self.A = randn(r, d_in) * 0.01         # trainable
        self.B = zeros(d_out, r)               # trainable, starts at 0: the model starts unchanged
        self.scale = alpha / r
    def forward(self, x):
        return self.W @ x + self.scale * (self.B @ (self.A @ x))
# after training: W_merged = W + scale * B @ A   (no extra cost at inference)

Catastrophic forgetting

Fine-tuning can overwrite what the model learned in pretraining. To reduce it:

  • use a small learning rate and fewer epochs;
  • freeze the lower layers, or use layer-wise learning-rate decay (lower layers learn slower);
  • use LoRA or adapters;
  • mix some old data back in (replay);
  • or penalize changes to the weights that mattered most (EWC; see Odds and ends in Deep learning basics).

Masking logits

Set the logits of invalid classes to \(-\infty\) before the softmax:

  • those classes get probability 0;
  • the softmax renormalizes over the valid set;
  • no gradient flows to the masked logits.

It's the same mechanism as a transformer's causal and padding masks. Codon-optimization models use it so every predicted codon matches its amino acid. Apply the mask at both training and inference. Masked only in training, the invalid logits are never pushed down and can win at inference. Masked only at inference, the ranking among valid classes is unchanged, but training wasted capacity pushing impossible classes down. In code, use a large negative number or the dtype's minimum rather than a literal \(-\infty\): if every entry in a row is masked, \(-\infty - (-\infty)\) inside the softmax gives NaN.

05Interview questions

Answer out loud first, then open the answer. Each answer stands on its own: the one-line answer first, then how it works, then why.

Transformers

T1Why did transformers replace LSTMs?

They train in parallel, connect any two tokens in one step, and keep every past token instead of one compressed state. That let them scale to far more data and compute.

  • Parallel training. In an RNN, \(h_t\) needs \(h_{t-1}\), so a sequence of length \(n\) takes \(n\) sequential steps and the GPU sits mostly idle. Attention computes every position at once as a few large matrix multiplies, \(\text{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_k})\,V\). The same hardware gets through far more data, which is what made scaling possible.
  • Short paths. Between tokens 1 and \(n\), information and gradient pass through \(n - 1\) recurrent steps in an RNN, each able to shrink them. In attention, any pair is one step apart.
  • No bottleneck. An RNN squeezes the whole past into one fixed-size \(h\). Attention keeps every token's vector and looks things up by content when it needs them.

The price: attention costs \(n^2\) time and memory per layer, generation is still one token at a time, and the KV cache grows with context. RNNs still win for streaming with constant work and memory per event. State-space models such as Mamba try to get both: a fixed-size, RNN-like state with parallel training.

T2Walk me through self-attention. Why separate Q, K and V?

Each token asks a question (query), advertises what it holds (key) and offers content (value). Its output is the average of all values, weighted by how well its query matches each key.

\[ \begin{aligned} Q &= XW_Q \\ K &= XW_K \\ V &= XW_V \\ \text{Attention}(Q, K, V) &= \text{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\Big) V \end{aligned} \]

Row \(i\) of \(QK^\top\) scores every token against token \(i\); the softmax turns that row into weights that sum to 1; multiplying by \(V\) takes the weighted average. It's a soft dictionary lookup, done for all tokens at once in two matrix multiplies.

  • Why queries differ from keys. With one shared projection \(W\), the score matrix \(XWW^\top X^\top\) is symmetric: \(i\)'s interest in \(j\) would equal \(j\)'s in \(i\), but relevance is often one-way (a verb looks for its subject, not the reverse). And since a dot product is at most the product of the two lengths, a token's self-score \(\lVert x_i W \rVert^2\) is at least its score with any token whose projection is no longer, so tokens over-attend to themselves.
  • Why values differ from keys. What a token is matched on (say, its grammatical role) needn't be what it should hand over (its meaning).

Likely follow-up: nothing here depends on order, so positions have to be added separately.

T3Why divide by \(\sqrt{d_k}\)?

To keep the scores' spread at about 1 whatever the head size, so the softmax doesn't saturate and its gradient doesn't vanish.

If the entries of \(q\) and \(k\) are independent with mean 0 and variance 1 (roughly true at initialization):

\[ \begin{aligned} q \cdot k &= \textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{d_k} q_j k_j \\ \text{Var}(q \cdot k) &= d_k \\ \text{Var}\big(q \cdot k / \sqrt{d_k}\big) &= 1 \end{aligned} \]
  • Why the variance is \(d_k\). The dot product is a sum of \(d_k\) independent terms, each with variance 1, and variances add. So the scores' standard deviation grows like \(\sqrt{d_k}\).
  • Why big scores hurt. Softmax exaggerates gaps: with widely spread scores, one weight is near 1 and the rest near 0. The softmax's slopes are products of weights, \(a_i(1 - a_i)\) and \(a_i a_j\), which are all near zero then, so the attention weights stop learning. It's the same saturation problem as a tanh stuck at \(\pm 1\).
  • Why \(\sqrt{d_k}\) and not \(d_k\). Dividing by the standard deviation brings the spread to 1. Dividing by \(d_k\) would shrink it toward 0, so every row would start nearly uniform.

Likely follow-up: training can still make attention sharp by growing the weights. The scaling only sets a sensible starting point, the same for any \(d_k\).

T4Why multi-head attention?

One softmax gives each token one attention pattern. Several heads let it attend to several things at once, for about the same cost.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{head}_i &= \text{Attention}\big(XW_Q^{(i)}, XW_K^{(i)}, XW_V^{(i)}\big) \\ \text{MultiHead}(X) &= [\text{head}_1; \dots; \text{head}_h]\, W_O \end{aligned} \]
  • Why one head isn't enough. A softmax row is one distribution over tokens, and the output is one weighted average. If a token needs both its subject and the previous word, a single head has to average them, which blurs both.
  • How heads split the work. Each head has its own \(W_Q, W_K, W_V\) of width \(d/h\), so each learns its own notion of relevance (syntax, coreference, nearby words). \(W_O\) mixes their outputs back to width \(d\).
  • Why the compute is the same. \(h\) heads of width \(d/h\) add up to the same \(d \times d\) projections, and their score computations add up to the same \(n^2 d\) as one full-width head. Memory isn't quite the same: each head keeps its own \(n \times n\) score matrix.

Likely follow-ups: many heads can be pruned after training with little loss (Michel et al., 2019), so they're partly redundant. Multi-query and grouped-query attention share keys and values across heads to shrink the KV cache.

T5How does a transformer know word order? What is RoPE?

Mostly through a position signal that has to be added, because attention treats its input as a set. RoPE adds it by rotating queries and keys, so their dot product depends on relative position.

  • Why attention ignores order. A score depends only on two tokens' contents, \(q_i \cdot k_j\), and the output is a weighted sum over all tokens. Shuffle the inputs and the outputs shuffle the same way: “dog bites man” looks like “man bites dog”.
  • Sinusoidal (the original paper): fixed sine and cosine waves of many wavelengths, added to the embeddings. Each position gets a unique pattern and a fixed offset is a fixed rotation, so relative positions are easy to read. Any length works.
  • Learned (BERT, GPT-2): one trained vector per position. Flexible, but there's no vector for positions past the longest trained length.
  • RoPE (Llama and most modern LLMs): rotate each pair of query and key dimensions by an angle proportional to the token's position; \(R_i\) is that rotation for position \(i\). Rotations compose, so:
\[ (R_i\, q) \cdot (R_j\, k) = q \cdot (R_{j-i}\, k) \]

The score depends only on the offset \(j - i\), so relative position sits in the score itself, not mixed into the content; each key is rotated once (so it works with a KV cache); and it stretches to longer contexts with interpolation tricks.

Likely follow-up: a causal decoder with no position signal can still infer some order, from how many tokens each position is allowed to see.

T6What do the FFN, the residual connections and LayerNorm each do? Why not BatchNorm?

Attention moves information between tokens, the FFN processes it inside each token, residuals keep a straight path through depth, and LayerNorm keeps each token's scale steady. BatchNorm doesn't fit padded, variable-length sequences.

\[ \begin{aligned} x &\leftarrow x + \text{MultiHead}(\text{LN}(x)) \\ x &\leftarrow x + \text{FFN}(\text{LN}(x)) \end{aligned} \]
  • FFN, \(W_2\,\text{GELU}(W_1 x)\): attention's output is a weighted average of value vectors, mostly linear mixing. The FFN adds non-linear computation per token, and at width \(4d\) it holds about two-thirds of a block's parameters.
  • Residuals: with \(y = x + F(x)\), the Jacobian is \(I + \partial F / \partial x\) (\(I\) is the identity), so the gradient reaches early layers even when \(F\)'s own is small. Each layer learns a change, like the LSTM's \(c_t = c_{t-1} + \dots\), across depth.
  • LayerNorm normalizes each token over its \(d\) features, so it doesn't depend on batch size, length or padding, and it behaves the same in training and inference.
  • Why not BatchNorm: it averages each feature over the batch and positions, so a token's output depends on what else, and how much padding, is in its batch; small batches make that noisy; and inference uses running averages that may not match. In a causal decoder, statistics over positions would also leak the future.

Pre-LN vs post-LN: post-LN normalizes after each residual add, interrupting the identity path, so early training is unstable without warmup. Pre-LN keeps the path clean, so modern models use it.

T7Encoder-only vs decoder-only vs encoder–decoder: what are cross-attention and the causal mask for?

They differ in what each token may see: an encoder sees both sides, a decoder only the past, and an encoder–decoder adds cross-attention so the output side can read the input side.

  • Encoder-only (BERT): no mask, so every token sees the whole input, the best context for understanding. Pretrained to fill in masked tokens; suits classification, tagging and embeddings. It can't generate left to right, because it was never trained to predict a token from the past alone.
  • Decoder-only (GPT, Llama): a causal mask and next-token prediction, \(p(x) = \prod_t p(x_t \mid x_{<t})\). Generation is natural, every position is a training example, and input and output are one sequence, so one model handles every task. Modern LLMs use this shape.
  • Encoder–decoder (T5, the original): a bidirectional encoder reads the input and a causal decoder writes the output. Suits mapping one sequence to another (translation, summarization).
\[ \text{CrossAttn}(S, H_{\text{enc}}) = \text{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{(S W_Q)(H_{\text{enc}} W_K)^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\Big) H_{\text{enc}} W_V \]

Cross-attention takes queries from the decoder states \(S\) and keys and values from the encoder outputs, so each output step can look up any input token: Bahdanau's attention, generalized. The causal mask sets the scores for \(j > i\) to \(-\infty\) before the softmax, so they get weight 0. Without it, training on all positions at once would let each position see the token it has to predict.

T8What does attention cost, and what is the KV cache?

Attention costs about \(n^2 d\) time and \(n^2\) memory per layer. The KV cache stores past keys and values, so each generated token costs about \(n d\) for attention per layer (plus about \(d^2\) for its own projections and FFN) instead of re-running the prefix.

  • Why \(n^2\). Every token scores every token: \(QK^\top\) is \(n \times n\) per head, each entry a \(d_k\)-long dot product, which over all heads is about \(n^2 d\). Multiplying the weights by \(V\) costs the same again, and the score matrix needs \(n^2\) memory per head.
  • The rest of the block (projections and FFN) costs about \(n d^2\), linear in \(n\). So short sequences are dominated by the FFN and long ones by attention.
  • Why a cache works. Under a causal mask, a past token's key and value never change when new tokens arrive. So each generation step computes only the new token's \(q, k, v\), appends \(k, v\) to the cache, and attends over it.
  • Its cost: \(2 \times n \times d\) numbers per layer per sequence, growing with context. That's what limits context length and batch size when serving.

Likely follow-ups: grouped-query attention shares keys and values across heads to shrink the cache; FlashAttention computes attention in tiles, so the \(n \times n\) matrix is never stored.

T9How many parameters does one transformer block have?

About \(12 d^2\): \(4 d^2\) in attention and \(8 d^2\) in the FFN, plus small bias and LayerNorm terms.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{attention } (W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O) &= 4 \cdot d \cdot d = 4d^2 \\ \text{FFN } (W_1, W_2) &= d \cdot 4d + 4d \cdot d = 8d^2 \\ \text{block} &\approx 12 d^2 \end{aligned} \]
  • Why multi-head doesn't change it. \(h\) heads each have \(d \times (d/h)\) projections, which add up to \(d \times d\).
  • Why the FFN is \(8d^2\). Its hidden width is \(4d\), and it has two matrices, in and out.
  • What's small: biases and LayerNorm's \(\gamma, \beta\) are a few multiples of \(d\), negligible next to \(d^2\).
  • Independent of \(n\): the same weights apply at every position, just as an RNN's weights are shared across time.

Outside the blocks: the embedding table adds vocabulary size × \(d\) (often tied to the output layer), plus learned positions if used. BERT-base: about 85M in its 12 blocks plus about 23M in embeddings, about 110M in all.

T10Greedy decoding, beam search or sampling: when each?

Greedy takes the single most likely token each step, beam search keeps the \(w\) most likely partial outputs (\(w\) is the beam width), and sampling draws from the distribution. Beam suits tasks with one right answer; sampling suits open-ended text.

\[ \text{score}(y_{1:t}) = \frac{1}{t^{\alpha}} \sum_{s=1}^{t} \log p(y_s \mid y_{<s}, x) \]

In words: a beam is ranked by its total log-probability, divided by a length penalty.

  • Greedy is fast but short-sighted: the best whole sequence may start with a token that wasn't the single best first step, and greedy can't go back.
  • Beam search extends each of the \(w\) kept prefixes by every token, then keeps the best \(w\) again, at about \(w\) times the cost. Log-probabilities are negative and add up, so each extra token lowers the score; without the length penalty the search is biased toward ending early.
  • Why beam fails on open-ended text. The most probable continuation is often bland and repetitive (Holtzman et al., 2019), so chat models sample.
  • Sampling controls: a temperature \(\tau\) divides the logits first (below 1 sharpens, above 1 flattens); top-\(k\) keeps the \(k\) likeliest tokens; top-\(p\) (nucleus) keeps the smallest set whose probabilities add up to at least \(p\).

Use: beam search for translation, summarization and speech recognition; sampling with a temperature and top-\(p\) for dialogue and creative text.

Pretrained models and training

P1Explain BERT's pretraining. Why the 80/10/10 rule?

Masked language modelling: hide 15% of the tokens and predict them from both sides' context. The 80/10/10 split stops the model relying on the [MASK] token, which never appears in downstream tasks.

\[ L_{\text{MLM}} = -\frac{1}{|\mathcal M|} \sum_{i \in \mathcal M} \log p_\theta(x_i \mid \tilde x) \]

In words: \(\mathcal M\) is the set of chosen positions and \(\tilde x\) the corrupted input; the loss counts only the chosen positions.

  • Why 15%. A trade-off: fewer gives little training signal per sequence, more leaves too little context to predict from. It's a choice, not a tuned optimum; later work found about 40% works as well or better for large models (Wettig et al., 2023).
  • Why not always [MASK]. The loss would only ask about [MASK] positions, so the model would have no reason to build good vectors for ordinary tokens, which are all it sees downstream.
  • The random 10%: any visible token might be a planted fake, so every token must be checked against its context.
  • The unchanged 10%: the true word is shown and still predicted, so the model learns to use what it sees, not ignore it.
  • Together: the model can't tell which visible tokens are being tested, so it keeps a good contextual vector for all of them.

Likely follow-ups: RoBERTa dropped next-sentence prediction and did better with longer training and a fresh mask each epoch. MLM learns from only 15% of tokens per pass, while GPT learns from every one.

P2Frozen backbone with a linear probe, full fine-tune, or LoRA: when each? Why can a probe beat a big trained model?

Probe when labelled data is small and the pretrained features already fit the task; fine-tune when there's more data or a domain shift; LoRA when memory is tight or many tasks share one base. A probe can win because it has too few parameters to overfit.

  • Probe: freeze the backbone and train only a linear head, \(dK + K\) parameters. Low variance, cheap, and the backbone's knowledge stays untouched. It fails when the features don't separate the classes linearly, usually under domain shift.
  • Full fine-tune: every weight moves, at a small learning rate (about 1e-5 to 5e-5). Most capacity, but on little data it overfits and can overwrite what pretraining learned.
  • Partial fine-tune: train the top layers and the head. Lower layers hold general features; the top ones are the most task-specific.
  • LoRA: train small low-rank updates to frozen weights. Only those need gradients and optimizer state, which saves most of the memory, and one base serves many tasks by swapping adapters.

Why a probe can win: the representation was learned from far more data than the labelled set, so the head only draws a boundary. A model trained on the small set must also learn the representation, from too little data. ppLM-CO fits this: a frozen encoder with 62K trainable parameters beat a 7.1M-parameter model.

P3What is LoRA, and why does it work?

LoRA freezes a pretrained weight matrix and learns a low-rank change to it, so fine-tuning often trains under 1% of the parameters and adds no inference cost.

\[ W' = W + \frac{\alpha}{r} BA, \qquad B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r},\ A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k},\ r \ll \min(d, k) \]

In words: instead of a full \(d \times k\) update, learn two thin matrices whose product is the update. Trainable parameters drop from \(dk\) to \(r(d + k)\).

  • Why it works. The weight change a new task needs tends to have low intrinsic rank: a few directions carry most of it.
  • Why \(B\) starts at 0. Then \(BA = 0\), so training starts from exactly the pretrained model. \(A\) starts random so that \(B\)'s gradient isn't zero.
  • Why \(\alpha / r\). It keeps the update's size roughly steady when you change \(r\), so the learning rate needn't be retuned.
  • Why it saves memory. Frozen weights need no gradients and no Adam state, which is most of fine-tuning's memory.
  • Why no inference cost. After training, \(BA\) is merged into \(W\), leaving one matrix.

Likely follow-ups: QLoRA keeps the frozen base in 4-bit precision, which fits big models on one GPU. Under a large domain shift, LoRA can fall short of a full fine-tune.

P4What does a \(-\infty\) logit mask do to the probabilities and the gradients?

A masked class gets probability exactly 0, the softmax renormalizes over the valid classes, and the masked logits get no gradient.

\[ \begin{aligned} p_k &= 0 && \text{masked } k \\ p_k &= \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j \text{ valid}} e^{z_j}} && \text{valid } k \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_k} &= p_k - y_k && \text{softmax + cross-entropy} \end{aligned} \]
  • Why 0. \(e^{-\infty} = 0\), so a masked class adds nothing to the numerator or the denominator.
  • Why no gradient. A masked class has zero probability and is never the label, so its gradient \(p_k - y_k\) is zero. The model spends nothing on learning to rule out impossible classes; all the learning goes into choosing among valid ones.
  • Masked only in training is the dangerous case: the invalid logits were never pushed down, so at inference they can win.
  • Masked only at inference is safe for ranking, since renormalizing over the valid set doesn't change their order, but it wastes training: the model spent capacity pushing impossible classes down, and the valid logits learned to compete against the wrong set. So mask in both.

In practice: use a large negative number or the dtype's minimum, not a literal \(-\infty\). A stable softmax subtracts the row's maximum, and if a whole row is masked that maximum is itself \(-\infty\), so \(-\infty - (-\infty)\) gives NaN (with the dtype's minimum the row comes out silently uniform instead). Causal and padding masks in attention work the same way.

P5Bi-LSTM or transformer for a per-token tagging task?

A pretrained transformer encoder, if a good one exists for the domain; otherwise a bi-LSTM is a strong, cheap baseline, especially on small data.

  • Both see both sides, which tagging needs: a token's label depends on what comes before and after it.
  • Bi-LSTM: context flows step by step through gated states, so far-apart tokens are \(n\) steps apart. Cost is linear in \(n\), and its built-in lean toward nearby context helps when labelled data is small.
  • Transformer: any two positions are one step apart, and it can start from a pretrained encoder (BERT, or a domain one such as ProtBert for proteins). Transfer from pretraining usually decides it. The cost is \(n^2\) per layer.
  • Length: learned position embeddings have no vectors past the longest trained length, so very long inputs need chunking; RoPE degrades more gracefully, and an RNN has no hard limit.

Enforcing valid outputs: a CRF layer on top scores label transitions (an “inside-person” tag can't follow “begin-location”) and decodes the best whole sequence with Viterbi (dynamic programming over label sequences). A logit mask enforces per-position rules, such as a codon having to match its amino acid.

P6Validation loss starts rising at epoch 5 while training loss keeps falling. What do you do?

It's overfitting: keep the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss (just before the rise), then regularize, after ruling out leakage, a shift between splits, and a gap between loss and metric.

  • Why this pattern means overfitting. Training loss keeps falling because the model is fitting details specific to the training set; validation loss rises because those details don't carry over.
  • Early stopping: that checkpoint generalized best. Use some patience, since validation loss is noisy.
  • Regularize: dropout (noise stops units relying on each other), weight decay (smaller weights, smoother function), augmentation (more effective data), a smaller model (less capacity), or more data (lower variance).
  • Learning rate: a lower one or a decay schedule turns later epochs into small refinements instead of big moves.

Check first:

  • leakage, or different distributions in the two splits, can produce the same shape;
  • the metric you care about: validation loss can rise while accuracy or AUC still improves, because the model grows overconfident on a few examples it gets wrong and cross-entropy punishes confident mistakes heavily. Stop on the metric if that's what matters.
P7Your loss suddenly spikes to NaN halfway through training. Causes and fixes?

Usually an exploding update: one huge gradient step throws the weights where activations overflow. Clip, lower the learning rate, and find the batch.

  • How it happens. A steep cliff in the loss or a bad batch gives one huge gradient; the step lands where activations are enormous; they overflow to infinity, and \(\infty - \infty\) or \(0 \times \infty\) gives NaN, which then spreads to every weight.
  • Usual causes: a learning rate too high, or no warmup with Adam, whose early step sizes are poorly estimated; long recurrent products; fp16 overflowing its small range; \(\log 0\) from computing softmax and log separately; NaN or extreme values in the inputs.

Fixes, each matching a cause:

  • clip the gradient norm, which caps the step;
  • lower the learning rate or add warmup;
  • use a fused, stable log-softmax and cross-entropy, so there's no \(\log 0\);
  • use loss scaling, or bf16, for mixed precision;
  • validate the inputs and find the offending batch.

To confirm: plot the gradient norm next to the loss. A spike just before the NaN points to an explosion. Restart from the last good checkpoint.

P8How would you optimize a metric that isn't differentiable?

Replace the metric with a differentiable stand-in that tracks it, or treat it as a reward and use a gradient estimator that doesn't need its derivative.

Metrics built from argmax, thresholds or counts are piecewise constant in the weights, so their gradient is 0 almost everywhere. The options:

  • Smooth surrogate: replace hard choices with soft ones, such as a softmax with temperature \(\tau\) for an argmax. As \(\tau \to 0\) it approaches the real metric, but its gradients get sharper and noisier.
  • Gumbel-softmax: a differentiable stand-in for sampling a one-hot choice.
  • Straight-through: use the hard choice in the forward pass and treat it as the identity in the backward pass. Biased, but simple, and it often works.
  • REINFORCE: treat the metric as a reward \(R\). It needs only reward values, so any metric works; it's unbiased but high-variance, and subtracting a baseline from \(R\) cuts the variance:
    \[ \nabla_\theta\, \mathbb{E}[R] = \mathbb{E}\big[R\, \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(y)\big] \]
  • Weighting: keep cross-entropy, and weight examples or classes the way the metric does.

Why it may not help: cross-entropy may already push toward what the metric rewards, and a surrogate can disagree with the real metric. Always check the real metric on validation.

P9What is catastrophic forgetting, and how do you reduce it?

Training on new data overwrites what the model knew, because nothing in the new loss asks it to keep its old abilities. The fixes keep the weights near where they were, or keep old data in the loss.

  • Why it happens. Gradient descent moves the shared weights to lower the new loss only. Weights that mattered for the old task move freely, and old performance drops.
  • Small learning rate, few epochs: stay close to the pretrained weights.
  • Freeze lower layers, or use layer-wise learning-rate decay: lower layers hold the most general features, so protect them most.
  • LoRA or adapters: the base weights never change, so removing the adapter gives back the old model.
  • Replay: mix old examples back in, which puts the old loss back into the objective.
  • EWC: penalize moving each weight in proportion to how much the old task depends on it. \(\theta_i^*\) is the weight after the old task, and \(F_i\), the Fisher information, is roughly the old loss's average squared gradient for weight \(i\): large means important.
    \[ L = L_{\text{new}} + \frac{\lambda}{2} \sum_i F_i\, (\theta_i - \theta_i^*)^2 \]

In production: retraining under drift is the same problem. Keep a slice of older data in the mix, and track performance on older segments.
