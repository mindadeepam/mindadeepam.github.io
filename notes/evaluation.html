Model evaluation and validation

How to tell, before shipping, whether a model will work, and how each way of measuring it can fool you: cross-validation schemes and nested CV, the confusion matrix and choosing a threshold, ROC and AUC built by hand, the tiny slice of the ROC curve that matters when you can act on few cases, precision–recall and average precision, log loss, Brier and calibration, ranking metrics, regression metrics and MAPE's traps, and comparing two models honestly.

About 60 minutes, plus 32 questions · one running example: eight scored cases, worked by hand · one fraud screen at 0.5% prevalence · every number checked by code

In short

  1. Validation must copy deployment. Stratified k-fold for independent rows, grouped folds when new customers or documents arrive, forward-in-time splits with a gap for anything temporal. A score you tuned on is optimistic: on pure-noise labels the best of 60 settings scored 0.58 accuracy in CV, while nested CV said 0.505, close to the true 0.50.
  2. A classifier is judged three ways: its ranking (AUC, average precision), its decisions at a threshold (precision, recall, F1, cost), and its probabilities (log loss, Brier, calibration). The threshold comes from costs or capacity: with calibrated probabilities, flag when \(p \gt C_{FP}/(C_{FP} + C_{FN})\). Youden's J and F1 answer different questions and can be far from the cost optimum.
  3. AUC is the chance a random positive outscores a random negative (the Mann–Whitney statistic). It ignores calibration and averages over every threshold. When a team can review only 1% of cases, only the leftmost 1% of the ROC curve matters, under 1% of the area: two models with AUC 0.95 caught 231 and 362 of 500 frauds there.
  4. Precision–recall and average precision show what a reviewer sees. A random model's AP equals the prevalence, so AP moves with the base rate. 300 false alarms pushed above every fraud cut AP from 0.30 to 0.12 while ROC-AUC fell by 0.003.
  5. Probabilities need proper scores and calibration. Log loss and Brier are minimized only by the true probabilities; Brier = reliability − resolution + uncertainty. Fix calibration on held-out data with Platt, temperature or isotonic scaling, and undo class reweighting by rescaling the odds by the ratio of real to training base-rate odds.
  6. The metric decides what the model predicts: RMSE the mean, MAE the median, pinball loss a quantile, MAPE something biased low. Ranking uses P@k, R@k, MRR, MAP and nDCG per query. Compare models on the same rows: a paired bootstrap, McNemar, or a corrected t-test for CV folds, because fold scores aren't independent.

01Generalization, splits and baselines

A model is only useful on data it hasn't seen. Its generalization error is the expected error on new cases drawn from the population it will serve. The training error underestimates it, because the model has partly fitted the noise in those particular rows: a 1-nearest-neighbour classifier gets every training row right and can be useless on new ones. So every honest estimate comes from rows that played no part in any decision about the model.

Train, validation, test

  • Three jobs. Training data fits the parameters and every preprocessing statistic; validation data (a held-out set or CV folds) chooses everything else, including the threshold and the calibration map; the test set is looked at once, after everything is frozen, since every look that leads to a change makes its score optimistic (honest splits, leakage types).
  • The test set should look like deployment: the most recent period, the same population, the same unit (customer, document, store). For time-ordered problems that means an out-of-time test with a gap, and its size is set by the number of positives, not rows (out of time, holdout size). Adversarial validation checks that test rows look like training rows (data-preparation note).
  • Refit, then don't touch. After choosing everything, it's common to refit on training plus validation data before the final test. Nothing changes after the test score is seen.
  • Offline is still a proxy. The last word is online: shadow mode, then an A/B test (shadow mode, A/B testing).

02Cross-validation schemes

One held-out split gives one noisy number, and wastes the held-out rows for training. Cross-validation rotates which part is held out: split the data into \(k\) folds, train on \(k - 1\) of them, score on the one left out, repeat \(k\) times, and average. Every row is used for validation exactly once.

What the average estimates. Not the score of the final model trained on all the data, but the expected score of the procedure “fit this model on \((k - 1)/k\) of a dataset like this one”. Training on less data usually scores slightly worse, so k-fold CV is a little pessimistic, more so for small \(k\).

The one rule that picks the scheme: each fold must imitate how the model will meet new data. Ask what's new at prediction time: new rows from the same mix, new customers, or the future.

k-fold, and the choice of k

  • Shuffle, then split into \(k\) equal folds, unless order carries meaning (time; see below).
  • \(k\) = 5 or 10 is the usual compromise. Smaller \(k\) trains each model on less data (more pessimistic) and is cheaper; larger \(k\) costs more fits.
  • Leave-one-out (\(k = n\)) is nearly unbiased but needs \(n\) fits, can't be stratified, and its estimate can be highly variable: the \(n\) training sets are almost identical, so the \(n\) fold errors are highly correlated and averaging them cancels little of the noise. A metric like AUC can't be computed on a one-row fold; you'd have to pool the predictions.
  • Pooling vs averaging. AUC computed on the pooled out-of-fold predictions mixes scores from \(k\) different models, whose scales can differ; averaging the per-fold AUCs doesn't. They usually agree closely; when they don't, the fold models disagree about the score scale, which also matters if you'll pick one threshold.
  • Repeated k-fold reruns CV with different shuffles and averages, which removes the luck of one particular partition (how noisy a CV estimate is, below).
  • Bagged models get a nearly free validation estimate from the rows each tree didn't see (out-of-bag, in the random-forests note).

Stratified k-fold

Each fold keeps the class proportions of the whole dataset. With rare positives, plain random folds can be badly uneven: with 1,000 rows, 20 positives and 5 shuffled folds, the ideal is 4 positives per fold, but in 32% of random shuffles some fold gets 0 or 1 positive (and in 41% some fold gets 7 or more). A fold with one positive gives a recall of 0 or 1 and an AUC that's pure noise. Stratification gives exactly 4, 4, 4, 4, 4. Use it by default for classification; for regression you can stratify on bins of \(y\).

Grouped k-fold

All rows of one group (a customer, patient, store, device, document, or vendor's invoice template) go into the same fold. Needed whenever deployment means new groups: if one customer's rows sit on both sides, the model can score well by recognizing the customer rather than by learning anything that transfers.

In numbers. 200 customers with 10 rows each; each customer has a hidden risk level and three fixed attributes that identify them but say nothing about that risk. A random forest:

  • The gap drops the rows between training and test. If you're simulating real forecasting, use forward splits.

How noisy is a CV estimate?

One dataset of 300 rows, a logistic regression, stratified 5-fold CV:

  • The five fold scores were 0.783, 0.633, 0.667, 0.717 and 0.783: a spread of 15 points between folds, standard deviation 0.068.
  • The partition alone moves the average: reshuffling the same rows 50 times gave CV accuracies from 0.687 to 0.743 (standard deviation 0.011). Picking between two models that differ by less than this on one shuffle is picking a seed. Repeated CV averages it away.
  • The naive standard error, fold standard deviation / \(\sqrt k\), treats the folds as independent. They aren't: training sets overlap, and all folds come from one dataset. Over 300 fresh datasets the CV estimate's real standard deviation was 0.029; the naive formula averaged 0.024, about 17% too small. There's no unbiased estimator of the variance of k-fold CV (Bengio and Grandvalet, 2004), so treat fold spreads as a rough guide and compare models with paired methods (section 10).

Nested cross-validation

Why tuning on the CV score is optimistic. Try 60 hyperparameter settings, each scored by CV, and report the best. Each score is truth plus noise, and taking the maximum picks the setting whose noise happened to be most favourable: the winner's curse. Measured: 100 rows, 20 features, labels that are pure coin flips (so every model's true accuracy is 0.50), a k-nearest-neighbours grid of 60 settings, repeated on 40 datasets:

EstimateMean over 40 datasets
Best inner CV score of the 60 settings0.578 (from 0.49 to 0.65)
Nested CV score0.505
Truth0.500

Eight points of “accuracy” on pure noise, from selection alone. The more settings tried and the smaller the data, the bigger the bias.

The fix: two loops. The outer loop estimates the score: split into \(k_{\text{out}}\) folds and hold each out once. The inner loop runs entirely inside each outer training part: a full CV search over the grid that picks the best setting, which is then refitted on the whole outer training part and scored once on the outer test fold. The outer test fold never influences any choice.

  • What its score means: the expected performance of the whole procedure, “search this grid with inner CV, then fit”. It does not pick a hyperparameter setting; different outer folds may choose different ones (and if they disagree wildly, the choice is unstable).
  • The final model: run the inner search once on all the data, and report the nested score as its expected performance.
  • Cost: \(k_{\text{out}} \times k_{\text{in}} \times\) (grid size) fits: 5 × 4 × 60 = 1,200 inner fits plus 5 outer refits for the design in the figure with a 60-setting grid, and one more search (4 × 60 + 1 = 241 fits) for the final model. With plenty of data, a single train / validation / test split does the same job far more cheaply: the test set plays the outer loop.

Without refitting: for likelihood models, AIC and BIC estimate out-of-sample fit from one fit by charging for the number of parameters (likelihood note); CV assumes less and works for any metric.

Which rates depend on the base rate

TPR, FNR, FPR and specificity each divide by a true class count, so they describe how the model treats positives and negatives separately. Change how many positives there are and, as long as each class's scores look the same, these don't move. Precision, NPV and accuracy mix the two classes, so they move with the prevalence \(\pi\), the share of positives. Precision written in terms of the other rates:

\[ \text{precision} = \frac{\text{TPR}\cdot\pi}{\text{TPR}\cdot\pi + \text{FPR}\cdot(1 - \pi)} \]

In words: of everything flagged, the true flags are the share of positives times the share of them caught; the false flags are the share of negatives times the share of them wrongly caught. Keep TPR = 1.00 and FPR = 0.25 from the eight rows, but move to a fraud stream where \(\pi = 0.5\%\): precision falls from 0.80 to \(0.005/(0.005 + 0.25 \times 0.995) = 0.02\). The same model now raises 50 alarms per real fraud.

Trap

“TPR and FPR don't depend on prevalence” holds only under pure label shift: the base rate changes but each class's score distribution doesn't. A new population usually changes the case mix too (a spectrum shift: more borderline positives, different kinds of negatives), and then TPR, FPR and AUC move as well.

The accuracy paradox

In the fraud stream used later in this note (0.5% fraud), flagging nothing scores 99.5% accuracy and catches nothing. Accuracy is a fair summary only when the classes are roughly balanced and both errors cost about the same. Two single numbers that don't reward the do-nothing model:

  • Balanced accuracy, the mean of recall and specificity. A constant prediction scores 0.5. On the eight rows at \(t = 0.55\): \((1.00 + 0.75)/2 = 0.875\).
  • Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), the correlation between predicted and true labels, from −1 to 1, with 0 for guessing. It uses all four cells, so it's high only when both classes are handled well. On the eight rows: 0.775.
\[ \text{MCC} = \frac{TP\cdot TN - FP\cdot FN}{\sqrt{(TP + FP)(TP + FN)(TN + FP)(TN + FN)}} \]

In words: the cross-product of right minus wrong cells, scaled by the four row and column totals. When the denominator is 0 (a constant predictor), libraries return 0. Cohen's kappa, accuracy corrected for the agreement two raters would reach by chance, is a related idea (0.75 on the eight rows), mostly used for agreement between annotators.

F1 and F-beta

When positives are rare and you care about them, the two numbers that matter are precision and recall. F1 combines them with a harmonic mean:

\[ \begin{aligned} F_1 &= \frac{2PR}{P + R} = \frac{2\,TP}{2\,TP + FP + FN} \\ F_\beta &= \frac{(1 + \beta^2)\,PR}{\beta^2 P + R} \end{aligned} \]

In words: \(F_1 = TP/\big(TP + \tfrac12(FP + FN)\big)\), true positives divided by true positives plus the average of the two error counts. \(F_\beta\) treats recall as \(\beta\) times as important as precision.

  • Why harmonic: it's dominated by the smaller of the two, so you can't score well by maxing one. Flagging a single sure case gives \(P = 1\), \(R = 0.01\): F1 is 0.0198, where the arithmetic mean would say 0.505.
  • What it ignores: true negatives. That suits rare-positive retrieval, but swapping which class is “positive” changes F1.
  • \(\beta\) in numbers: with \(P = 0.4\) and \(R = 0.8\), F1 = 0.533, \(F_2\) = 0.667 (recall-heavy: screening, where a miss is worse) and \(F_{0.5}\) = 0.444 (precision-heavy: auto-actions, where a false alarm is worse).
  • It isn't a cost. It has no knob for “a missed fraud costs 50 false alarms”. If you know the costs, minimize the expected cost (below).

Choosing a threshold

The threshold is a business decision made on validation data, after the model is trained. 0.5 has no special status: it's right only when the probabilities are calibrated and both errors cost the same. Precision zigzags as you go down (1.00, 0.50, 0.67…) because each negative knocks it down and each positive lifts it. On these balanced rows the F1 and Youden criteria agree. On imbalanced data they don't, and neither is what a business usually wants. The candidates:

  • F1-optimal: the cut with the highest F1. A neat fact: with calibrated probabilities, the F1-optimal threshold is half the best achievable F1 (exactly in the population, approximately on a finite sample; Lipton and colleagues, 2014). Why: \(F_1 = 2TP/(\#\text{flagged} + \#\text{positives})\). Flagging one more case with probability \(t\) adds \(2t\) to the expected numerator and 1 to the denominator, which raises the ratio only if \(2t\) exceeds the current F1. So keep flagging while \(t \gt F_1/2\); at the best cut, \(t^* = F_1^*/2\). The fraud screen below confirms it: best F1 0.348, threshold 0.174.
  • Youden's J = TPR − FPR, the height of the ROC curve above the diagonal. It treats a missed positive and a false alarm as equally bad per class, which ignores how many of each there are.
  • Cost-based: minimize the expected cost. This is the principled one.
  • A constraint: recall at least 95% (medical screening), precision at least 90% (automatic actions), or capacity: the review team can check 1,000 cases a day, so flag the top 1,000. That last one is precision@k (section 08).

The cost-based threshold, derived. A case has calibrated probability \(p\) of being positive. Flagging it costs \(C_{FP}\) if it's negative; not flagging costs \(C_{FN}\) if it's positive. Correct decisions cost nothing here.

  1. Expected cost of flagging: \((1 - p)\,C_{FP}\).
  2. Expected cost of not flagging: \(p\,C_{FN}\).
  3. Flag when the first is smaller: \((1 - p)\,C_{FP} \lt p\,C_{FN}\), i.e. when \(p \gt C_{FP}/(C_{FP} + C_{FN})\).
\[ \begin{aligned} t^* &= \frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP} + C_{FN}} \\ t^*_{\text{general}} &= \frac{C_{FP} - C_{TN}}{(C_{FP} - C_{TN}) + (C_{FN} - C_{TP})} \end{aligned} \]

In words: the threshold is the false alarm's share of the total cost of the two mistakes; the general form measures each mistake against the cost of getting that case right. If a missed fraud costs 50 and a false alarm 1, \(t^* = 1/51 \approx 0.02\): flag anything with even a 2% chance. Watch which costs are real: if every flag costs a 5-unit review including flags on real fraud, and a miss costs 100, then \(C_{TP} = C_{FP} = 5\), \(C_{TN} = 0\), \(C_{FN} = 100\), and \(t^* = 5/100 = 0.05\), not \(5/105\). The applied version, a lender's break-even default probability with money on both sides, is in the project-lifecycle note.

The formula needs calibrated probabilities (section 07). With uncalibrated scores, sweep thresholds on validation data and pick the lowest total cost, \(C_{FP}\cdot FP + C_{FN}\cdot FN\).

The same answer on the ROC curve. Moving the threshold slightly trades a little TPR for a little FPR. The expected cost is flat where those trades balance, which happens where the ROC curve's slope is

\[ \text{slope}^* = \frac{1 - \pi}{\pi}\cdot\frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FN}} \]

In words: the cost-optimal point is where one more unit of FPR buys exactly enough TPR to pay for it, and both the base rate and the cost ratio set that exchange rate. Youden's J picks the point of slope 1, so it is cost-optimal only if a miss costs \((1 - \pi)/\pi\) times a false alarm: 1 times at 50% prevalence, 199 times at 0.5%. That's why, on rare-event problems, Youden flags far too much unless a miss really costs hundreds of false alarms.

Why the slope: the ROC curve's slope at a threshold is the likelihood ratio \(f_+(s)/f_-(s)\), the density of positive scores over the density of negative scores there, and posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio. So this is the rule \(p \gt t^*\) restated in ROC terms. In the fraud screen below, the cost-optimal cut sits where the slope is \(199/50 = 3.98\).

04ROC curves and AUC

The threshold table above has one row per cut-off. The ROC curve (receiver operating characteristic) draws all of them at once: TPR on the vertical axis against FPR on the horizontal, as the threshold slides from above the top score (nothing flagged, the point (0, 0)) to below the bottom one (everything flagged, (1, 1)). The area under it, AUC, is one threshold-free number for the ranking.

A perfect ranking goes straight up to (0, 1) and then right: AUC 1. A random one hugs the diagonal: AUC 0.5. Here the area is \(1/16 + 3 \times 4/16 = 13/16 = 0.8125\).

AUC is a probability: pairs won

Look at what the columns measure. Each column is \(1/n_-\) wide and as tall as the share of positives above that negative, so its area is (positives above it) / \((n_+ n_-)\). Adding the columns counts every (positive, negative) pair in which the positive is ranked higher:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{AUC} &= P(s_+ \gt s_-) + \tfrac12\,P(s_+ = s_-) \\ &= \frac{\#\{\text{pairs the positive wins}\} + \tfrac12\,\#\{\text{ties}\}}{n_+\,n_-} \end{aligned} \]

In words: pick one positive and one negative at random; AUC is the chance the positive gets the higher score, with ties counting half. On the eight rows: A beats all four negatives (4); C, D and E each beat F, G and H but lose to B (3 each). That's 13 of 16 pairs, 0.8125, the same as the area.

It's the Mann–Whitney U statistic. \(U\), the number of pairs the positives win (ties counting half), is the statistic of the rank-sum test (Mann–Whitney), so \(\text{AUC} = U/(n_+ n_-)\). That gives a way to compute it from ranks alone, without building the curve. Rank all scores from smallest (1) to largest, giving tied scores their average rank, and add up the positives' ranks \(W_+\):

\[ \text{AUC} = \frac{W_+ - n_+(n_+ + 1)/2}{n_+\,n_-} \]

In words: a positive with overall rank \(r\) that is the \(j\)-th smallest positive has \(r - 1\) cases below it, \(j - 1\) of them positives, so it beats \(r - j\) negatives. Summing \(r - j\) over positives gives \(W_+ - (1 + 2 + \dots + n_+)\). Here the ranks are H 1, G 2, F 3, E 4, D 5, C 6, B 7, A 8, so \(W_+ = 8 + 6 + 5 + 4 = 23\) and \(\text{AUC} = (23 - 10)/16 = 0.8125\). This is the \(O(n \log n)\) way to compute AUC: one sort.

Ties

Suppose E (positive) and F (negative) both scored 0.50. The pair (E, F) is now a tie and counts half: \(12.5/16 = 0.781\). On the curve, the tied group moves up and right at the same time, a diagonal segment from (.25, .75) to (.50, 1), and the trapezoid under a diagonal is exactly the half credit.

Trap

Emitting one ROC point per row instead of per distinct score. Then tied rows are processed in whatever order the sort left them, and the AUC changes with that order: E-then-F gives 0.8125, F-then-E gives 0.75. Group tied scores and move once per group. Ties are common with rounded scores, tree models (one score per leaf) and isotonic calibration.

What AUC ignores

  • The actual score values. Only the order matters, so any strictly increasing transform leaves AUC unchanged. Cube the eight scores: still 0.8125. Halve the probabilities: still 0.8125, while the log loss worsens from 0.540 to 0.656 and the Brier score from 0.176 to 0.244. So AUC says nothing about calibration. A model can have a great AUC and probabilities that are off by a factor of two.
  • Prevalence (under pure label shift): TPR and FPR each condition on the true class, so the curve doesn't move when the base rate does. Useful for comparing rankings across populations; useless for telling you what precision you'll get.
  • Which thresholds you'll use. AUC averages the ranking quality over every cut-off, including ones nobody will ever operate at. The next two subsections show why that matters.
  • Below 0.5 means the ranking is inverted: flip the scores and you get \(1 - \text{AUC}\). Exactly 0.5 can still hide signal that isn't monotone (positives at both extremes).

Why AUC looks fine when precision is terrible

The fraud screen's model (model A from here on) has AUC 0.95, which sounds excellent. Yet at Youden's threshold it flags 12,617 cases a day for 439 frauds, a precision of 3.5%, and even the top 1,000 are 77% false alarms. Two reasons the ROC view hides this:

  • FPR divides by the huge negative count. 769 false alarms in the top 1,000 is an FPR of only \(769/99{,}500 = 0.77\%\). On a ROC plot that point sits a hair from the left edge and looks superb.
  • Most pairs are easy. AUC counts all \(500 \times 99{,}500 \approx 50\) million (fraud, legitimate) pairs equally. Most legitimate transactions are obviously legitimate and sit below almost every fraud; those pairs are won for free and fill up the AUC. The operational question, whether frauds beat the roughly 770 most suspicious-looking legitimate transactions, is decided by \(770 \times 500 \approx 385{,}000\) pairs, under 1% of the 50 million.

The tiny slice that matters: partial AUC

The setting. 100,000 transactions a day, 500 of them fraud, and a team that can review 1,000. Whatever threshold you pick, you can't flag more than 1,000 cases, so you can't produce more than 1,000 false positives. Out of 99,500 legitimate transactions, that caps the false positive rate at

\[ \text{FPR}_{\max} = \frac{1{,}000}{99{,}500} \approx 1.0\% \]

In words: every threshold you could ever use lives in the leftmost 1% of the ROC plot's width. The other 99% of the curve describes thresholds that would flag more cases than the team can touch. That strip from FPR 0 to 1% is the tiny slice.

Two different 1%s meet here. The team reviews 1% of all transactions (1,000 of 100,000), and that caps the false positive rate, which counts only the legitimate ones, at 1,000/99,500. The real operating point sits a little further left, because some of the 1,000 flags are frauds: model A's top 1,000 hold 769 false alarms, an FPR of 0.77% (the circles in the figure below). Operating at an FPR of exactly 1% would mean 995 false alarms plus about 250 frauds, about 1,245 flags, more than the team can review; that's why A's TPR at 1%, 0.50, is above its recall at 1,000 flags, 0.46.

How little of the AUC lives there. The slice is 0.01 wide and at most 1 tall, so at most 0.01 of area can come from it. Model A's curve gives 0.00375 there: 0.4% of its AUC of 0.95. The other 0.946 is earned at false positive rates you will never operate at. So AUC is almost entirely a measure of how frauds rank against legitimate transactions the team will never look at.

Partial AUC is the area under the ROC curve over only the FPR range you can operate in, here \([0, 0.01]\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{pAUC} &= \int_0^{0.01} \text{TPR}(f)\,df \\ \frac{\text{pAUC}}{0.01} &= \text{average recall across the slice} \end{aligned} \]

In words: \(\text{TPR}(f)\) is the height of the ROC curve at false positive rate \(f\). Integrate it only where you'll work, then divide by the slice width to get a number between 0 and 1: the average share of positives caught over the usable thresholds. A gets 0.375, B 0.692. scikit-learn's roc_auc_score(y, s, max_fpr=0.01) reports a rescaled version (the McClish correction, \(\tfrac12\big[1 + (\text{pAUC} - f_0^2/2)/(f_0 - f_0^2/2)\big]\) with \(f_0 = 0.01\): \(f_0^2/2\) is the area the diagonal gets in the slice, \(f_0\) the area a perfect model gets) in which a random model scores 0.5 and a perfect one 1: 0.686 for A and 0.845 for B.

The same thing in pairs. pAUC / 0.01 is the chance that a random fraud outscores a random legitimate transaction drawn from the 1% most suspicious-looking legitimate ones (about 995 a day). Full AUC draws that legitimate transaction from all 99,500, most of them easy wins. The slice average (0.375 for A) is below A's recall in the top 1,000 (0.46) because it averages the curve's height across the whole slice, including the stricter thresholds at its left end.

  • Choosing the slice: from capacity (as here), or from a tolerable false-alarm rate. Simpler alternatives that answer the same question: TPR at a fixed FPR, recall@k or precision@k at the real k.
  • The general lesson: a metric should measure the model where the decision is made. AUC is right when you need the whole ranking (the order of a credit book, comparing rankings across populations); when you act on a small top slice, measure the slice.

Gini and KS: AUC in credit clothing

  • Gini \(= 2\,\text{AUC} - 1\), rescaling AUC so a random ranking scores 0 and a perfect one 1. Eight rows: 0.625; fraud models: 0.90. It's the area between the curve and the diagonal, doubled (and also called Somers' D). Credit scorecards report Gini by convention. It has nothing to do with the Gini impurity used to split trees.
  • KS (Kolmogorov–Smirnov) = the largest gap between the cumulative score distributions of positives and negatives, which is \(\max_t (\text{TPR} - \text{FPR})\): the best Youden's J. Eight rows: 0.75, at the top-five cut. It summarizes one point of the curve, where AUC summarizes all of it.
  • Lift@k = precision@k / prevalence: how many times better than random the top \(k\) is. Fraud screen, model A's top 1,000: \(0.231/0.005 = 46\times\). The cumulative gains curve plots recall against the share of all cases flagged; at low prevalence it nearly coincides with the ROC curve.

How these feed a lending decision is in the lifecycle note's offline-metric section.

Survival models use the same pairs-won idea: Harrell's C-index is the share of comparable pairs whose predicted risks are ordered like their observed event times, with censoring deciding which pairs count.

More than two classes: one-vs-rest AUC (each class against the rest, then a macro or prevalence-weighted mean), or one-vs-one (Hand and Till's average over every pair of classes, less sensitive to imbalance).

Say it in an interview: “AUC is the chance a random positive outscores a random negative, which is the Mann–Whitney U divided by the number of pairs. It depends only on the order, so it ignores calibration and, under pure label shift, prevalence. Its weakness is that it averages over every threshold. If a team can act on 1% of cases, only FPR from 0 to about 1% matters, which is under 1% of the area; two models with AUC 0.95 caught 231 and 362 of 500 frauds there. So I'd report partial AUC or precision and recall at the real k.”

05Precision–recall curves and average precision

The precision–recall (PR) curve plots precision against recall as the threshold slides. Where ROC asks “what share of negatives did I disturb?”, PR asks “what share of my flags were real?”, which is the question a reviewer, a user or an alert queue experiences. The eight rows' (recall, precision) points come straight from the sweep table: (0.25, 1.00), (0.25, 0.50), (0.50, 0.67), (0.75, 0.75), (1.00, 0.80), then down to (1.00, 0.50).

  • The baseline is the prevalence, not 0.5. A random ranking has precision \(\pi\) at every recall, a flat line. Here that's 0.5; in the fraud screen it's 0.005. So an AP of 0.30 is worse than random at \(\pi = 0.5\) and strong (60 times random) at \(\pi = 0.005\). Always quote AP next to the prevalence.
  • It isn't monotone. Precision rises with each positive and falls with each negative, so the curve zigzags.

Average precision

The usual summary is average precision (AP), a step sum: each rise in recall is weighted by the precision at that point.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{AP} &= \sum_k (R_k - R_{k-1})\,P_k \\ &= \frac{1}{n_+}\sum_{\text{positives } i} \text{precision at the rank of } i \end{aligned} \]

In words: recall only rises when you pass a positive, by \(1/n_+\) each time, so AP is the average, over the positives, of the precision you have when you reach each one. Eight rows: the positives sit at ranks 1, 3, 4 and 5, where precision is 1, 2/3, 3/4 and 4/5, so \(\text{AP} = (1 + 0.667 + 0.75 + 0.8)/4 = 0.804\). AP cares most about the top: move B up to rank 1 and AP falls by 0.125 (to 0.679), while AUC falls by only 0.0625 (to 0.75).

  • AP is not the trapezoid under the PR curve. Joining PR points with straight lines invents operating points no threshold can achieve: between two real points, precision doesn't change linearly with recall (Davis and Goadrich, 2006). In ROC space a straight line is fine, because flipping a coin between two thresholds mixes their TPRs and FPRs linearly; precision is a ratio of counts, so the same mixture traces a curve, not a line. On the eight rows the trapezoid gives 0.767, the step sum 0.804. Which one is larger depends on the zigzag; here the trapezoid is lower because it averages each step's precision with the dip before it. When someone quotes “PR-AUC”, ask which one; scikit-learn's average_precision_score is the step sum.
  • Interpolated AP (older information-retrieval and object-detection benchmarks) first replaces each precision by the best precision at any recall at least as high, which smooths the zigzag and gives slightly higher numbers.

06Scoring probabilities: log loss and Brier

Ranking metrics can't tell 0.9 from 0.6 as long as the order holds. When the number itself is used (an expected-cost threshold, a price, a risk estimate shown to someone, a confidence that routes a case to a human), judge the probabilities directly, case by case.

Log loss

\[ \text{LogLoss} = -\frac1n\sum_i \big[y_i \log \hat p_i + (1 - y_i)\log(1 - \hat p_i)\big] \]

In words: the average surprise, \(-\log\) of the probability the model gave to what actually happened. It's the negative log-likelihood of a Bernoulli model, the same function as the training loss of logistic regression and most neural classifiers; where it comes from and its gradient are in the loss-functions note.   • Unbounded: a probability of 0.01 on something that happened costs 4.6; exactly 0 costs infinity. Libraries clip predictions to \([\varepsilon, 1 - \varepsilon]\). One confidently wrong case can dominate a comparison.
  • Use it when confident errors are truly costly, for choosing among probability models, and as the early-stopping metric when you need probabilities.

Brier score

\[ \text{Brier} = \frac1n\sum_i (\hat p_i - y_i)^2 \]

In words: the mean squared error of the probabilities against the 0/1 outcomes, between 0 and 1. Eight rows: 0.176. The base-rate predictor scores \(\pi(1 - \pi)\): 0.25 here, 0.005 at 0.5% prevalence. The Brier skill score \(1 - \text{Brier}/[\pi(1 - \pi)]\) is the share of that baseline's error removed: 0.295 here. On rare events, quote the skill score; a raw Brier of 0.0045 at 0.5% fraud removes only 10% of the do-nothing error.

Log loss or Brier? Both reward the right things. Log loss punishes confident mistakes without limit (B: 1.90 against Brier's 0.72) and is more sensitive among tiny probabilities, which matters for rare events. Brier is bounded and more forgiving of label noise. Report one of them, with its baseline.

Why these two: proper scoring rules

A proper scoring rule is one where, if the true chance is \(p\), your expected score is best when you report exactly \(p\). Then no model can gain by shading its probabilities. Suppose the true chance is 0.3 and the model reports \(q\):

The Brier decomposition: reliability, resolution, uncertainty

Group the cases by their forecast value (bins \(k\), \(n_k\) cases each, forecast \(f_k\), observed rate \(\bar o_k\), overall rate \(\bar o\)). The Brier score splits into three parts:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Brier} &= \text{REL} - \text{RES} + \text{UNC} \\ \text{REL} &= \frac1n\sum_k n_k (f_k - \bar o_k)^2 \\ \text{RES} &= \frac1n\sum_k n_k (\bar o_k - \bar o)^2 \\ \text{UNC} &= \bar o\,(1 - \bar o) \end{aligned} \]

In words: reliability (lower is better) is the calibration error, how far each forecast sits from what happened when it was issued; resolution (higher is better) is how much the groups' outcomes differ from the overall rate, i.e. how well the model separates cases; uncertainty is how hard the problem is, fixed by the base rate, and no model can change it. The split is exact when all cases in a bin share one forecast value (as in the example below); with binned continuous scores, two small within-bin terms are left over, so the parts add up only approximately.

By hand. A model gives 20 cases only two scores: 0.2 to ten of them (3 turn out positive) and 0.8 to the other ten (6 positive). Overall rate 9/20 = 0.45.

  • REL \(= [10(0.2 - 0.3)^2 + 10(0.8 - 0.6)^2]/20 = (0.1 + 0.4)/20 = 0.025\).
  • RES \(= [10(0.3 - 0.45)^2 + 10(0.6 - 0.45)^2]/20 = 0.0225\).
  • UNC \(= 0.45 \times 0.55 = 0.2475\).
  • Brier \(= 0.025 - 0.0225 + 0.2475 = 0.25\), matching the direct average of squared errors.

Read it: the model does separate cases (resolution 0.0225), but it's overconfident (0.2 and 0.8 where 0.3 and 0.6 happened), and that costs more than the separation earns, so it scores worse than predicting 0.45 for everyone (0.2475). Replace its two outputs with 0.3 and 0.6, which changes no ranking, and REL drops to 0: Brier \(= 0.2475 - 0.0225 = 0.225\). That is exactly what recalibration does (next section): it removes the reliability term and keeps the resolution.

Trap

With continuous scores, the decomposition depends on the bins. If every case gets its own bin, each observed rate is 0 or 1, the resolution equals the uncertainty, and the whole Brier score shows up as “reliability”. Bin coarsely enough that each bin's observed rate means something.

Say it in an interview: “When probabilities are used, I'd judge them with a proper score, log loss or Brier, which are minimized only by the true probabilities, and quote the skill against predicting the base rate, because on rare events the raw numbers are tiny. Brier splits into reliability minus resolution plus uncertainty: calibration error, separation, and the problem's difficulty. Recalibration removes the first without touching the second.”

07Calibration

A model is calibrated if, among the cases it gives probability \(q\), a share \(q\) turn out positive, for every \(q\):

\[ P(y = 1 \mid \hat p = q) = q \]

In words: “30%” means 30%. Calibration and ranking are separate properties: the base-rate predictor is perfectly calibrated and ranks nothing (AUC 0.5); a model with AUC 0.95 can have every probability off by a factor of two. Calibration matters whenever the number is used: expected-cost thresholds (section 03), pricing, expected loss, a lender's break-even rule (in the lifecycle note), combining models, comparing scores across segments, and deciding when a model's own confidence is high enough to act without a human.

ECE, and why not to trust it alone

\[ \text{ECE} = \sum_b \frac{n_b}{n}\,\big\lvert \bar o_b - \bar p_b \big\rvert \]

In words: the expected calibration error is the case-weighted average gap between the observed rate and the mean prediction in each bin, the average vertical distance from the diagonal. For the overconfident model: 0.089. Four pitfalls:

  • It's biased upwards by noise, more so with more bins. A perfectly calibrated model on 1,000 cases scores ECE 0.022 with 10 bins and 0.046 with 50 (averaged over 200 simulated test sets, with scores spread like the example above), purely from sampling noise in each bin. On 10,000 cases: 0.007 and 0.014. Compare ECEs only with the same bins and the same test size.
  • A useless model can score 0. Predicting the base rate for everyone puts every case in one bin whose mean prediction equals its observed rate: ECE 0, AUC 0.5. ECE measures reliability only, never resolution.
  • Equal-width bins can be nearly empty. Above, 82% of cases fall in the first bin and the top bin has 13 cases. Equal-mass (quantile) bins give every bin the same count; show the counts either way.
  • Multi-class “top-label” ECE checks only the confidence of the predicted class, not the rest of the probability vector.

So report ECE (or just the diagram) next to a proper score, log loss or Brier, never alone.

Fixing it: Platt, isotonic, temperature

All three learn a monotone map from the model's score to a probability, fitted on held-out data the model wasn't trained on (training-set predictions are overconfident, especially for trees and boosting), and never on the test set. With little data, cross-fit: train the model on \(k - 1\) folds, fit the calibrator on the remaining fold, repeat (scikit-learn's CalibratedClassifierCV).

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Platt: } \hat p &= \sigma(a\,s + b) \\ \text{Temperature: } \hat p &= \operatorname{softmax}(z / T) \\ \text{Isotonic: } \hat p &= m(s), \ m \text{ non-decreasing} \end{aligned} \]

In words: Platt fits a two-parameter logistic regression with the model's score \(s\) (or its log-odds) as the only input; temperature scaling divides a network's logits \(z\) by one learned number \(T\), the same for every class; isotonic regression fits any non-decreasing step function.

For two classes, temperature scaling is Platt with \(b\) fixed at 0 and \(a = 1/T\), which is why the two fits agree here (\(a = 0.495\), \(1/T = 0.50\)). With several classes one \(T\) rescales every logit, so each row's top class stays the top class, but a class's probabilities can reorder across rows: logits (3, 0, 0) and (1, 0.9, −10) give class 0 probabilities 0.909 and 0.525 at \(T = 1\), but 0.403 and 0.430 at \(T = 10\), so one-vs-rest AUC can shift slightly. Multi-class calibration is checked per class (classwise ECE) or with the multi-class Brier score, the squared error summed over the probability vector.

Isotonic by hand: pool adjacent violators (PAV). Sort the eight cases by score, lowest first, and read their labels: H 0, G 0, F 0, E 1, D 1, C 1, B 0, A 1. The fitted values must never decrease. Go left to right, and whenever a new value is below the block before it, merge them into one block holding their average; keep merging backwards while the order is still violated.

  1. H, G, F: 0, 0, 0. E, D, C: 1, 1, 1. Fine so far.
  2. B is 0, below the block of 1s. Merge B with C: (1 + 0)/2 = 0.5. Still below D's 1: merge, (1 + 1 + 0)/3 = 0.67. Still below E's 1: merge, 3/4 = 0.75. Now above F's 0: stop.
  3. A is 1, above 0.75. Fine.

Result: H, G, F → 0; E, D, C, B → 0.75; A → 1. Each block's value is its observed positive rate, which is why isotonic fits are calibrated on the data they were fitted to.

Trap

Scoring a calibrator on the rows it was fitted to. In-sample, the isotonic fit above turns B's three lost pairs into ties, so AUC “rises” from 0.8125 to 0.906 and the Brier score “improves” from 0.176 to 0.094. None of that is real. Fit on one held-out set, evaluate on another.

Class weights, undersampling and the prior-shift correction

Train on data whose positive share \(\pi_s\) differs from the real one \(\pi\) (by undersampling negatives, oversampling positives, or weighting them), and the model learns the training odds. If only the base rate differs (each class's features look the same), the fix is to rescale the odds:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{odds}_{\text{true}} &= \text{odds}_{\text{model}} \times \frac{\pi/(1 - \pi)}{\pi_s/(1 - \pi_s)} \\ \hat p &= \frac{\text{odds}_{\text{true}}}{1 + \text{odds}_{\text{true}}} \end{aligned} \]

In words: multiply the model's odds by the ratio of the real base-rate odds to the training base-rate odds. A model trained on a 50/50 sample and deployed where 1% are positive: its 0.5 means 1%, its 0.9 means 8.3%, and it takes 0.99 to mean 50%. Undersampling negatives at rate \(q\) is the special case \(\operatorname{logit} \hat p = \operatorname{logit} \hat p_s + \log q\) in the data-preparation note; class weight \(w\) on positives acts like \(q = 1/w\). The same correction is derived by Bayes' rule in the regression note.

  • The same formula handles a deployment shift in the base rate (fraud rises from 0.5% to 1%), with \(\pi_s\) the training rate and \(\pi\) the new one, under the same assumption that only the mix changed.
  • It changes no ranking, so AUC is untouched; precision, log loss and every cost threshold do change.
  • When the assumption fails (the kinds of fraud change, not just their number), refit a calibrator on recent labelled data instead.

Say it in an interview: “Calibrated means 30% happens 30% of the time. I'd check it with a reliability diagram and a proper score, not ECE alone, since ECE depends on the bins and is zero for the base-rate predictor. To fix it I'd fit Platt or temperature scaling on held-out data when the distortion is a simple scaling, isotonic when there's lots of data; they keep the ranking. If I'd undersampled or weighted classes, I'd first rescale the odds by the ratio of the real to the training base-rate odds.”

08Ranking metrics

Search, recommendations, retrieval for a language model, and review queues all return an ordered list per query (a search, a user, a question, a day's queue). Each item has a relevance label, binary (relevant or not) or graded (0 to 3, say). A ranking metric scores one list; the reported number is its average over queries.

The worked example. One query returns five documents. The corpus holds four relevant documents for it: grades 3, 2, 2 and 1. The system retrieved three of them; the fourth (grade 2) is missing from its list.

Precision@k, recall@k, hit rate

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{P@}k &= \frac{\#\text{relevant in the top } k}{k} \\ \text{R@}k &= \frac{\#\text{relevant in the top } k}{\#\text{relevant in total}} \end{aligned} \]

In words: precision@k is the share of the shown slots that are useful; recall@k is the share of everything useful that got shown. Here P@3 = 2/3 and R@3 = 2/4 = 0.5. Hit rate@k is 1 if at least one relevant item is in the top \(k\), else 0; averaged over queries, it's the share of queries with something useful shown. When each query has exactly one relevant item (one gold passage), recall@k and hit rate@k are the same number. Neither cares about the order within the top \(k\).

Reciprocal rank and MRR

\[ \text{MRR} = \frac{1}{\lvert Q\rvert}\sum_{q \in Q} \frac{1}{\text{rank of the first relevant item for } q} \]

In words: for each query, 1 over the position of the first useful result (0 if there is none), averaged over queries. Here the first relevant item is at rank 2: reciprocal rank 0.5. Across three queries whose first hits are at ranks 2, 1 and nowhere: \((0.5 + 1 + 0)/3 = 0.5\). It suits tasks where one good answer ends the search: a navigational query, a question with one right passage. It ignores everything after the first hit.

Average precision and MAP

The same AP as section 05, per query: the mean of the precision at the rank of each relevant item, over all relevant items, counting a relevant item that was never retrieved as precision 0:

\[ \text{AP} = \frac{1}{4}\Big(\tfrac12 + \tfrac23 + \tfrac35 + 0\Big) = \frac{1.767}{4} = 0.442 \]

In words: relevant items arrive at ranks 2, 3 and 5, with precision 1/2, 2/3 and 3/5 at those points, and the fourth relevant document never arrives. MAP is AP averaged over queries. It rewards putting every relevant item high, with binary labels.

Trap

Dividing by the number of relevant items retrieved (3) instead of the number that exist (4) gives 0.589 and hides the miss. Libraries and papers differ; check which denominator a quoted MAP uses.

nDCG: graded relevance with a position discount

When relevance is graded and position matters (users look at the top few), discounted cumulative gain adds each item's gain, discounted by how far down it sits:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{DCG@}k &= \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{2^{\text{rel}_i} - 1}{\log_2(i + 1)} \\ \text{nDCG@}k &= \frac{\text{DCG@}k}{\text{IDCG@}k} \end{aligned} \]

In words: a grade-3 item is worth \(2^3 - 1 = 7\), a grade-2 item 3, a grade-1 item 1; the item at position \(i\) is divided by \(\log_2(i + 1)\) (1 at the top, 1.585 at position 2, 2 at position 3). IDCG is the DCG of the best possible ordering of all the relevant items, so nDCG runs from 0 to 1. By hand, \(k = 3\):

The metric decides what the model predicts

Each metric is minimized by a different summary of the outcome's distribution, so choosing the metric chooses what the model will learn to output. MAE or RMSE

  • RMSE punishes large errors more than proportionally: one 20-unit miss counts as much as four 10-unit misses. Use it when a big miss really is disproportionately bad, and when the mean is what you need.
  • MAE is robust to outliers and easy to explain: “off by 11 units on average”.
  • Their ratio is a diagnostic: \(\text{MAE} \le \text{RMSE} \le \sqrt n\,\text{MAE}\). Equal means every error has the same size; a ratio far above 1 means a few large errors dominate. Here 12.04/11 = 1.09: errors are similar in size.

MAPE, and why forecasters distrust it

\[ \text{MAPE} = \frac{100\%}{n}\sum_i \left\lvert \frac{y_i - \hat y_i}{y_i} \right\rvert \]

In words: the average error as a share of the actual. It's scale-free and easy for a business audience, which is why it's popular. Its problems:

  1. Zeros and near-zeros. At \(y = 0\) it's undefined (item 5); near zero it explodes. Intermittent demand, with many zero days, breaks it.
  2. Small actuals dominate. A 10-unit miss on a 10-unit item counts 100%; the same miss on an 80-unit item 12.5%. Item 4 alone contributes 25 of the 37.3 points.
  3. It biases forecasts low. For a known actual it's just absolute error divided by that actual, symmetric around it (for an actual of 100, forecasts of 50 and 150 both cost 50%). But you forecast before the actual is known, and each possible outcome's miss is divided by that outcome: if demand turns out low, the miss is a big percentage; if high, a small one. So the forecast that minimizes expected MAPE is a median weighting each outcome by \(1/y\), pulled towards small values. In ratio terms: forecasting double the actual costs 100%, half of it only 50%.

The bias in numbers. Demand of 20, 40, 60, 80 and 100 is perfectly symmetric: mean and median are both 60. The constant with the lowest MAPE is 40 (MAPE 48.7%, against 63% at 60). A model tuned on MAPE will under-forecast systematically, and in inventory that means stock-outs.

The alternatives:

  • sMAPE (symmetric MAPE) divides by the average of actual and forecast: \(2\lvert y - \hat y\rvert/(\lvert y\rvert + \lvert \hat y\rvert)\). It's bounded (0 to 200%), but it's 200% whenever exactly one of the two is zero, whatever the size of the miss (item 5: a 5-unit miss scores 200%, pulling the five-item sMAPE to 63.8%). And it isn't symmetric either: for an actual of 100, forecasting 50 costs 66.7% but 150 costs 40%, so now under-forecasts are punished more. Definitions vary between papers, so always write the formula.
  • WAPE = MAE / mean actual (21.2% here). Volume-weighted, fine with zeros (unless everything is zero), and what most demand-planning teams report for aggregated demand.
  • MASE (mean absolute scaled error): MAE divided by the in-sample MAE of a naive forecast (tomorrow = today, or = the same day last week). Below 1 beats the naive forecast. Comparable across series of different scales, and fine with zeros unless the training series is constant.
  • RMSLE, the RMSE of \(\log(1 + y)\): measures relative error, for positive targets spanning orders of magnitude.

R² and adjusted R²

\[ \begin{aligned} R^2 &= 1 - \frac{\sum_i (y_i - \hat y_i)^2}{\sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2} \\ R^2_{\text{adj}} &= 1 - (1 - R^2)\,\frac{n - 1}{n - p - 1} \end{aligned} \]

In words: R² is the share of the variance around the mean that the model removes, i.e. MSE measured against the “always predict the mean” baseline. Five forecasts: \(1 - 725/9{,}880 = 0.927\). Adjusted R² charges for the number of features \(p\): with \(n = 50\) and \(p = 10\), an R² of 0.40 adjusts to 0.25.

  • It can be negative on test data: worse than predicting the test mean. In training, with an intercept, it can't.
  • It depends on the test set's spread. The same model and the same errors give a higher R² on a more varied test set, so don't compare R² across datasets.
  • Adjusted R² is an in-sample correction for linear models (regression note: training R² never falls when a feature is added). For held-out data, just report test R² or RMSE: the held-out score is already honest.

Pinball loss and prediction intervals

\[ \rho_\tau(e) = \max\big(\tau\,e,\ (\tau - 1)\,e\big), \quad e = y - \hat y \]

In words: an under-forecast (\(e \gt 0\)) costs \(\tau\) per unit and an over-forecast \(1 - \tau\) per unit. At \(\tau = 0.9\), an actual of 100: forecasting 80 costs \(0.9 \times 20 = 18\), forecasting 120 costs \(0.1 \times 20 = 2\). Running out is nine times as costly as overstocking, so the forecast settles at the 90th percentile. At \(\tau = 0.5\) it's half the absolute error. A pair of quantile forecasts (0.05 and 0.95) makes a 90% prediction interval; check it by coverage: about 90% of actuals should fall inside, and the interval should be as narrow as possible while they do. Split conformal prediction turns any model into intervals: on held-out calibration rows compute the absolute errors, take the \(\lceil (n + 1)(1 - \alpha) \rceil\)-th smallest as \(q\), and predict \(\hat y \pm q\). With 19 calibration errors and \(\alpha = 0.1\), \(q\) is the 18th smallest. Coverage is at least \(1 - \alpha\) on average over cases, provided new data are exchangeable with the calibration rows (no drift); conformalized quantile regression applies the same correction to the 0.05 and 0.95 quantile forecasts, so intervals stay wider where the data are noisier.

Evaluating forecasts

  • Backtest from many origins: roll the forecast origin forward (the time-series splits in section 02) and average, so one unusual period doesn't decide.
  • Report error by horizon: error grows with how far ahead you forecast; one average over days 1 to 28 hides that day 1 is fine and day 28 is poor. Report the horizon the decision uses.
  • Compare with naive and seasonal-naive at every horizon. Many “models” don't beat last week's value; MASE builds that comparison in.
  • Intervals for the metric need a block bootstrap (resample whole weeks), because forecast errors are correlated in time.

10Comparing models honestly

“B scores 0.822 and A 0.805” is not yet a finding. Every metric is an estimate from a finite test set; the question is whether the gap is bigger than the noise, measured the right way.

How noisy is one number?

  • A rate on independent rows has standard error \(\sqrt{a(1 - a)/n}\). Accuracy 0.90 on 1,000 rows: 0.0095, so a 95% interval of about ±1.9 points. A “0.5-point gain” judged against that alone is noise.
  • The effective \(n\) is the relevant denominator, not the test-set size: predicted positives for precision, actual positives for recall. With 20 positives, recall moves in steps of 5 points.
  • Small counts need the Wilson interval. Recall of 20/20 gives a standard error of 0 by the formula above, so a useless interval [1, 1]. Wilson gives [0.84, 1.00]. For 18/20 the textbook (Wald) interval runs above 1, to 1.03; Wilson gives [0.70, 0.97].
  • AUC's noise is set by the rarer class. With 300 positives, AUC near 0.8 has a standard error of about 0.013 in the bootstrap below and about 0.016 by Hanley and McNeil's formula (it depends on the score distributions), so roughly ±0.025 to ±0.03; the lifecycle note sizes a test set this way (holdout size).
  • Correlated rows shrink \(n\). Ten rows from one customer are closer to one row than to ten.

Bootstrap intervals, and why to pair them

For metrics with no simple formula (AUC, AP, F1, WAPE), resample the test rows with replacement, recompute the metric each time, and take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. How the bootstrap works and where it fails is in the hypothesis-testing note; what's specific to model evaluation:

  • Stratify by class (resample positives and negatives separately) when positives are rare, or some resamples have none and AUC is undefined.
  • Resample the independent unit: whole customers or documents (a cluster bootstrap), whole weeks for time series (a block bootstrap), whole queries for ranking metrics.
  • Pair the comparison. To compare two models, resample the same row indices for both and record the difference each time.

Why pairing matters, in numbers. Two models scored on one test set of 10,000 rows (300 positives). They share most of their inputs, so their scores correlate at 0.87 and they tend to get the same rows wrong.

In words: how far the split of disagreements is from 50/50, in chi-square units with one degree of freedom (the −1 is a continuity correction). \(p = 0.057\): a 2-point accuracy gap that is not quite significant at 5%. Comparing on cross-validation folds

The tempting test: run 10-fold CV for both models, take the 10 per-fold differences, and do a paired t-test. The problem is that fold scores aren't independent: any two training sets share 8/9 of their rows, and every fold comes from the same dataset, so the folds' differences move together. With \(J\) fold differences whose variance is \(S^2\), the t-test estimates the variance of their mean as \(S^2/J\), which understates it. Nadeau and Bengio's corrected resampled t-test inflates it:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(\bar d) &\approx S^2\Big(\frac1J + \frac{n_{\text{test}}}{n_{\text{train}}}\Big) \\ t &= \frac{\bar d}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\bar d)}}, \quad J - 1 \text{ degrees of freedom} \end{aligned} \]

In words: \(J\) is the number of train/test results, \(S^2\) the variance of the \(J\) differences, and the extra term accounts for the overlap between training sets. For one run of 10-fold, \(1/10 + 1/9 = 0.211\) instead of 0.1: the variance doubles and \(t\) shrinks by 1.45.

Worked: B beats A by 0.6 points on average over 10 folds, with a standard deviation of 0.8 points across folds. Naive: \(t = 2.37\), \(p = 0.042\), “significant”. Corrected: \(t = 1.63\), \(p = 0.137\), not.

How wrong the naive test is, simulated. Two logistic regressions with equally informative feature sets (so neither is better), compared on 300 datasets of 200 rows. Share of comparisons declared significant at 5%:

Repeating CV adds more results, but not more independent data: with \(J = 100\) the naive test divides the variance by 100 and declares most ties “significant”. The correction tracks the nominal 5% there; on a single 10-fold run it's conservative. Two habits help as much as any test: look at the per-fold differences (B winning on 10 of 10 folds is strong evidence; 6 of 10 is not), and confirm on a held-out test set with a paired bootstrap.

Many models, many slices

  • The best of many looks lucky. Comparing 20 models or 20 segments and reporting the winner is a multiple-comparisons problem (corrections in the hypothesis-testing note); the winner's score is optimistic for the same reason tuning on CV is (section 02).
  • Simpson's paradox: a metric can be better overall and worse in every segment when the segment mix differs between the two numbers compared (last month's model on last month's data against this month's). Two models scored on the same rows with a row-averaged metric can't reverse this way.
  • Slices and fairness: report the key metric per segment with its interval; an average can hide a segment where the model fails. Fairness checks compare error rates across groups: equal opportunity asks for equal TPR, equalized odds for equal TPR and FPR, demographic parity for equal flag rates. When base rates differ between groups, calibration within each group and equal error rates can't all hold at once (unless the model is perfect).
  • Practical significance: a real 0.003 AUC gain may not be worth a more complex model. Translate the gain into the business unit (frauds caught at capacity, rupees) before deciding.

Say it in an interview: “I'd compare on the same rows, paired. A paired bootstrap of the AUC difference cut the standard error from 0.018 to 0.0075 in one example, because both models share the test set's luck. For accuracy, McNemar on the disagreements. For CV, fold scores are correlated, so a naive t-test on 10-by-10 CV called about half of equal models different (57% in this simulation); the Nadeau–Bengio correction adds \(n_{\text{test}}/n_{\text{train}}\) to the variance factor.”

ROC curve and AUC by sorting

def roc_curve(y, s):
    order = argsort(-s, kind="stable")   # best first
    y, s = y[order], s[order]
    P, N = sum(y), len(y) - sum(y)
    fpr, tpr = [0.0], [0.0]
    tp = fp = 0
    for i in range(len(y)):
        tp += y[i]; fp += 1 - y[i]
        # one point per distinct score, after
        # the whole tie group is counted
        if i == len(y) - 1 or s[i+1] != s[i]:
            fpr.append(fp / N); tpr.append(tp / P)
    return fpr, tpr

def auc_trapezoid(x, h):           # x ascending
    return sum((x[i] - x[i-1]) * (h[i] + h[i-1]) / 2
               for i in range(1, len(x)))

Average precision

def average_precision(y, s):
    order = argsort(-s, kind="stable")
    y, s = y[order], s[order]
    P = sum(y); tp = fp = 0
    ap, prev_r = 0.0, 0.0
    for i in range(len(y)):
        tp += y[i]; fp += 1 - y[i]
        if i == len(y) - 1 or s[i+1] != s[i]:
            r = tp / P
            ap += (r - prev_r) * tp / (tp + fp)  # step
            prev_r = r
    return ap

# eight rows → 0.804 (= mean of 1, 2/3, 3/4, 4/5)

def bootstrap_auc_ci(y, s, s2=None, B=2000,
                     alpha=0.05, seed=0):
    rng = default_rng(seed)
    pos, neg = where(y == 1), where(y == 0)
    reps = []
    for _ in range(B):
        # stratified: every resample has both classes
        idx = concat(rng.choice(pos, len(pos)),
                     rng.choice(neg, len(neg)))
        a = auc(y[idx], s[idx])
        if s2 is not None:          # paired: same rows
            a = auc(y[idx], s2[idx]) - a
        reps.append(a)
    return percentile(reps, [100*alpha/2,
                             100*(1 - alpha/2)])

Isotonic calibration: pool adjacent violators

def pav(y_sorted):          # labels sorted by score, low first
    blocks = []             # [sum, count] per block
    for v in y_sorted:
        blocks.append([v, 1])
        # merge backwards while the order is violated
        while (len(blocks) > 1 and
               mean(blocks[-2]) > mean(blocks[-1])):
            s, c = blocks.pop()
            blocks[-1][0] += s; blocks[-1][1] += c
    return [b[0] / b[1] for b in blocks
            for _ in range(b[1])]   # one value per row

14Interview questions

Answer out loud first, then open the answer. Each answer stands on its own: the short answer, the mechanism, the why, then the trade-off or the likely follow-up. Questions 7, 14, 27 and 28 are hand drills; do them with a pen.

1Why do you need a test set if you already cross-validate?

Because the CV score was used to make choices, so it's optimistic; the test set, touched once, is not.

  • Selection inflates scores: picking the best of many settings by CV keeps the one whose noise happened to be favourable (the winner's curse). On pure-noise labels, the best of 60 settings scored 0.578 accuracy in CV against a truth of 0.50.
  • Every look spends a set: features, hyperparameters, the threshold and the model family were all chosen on CV.
  • The test set also checks the split: an out-of-time or new-customer test checks what random CV folds can't (drift, unseen groups).

Follow-up: with too little data for a separate test set, use nested CV, whose outer loop plays the test set's role. (02)

2Walk me through k-fold, stratified, grouped and time-series CV. When do you use each?

Pick the scheme that copies how the model will meet new data.

  • k-fold: shuffle, split into \(k\) folds, train on \(k - 1\), score the last, rotate, average. For independent rows; \(k\) = 5–10.
  • Stratified: each fold keeps the class mix. Default for classification; essential with rare classes (with 20 positives in 5 random folds, 32% of shuffles leave some fold with 0 or 1 positive).
  • Grouped: all rows of a customer, patient or document stay together. Needed when deployment means new groups; otherwise the model is graded on recognizing them (random folds AUC 0.75, grouped 0.63, truly new customers 0.59 in one simulation).
  • Time series: train on the past, validate on the next period, expanding or rolling window, with a gap as long as the label horizon.

Follow-up: stratified group k-fold combines the middle two; with lots of data, a single train/validation/test split is enough. (02)

3Why can't you shuffle time-series data into k folds? What is a gap for?

Shuffled folds train on the future to predict the past, and turn forecasting into interpolation; the score comes out optimistic.

  • Leakage through time: trends, regime changes and features computed over the whole series flow backwards.
  • Autocorrelation: neighbouring time points are near-copies, so a validation row is predicted from its neighbours on both sides.
  • Mismatch with deployment, which always predicts forward, often after a delay.
  • The gap drops rows between train and test when labels span a horizon (the last training labels are only observed during the test period), when lagged or rolling features would straddle the cut, or to copy a real deployment delay.

Follow-up: expanding vs rolling windows (rolling when old data is stale); purging and embargo when history is short. (02)

4What is nested cross-validation, and what does its score estimate?

Two loops: an inner CV that tunes inside each outer training part, and an outer CV that scores the tuned model on data the tuning never saw.

  • Why: reporting the best inner CV score is optimistic (0.578 vs 0.505 nested vs 0.50 truth on noise labels, 60 settings).
  • Mechanics: for each outer fold, run a full grid search with inner CV on the outer training part, refit the best setting on that whole part, score it once on the outer test fold. Average the outer scores.
  • What it estimates: the performance of the whole procedure (“search this grid, then fit”), not of one setting. Outer folds may pick different settings.
  • Final model: rerun the inner search on all the data.

Follow-up: cost is \(k_{\text{out}} \times k_{\text{in}} \times\) grid size fits; with plenty of data a held-out test set does the same job. (02)

5How noisy is a 5-fold CV estimate, and how would you report it?

Noisier than the fold spread suggests: report the repeated-CV mean with the fold standard error as a rough, optimistic guide (the reshuffle spread, 0.011 here, measures only partition luck; the real uncertainty was 0.029), and compare models with paired fold differences.

  • Folds vary: on 300 rows, five fold accuracies ranged from 0.633 to 0.783.
  • The partition alone matters: 50 reshuffles of the same rows gave CV means from 0.687 to 0.743. Repeated CV averages that away.
  • Fold scores aren't independent (overlapping training sets, one dataset), so fold sd / \(\sqrt k\) understates the uncertainty: 0.024 against a true 0.029 in simulation. There is no unbiased estimator of k-fold CV's variance.

Follow-up: to compare two models, look at per-fold differences and use the corrected resampled t-test. (02, 10)

6Define precision, recall, specificity, FPR and NPV. Which depend on prevalence?

Recall, specificity and FPR condition on the true class and don't depend on prevalence (under pure label shift); precision and NPV condition on the prediction and do.

  • Recall (TPR) = TP / (TP + FN): of real positives, how many caught. Specificity = TN / (TN + FP). FPR = FP / (FP + TN) = 1 − specificity.
  • Precision = TP / (TP + FP): of flags, how many real. NPV = TN / (TN + FN).
  • The link: precision \(= \text{TPR}\,\pi / (\text{TPR}\,\pi + \text{FPR}(1 - \pi))\). TPR 1.0 and FPR 0.25 give precision 0.80 at \(\pi = 0.5\) but 0.02 at \(\pi = 0.005\).

Follow-up: “doesn't depend on prevalence” fails when the case mix changes too (spectrum shift); then TPR, FPR and AUC move as well. (03)

Precision 0.40, recall 0.80, F1 0.533, F2 0.667, FPR 0.012, accuracy 0.986, MCC 0.56.

  • \(n = 10{,}000\), prevalence \((80 + 20)/10{,}000 = 1\%\).
  • Precision \(= 80/200 = 0.40\); recall \(= 80/100 = 0.80\).
  • F1 \(= 2 \times 80/(160 + 120 + 20) = 160/300 = 0.533\). F2 \(= 5PR/(4P + R) = 1.6/2.4 = 0.667\); F0.5 = 0.444.
  • FPR \(= 120/9{,}900 = 0.0121\); specificity 0.988; NPV \(= 9{,}780/9{,}800 = 0.998\).
  • Accuracy \(= 9{,}860/10{,}000 = 0.986\), below the 0.99 of flagging nobody. Balanced accuracy 0.894.

8Your classifier has 99% accuracy. Is it good?

Unknown until you compare with the base rate: if 99% of cases are negative, predicting “negative” for everything also scores 99%.

  • The accuracy paradox: at 0.5% fraud, flagging nothing is 99.5% accurate and catches nothing.
  • Ask for: the confusion matrix, precision and recall on the positive class, AP against the prevalence, or the cost at the operating threshold.
  • Imbalance-aware single numbers: balanced accuracy (constant predictor 0.5), MCC (constant predictor 0).

Follow-up: accuracy is fine when classes are balanced and both errors cost about the same. (03)

9Why is F1 a harmonic mean? When would you use F2 or F0.5?

The harmonic mean is dominated by the smaller value, so a model can't score well by maxing precision or recall alone.

  • Example: \(P = 1\), \(R = 0.01\) (flag one sure case): F1 = 0.0198; the arithmetic mean would say 0.505.
  • Counts form: \(F_1 = 2TP/(2TP + FP + FN)\): false positives and false negatives weigh equally; true negatives are ignored.
  • F-beta: \(F_\beta = (1 + \beta^2)PR/(\beta^2 P + R)\) treats recall as \(\beta\) times as important. F2 for screening (a miss is worse), F0.5 for automatic actions (a false alarm is worse). At \(P = 0.4\), \(R = 0.8\): F1 0.533, F2 0.667, F0.5 0.444.

Follow-up: if the costs are known, minimize expected cost instead; F1 has no cost knob. (03)

10Macro, micro or weighted F1?

Macro when every class matters equally (rare ones included), micro when every case matters equally, weighted as a size-weighted macro; always show per-class numbers.

  • Macro: plain mean of per-class F1s. A three-class example: 0.890, 0.718, 0.400 → 0.669; the rare class pulls it down.
  • Micro: pool TP, FP, FN across classes. In single-label multi-class it equals accuracy (0.82 here), because each mistake is one FP and one FN.
  • Weighted: per-class F1 weighted by support: 0.807, close to micro, hiding the weak rare class (recall 0.30).

Follow-up: multi-label adds “samples” averaging (F1 per row, then the mean). (03)

11How do you choose a classification threshold? Derive the cost-based one.

From the decision: minimize expected cost with calibrated probabilities, or meet a constraint (capacity, minimum recall or precision); choose on validation data, never on test.

  1. Flagging a case with probability \(p\) costs \((1 - p)\,C_{FP}\) in expectation; not flagging costs \(p\,C_{FN}\).
  2. Flag when \((1 - p)C_{FP} \lt p\,C_{FN}\), i.e. \(p \gt t^* = C_{FP}/(C_{FP} + C_{FN})\).
  3. With costs on correct decisions too: \(t^* = (C_{FP} - C_{TN})/[(C_{FP} - C_{TN}) + (C_{FN} - C_{TP})]\). If every flag costs a 5-unit review (fraud included) and a miss 100: \(t^* = 0.05\).
  • Uncalibrated scores: sweep thresholds on validation data and take the lowest total cost.
  • Capacity: the team reviews \(k\) a day, so flag the top \(k\): precision@k.

Follow-up: the threshold drifts with prevalence; monitor the flag rate. (03)

12Youden's J, F1-optimal or cost-optimal: why do they give different thresholds on a fraud problem?

Each assumes a different exchange rate between misses and false alarms; Youden assumes the one that only fits balanced problems.

  • On the ROC curve, the cost-optimal point is where the slope equals \(\frac{1 - \pi}{\pi}\cdot\frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FN}}\). Youden's J (max TPR − FPR) picks slope 1, which is cost-optimal only if a miss costs \((1 - \pi)/\pi\) false alarms: 199 at 0.5% fraud.
  • Fraud screen (AUC 0.95, 0.5% fraud, miss = 50 false alarms): Youden flags 12,617 a day at 3.5% precision (cost 15,238); cost-optimal flags 4,285 at 8.3% (cost 11,042); F1-optimal flags 444 at 37% but misses two thirds (cost 17,061).
  • F1-optimal threshold on calibrated probabilities is half the best F1 (0.174 = 0.348/2): it ignores the cost ratio.

Follow-up: in practice the binding constraint is often capacity: the cost optimum wanted four times what the team can review. (03)

13What does AUC mean probabilistically? How does it relate to the Mann–Whitney test?

AUC is the probability that a randomly chosen positive gets a higher score than a randomly chosen negative, ties counting half; that's the Mann–Whitney U statistic divided by \(n_+ n_-\).

  • Why the area equals pair counting: slice the area under the ROC staircase into one column per negative; each column's height is the share of positives ranked above that negative. Summing columns counts winning pairs.
  • Rank formula: \(\text{AUC} = (W_+ - n_+(n_+ + 1)/2)/(n_+ n_-)\), with \(W_+\) the positives' rank sum (ties averaged). Eight rows: \((23 - 10)/16 = 0.8125\).
  • Reading it: AUC 0.8 means 80% of (positive, negative) pairs are ordered correctly.

Follow-up: Gini \(= 2\,\text{AUC} - 1\). (04)

AUC = 10.5/16 = 0.656.

15Write the pseudocode for ROC-AUC.

Sort by score, walk down accumulating TP and FP, emit a point per distinct score, and integrate with the trapezoid rule; or use the rank formula.

  1. Sort rows by score, highest first. \(P\) = positives, \(N\) = negatives.
  2. Start at (0, 0). For each row add \(y\) to TP and \(1 - y\) to FP.
  3. At the end of each group of tied scores, append (FP/\(N\), TP/\(P\)).
  4. AUC \(= \sum_i (x_i - x_{i-1})(h_i + h_{i-1})/2\).
  • Rank version: average ranks for ties, \(W_+\) = positives' rank sum, AUC \(= (W_+ - P(P + 1)/2)/(PN)\). One sort: \(O(n \log n)\).
  • Pitfalls: a point per row instead of per tie group makes AUC depend on sort order; fail loudly if only one class is present.

16Why is AUC invariant to monotone transforms, and what follows from that?

AUC depends only on the order of the scores, and a strictly increasing transform doesn't change the order; so AUC can't see calibration.

  • Example: halving the eight rows' probabilities leaves AUC at 0.8125 while log loss goes from 0.540 to 0.656 and Brier from 0.176 to 0.244.
  • Consequences: a great AUC says nothing about whether 0.3 means 30%; Platt and binary temperature scaling never change AUC; isotonic can, slightly, because its flat steps create ties.
  • Use: compare rankings with AUC; judge probabilities with log loss, Brier and a reliability diagram.

Follow-up: model A has the higher AUC, model B the lower log loss. If only the order is used (a top-k queue, a fixed approval rate), A; if the numbers feed decisions (a cost threshold, pricing), B, or recalibrate A on held-out data, which keeps its AUC, and compare log loss again. (04)

17AUC is 0.95, but the fraud team says the model is useless. What could be going on?

Most likely, AUC is measuring the wrong part of the ranking: at the team's operating point, precision is poor.

  • Imbalance: at 0.5% fraud, the top 1,000 flags hold 231 frauds and 769 false alarms (precision 23%), though that's an FPR of only 0.77%, which looks superb on a ROC plot.
  • Easy pairs: AUC counts about 50 million (fraud, legitimate) pairs equally; nearly all are won for free against obviously legitimate cases.
  • Other causes: a leaking feature inflating offline AUC; miscalibrated scores used as probabilities; predictions too late to act on; the wrong unit (per row vs per customer); a proxy label the business doesn't care about.

Follow-up: report precision and recall at capacity, partial AUC, or AP with the prevalence. (04)

18What is partial AUC, and why does only a tiny slice of the ROC curve matter when you can act on few cases?

Partial AUC is the area under the ROC curve over only the false-positive rates you can operate at; with capacity for 1% of cases, that's FPR from 0 to about 1%, and it holds well under 1% of the total area.

  • The slice: 100,000 cases a day, 500 positive, 1,000 reviews. You can't flag more than 1,000, so FP ≤ 1,000 and FPR ≤ 1,000/99,500 ≈ 1%. Every usable threshold lives in the leftmost 1% of the plot.
  • How little area: the slice can hold at most 0.01; a model with AUC 0.95 got 0.00375 there, 0.4% of its AUC.
  • Same AUC, different slice: two models with AUC 0.95 caught 231 and 362 frauds in the top 1,000; their mean TPR across the slice was 0.375 and 0.692. Their curves cross outside the slice.
  • Computing it: integrate TPR over FPR in [0, 0.01], divide by 0.01 for the average recall across the slice; scikit-learn's max_fpr reports a rescaled version (0.5 random, 1 perfect).

Follow-up: simpler alternatives answer the same question: recall at a fixed FPR, precision@k. (04)

19ROC-AUC or PR-AUC under heavy imbalance? What's the PR baseline?

Average precision (PR-AUC) when the rare positives are what you act on; its random baseline is the prevalence, not 0.5.

  • Why PR: precision counts false alarms per flag, which is what a reviewer experiences; FPR divides them by a huge negative count and hides them.
  • Sensitivity to the top: 300 false alarms pushed above every fraud cut AP from 0.302 to 0.124; ROC-AUC fell by 0.003.
  • Prevalence: the same model's AP is 0.951 at 50% prevalence, 0.662 at 5%, 0.302 at 0.5%, with AUC 0.95 throughout. So quote AP with the base rate and never compare across base rates.
  • When ROC: both classes matter, or rankings are compared across populations with different base rates.

Follow-up: dominance in ROC space is equivalent to dominance in PR space, but the higher-AUC model need not have the higher AP when curves cross. (05)

20How is average precision computed, and why isn't it the trapezoid under the PR curve?

AP is a step sum, \(\sum_k (R_k - R_{k-1})P_k\): the mean of the precision at the rank of each positive. The trapezoid joins PR points with straight lines that no threshold can achieve.

  • Eight rows: positives at ranks 1, 3, 4, 5 with precision 1, 2/3, 3/4, 4/5: AP = 0.804. The trapezoid gives 0.767.
  • Why lines are wrong: between two achievable points, precision doesn't change linearly with recall (Davis and Goadrich), so linear interpolation invents operating points. (In ROC space a line between two points is achievable by randomizing between the thresholds.)
  • Variants: interpolated AP (precision replaced by the best at any recall at least as high), 11-point AP, COCO mAP over IoU thresholds. Ask which one a quoted number is.

(05)

21What are Gini and KS in credit scoring, and how do they relate to AUC?

Gini = 2·AUC − 1, AUC rescaled so random is 0 and perfect is 1; KS = the largest gap between the cumulative score distributions of goods and bads, which is the maximum of TPR − FPR.

  • Eight rows: AUC 0.8125 → Gini 0.625; KS 0.75, at the top-five cut.
  • Gini summarizes the whole ranking; KS one point of it (it's Youden's J at its best threshold).
  • Not to be confused with Gini impurity in decision trees.

Follow-up: neither says anything about calibration, which a lender needs for expected loss and pricing. (04)

22Log loss or Brier? What is a proper scoring rule, and why not use absolute error on probabilities?

Both log loss and Brier are strictly proper: their expected value is lowest only when you report the true probability. Absolute error isn't: it rewards rounding to 0 or 1.

  • True chance 0.3: expected Brier is 0.210 at the truth and 0.220 at 0.2 or 0.4; expected log loss 0.611 at the truth; expected absolute error is lowest (0.300) at 0, versus 0.420 at the truth.
  • Log loss: unbounded; one confident miss dominates (case B: 1.90 of the eight rows' 4.32 total). Sensitive among tiny probabilities, good for rare events and model selection.
  • Brier: bounded MSE on probabilities, more forgiving of label noise; decomposes into reliability − resolution + uncertainty.

Follow-up: compare each with its base-rate baseline (entropy; \(\pi(1 - \pi)\)). (06)

23Your fraud model has a Brier score of 0.0045 at 0.5% fraud. Good?

Barely better than doing nothing: predicting 0.005 for everyone scores \(0.005 \times 0.995 = 0.004975\).

  • Brier skill score: \(1 - 0.0045/0.004975 = 0.095\): it removes under 10% of the do-nothing error.
  • Log loss has the same issue: the base-rate predictor scores 0.031 nats at 0.5%.
  • Also look at: AP against the 0.005 baseline, recall at capacity, and the reliability diagram in the high-score bins where decisions happen.

Follow-up: the Brier decomposition says whether the problem is calibration (reliability) or weak separation (resolution). (06)

24What is calibration? How do you check it, and what's wrong with ECE?

Calibrated means that among cases scored \(q\), a share \(q\) are positive. Check with a reliability diagram and a proper score; ECE alone misleads.

  • Reliability diagram: mean prediction vs observed rate per bin. Flatter than the diagonal = overconfident; steeper = underconfident. Also compare the mean prediction with the base rate (calibration-in-the-large).
  • ECE = case-weighted mean gap per bin. Pitfalls: noise inflates it, more with more bins (a perfectly calibrated model on 1,000 cases: 0.022 with 10 bins, 0.046 with 50); the base-rate predictor scores 0 with AUC 0.5; equal-width bins can be nearly empty; top-label ECE ignores the other classes.
  • So: show the diagram with bin counts, and report log loss or Brier alongside.

Follow-up: calibration matters only when the number is used: costs, pricing, expected loss, combining models, confidence-based routing. (07)

25Platt, isotonic or temperature scaling? Does calibration change AUC?

Platt (two parameters) or temperature (one) when the distortion is a smooth scaling and data is limited; isotonic for any monotone distortion when data is plentiful. Platt and temperature (binary) never change AUC; multi-class temperature keeps the argmax but can shift one-vs-rest AUC slightly; isotonic can, slightly, through ties.

  • Platt: \(\sigma(a s + b)\) fitted on held-out scores. On a model whose log-odds were doubled it found \(a = 0.495\): log loss 0.464 → 0.372, ECE 0.089 → 0.006.
  • Temperature: logits / \(T\), one \(T\) for all classes (found 2.00); the standard fix for neural networks.
  • Isotonic: pool adjacent violators; flexible but overfits small sets (200 cases: log loss 0.425 vs Platt 0.379).
  • Always on held-out data (or cross-fitted), never on training predictions or the test set.

(07)

26Which models are typically miscalibrated, and in which direction?

Models that don't minimize log loss, or that can drive it down by inflating confidence, come out distorted.

  • Overconfident: naive Bayes (double-counts correlated evidence), deep decision trees (pure leaves), modern deep networks (growing logits after the labels are fitted).
  • Squeezed to the middle: random forests (averaged votes rarely unanimous); SVMs and boosted stumps (sigmoid-shaped distortion; scores aren't probabilities).
  • Usually close: logistic regression (its loss is log loss), gradient boosting with log loss (can drift with many rounds).
  • Distorted on purpose: class weights, resampling, focal loss.

Follow-up: any model loses calibration under drift before it loses its ranking, so recalibrate on recent data. (07)

27Drill: you undersampled negatives so training had 20% positives; production has 2%. The model says 0.6. What's the real probability?

About 0.11.

  • Rescale the odds by the ratio of real to training base-rate odds: model odds \(0.6/0.4 = 1.5\).
  • Real base-rate odds \(0.02/0.98 = 0.0204\); training odds \(0.2/0.8 = 0.25\); ratio 0.0816.
  • True odds \(1.5 \times 0.0816 = 0.122\), so \(p = 0.122/1.122 = 0.109\).
  • Same in log-odds: \(\operatorname{logit} p = \operatorname{logit} 0.6 + \log 0.0816\).

Follow-up: it changes no ranking (AUC unchanged) but every cost threshold. It assumes only the class mix changed; otherwise recalibrate on real-prevalence data. (07)

28Drill: a query's results have grades 3, 0, 2 at ranks 1–3, and its relevant documents have grades 3, 2, 1. Compute nDCG@3. Which ranking metric would you use to evaluate retrieval for RAG?

nDCG@3 = 8.5/9.393 = 0.905. For RAG retrieval: recall@k (or hit rate@k) at the k passed to the model, plus MRR if one passage answers the question.

  • DCG@3 with gain \(2^{\text{rel}} - 1\) and discount \(\log_2(i + 1)\): \(7/1 + 0/1.585 + 3/2 = 8.5\).
  • IDCG@3, ideal order 3, 2, 1: \(7/1 + 3/1.585 + 1/2 = 7 + 1.893 + 0.5 = 9.393\).
  • nDCG@3 \(= 0.905\). Swap the first two results (0, 3, 2) and it drops to 0.630: the top position carries the most weight.
  • Others: P@k (share of shown slots useful), MRR (1 / rank of the first hit), MAP (binary labels, all relevant items high).

Follow-up: IDCG must include relevant documents the system missed; per-query averaging; unjudged documents count as irrelevant. (08)

29MAE or RMSE? What does each make the model predict?

RMSE is minimized by the mean and punishes big misses heavily; MAE is minimized by the median and is robust. Choose by what the decision needs.

  • Constant forecast for demand 10, 20, 30, 40, 400: MSE picks 100 (the mean), MAE 30 (the median), MAPE 20, pinball at 0.9 picks 400.
  • RMSE when big errors are disproportionately bad or totals matter; MAE for a typical-case, explainable error.
  • Ratio check: MAE ≤ RMSE ≤ \(\sqrt n\)·MAE; a large RMSE/MAE ratio means a few large errors dominate.
  • Train with the matching loss; training on MSE and judging on MAE optimizes the mean and grades the median.

(09)

30What's wrong with MAPE? What would you use instead? And can R² be negative?

MAPE is undefined at zero, dominated by small actuals, and weighted towards small actuals in a way that biases forecasts low; WAPE, MASE or pinball loss are usually better. Yes, test R² can be negative.

  • Zeros: an actual of 0 makes it undefined; near-zero actuals explode it.
  • Small items dominate: a 10-unit miss on a 10-unit item is 100%, on an 80-unit item 12.5%.
  • Biased low: each outcome's miss is divided by that outcome, so low outcomes weigh more and the MAPE-best forecast is a \(1/y\)-weighted median. On symmetric demand 20–100 (mean 60), the MAPE-best constant is 40.
  • sMAPE is bounded but 200% whenever one side is 0, and penalizes under-forecasts more. WAPE = Σ|error| / Σ actual handles zeros; MASE scales by the naive forecast's error.
  • R² \(= 1 - \text{SSE}/\text{SST}\); on test data it's negative when the model does worse than predicting the test mean, and it depends on the test set's spread.

31Model B beats model A by 0.6 points on 10-fold CV. Is the difference real?

Not established yet: fold scores are correlated, so test the paired differences with a correction, and confirm on held-out data with a paired test.

  • Per-fold differences: does B win on 10 of 10 folds, or 6?
  • Corrected resampled t-test (Nadeau–Bengio): variance factor \(1/J + n_{\text{test}}/n_{\text{train}}\) = 0.211 instead of 0.1 for 10-fold. Mean 0.6, sd 0.8: naive \(p = 0.042\), corrected \(p = 0.137\). In simulation, a naive test on 10×10 CV called about half of equal models different (57% in this design).
  • On a test set: paired bootstrap of the metric difference (resample the same rows for both), or McNemar on the rows where they disagree: \(b = 40\), \(c = 60\) gives \(\chi^2 = 3.61\), \(p = 0.057\).
  • Why pairing: both models share the test set's luck; pairing cut an AUC gap's standard error from 0.018 to 0.0075.

Follow-up: even if real, is 0.6 points worth the extra complexity in business terms? (10)

32Training accuracy is 0.99 and validation 0.80. What do you do? How do learning curves help?

A large gap means high variance (overfitting), unless leakage or a shift between the sets causes it; a learning curve tells you whether more data would close it.

  • Rule out data causes first: a feature leaking only in training rows, or a validation period unlike training, opens a gap regularization won't close.
  • Learning curve (scores vs training size): a gap that's still closing means more data helps; curves meeting low means high bias, so more data won't help (logistic regression on a nonlinear problem flattened at 0.664 while the best possible was about 0.76).
  • Variance fixes: regularize (depth limits, pruning, weight decay, early stopping), fewer features, bagging, more data.
  • Validation curve over one complexity setting: tree depth peaked at 6 (0.717) and fell to 0.675 at depth 15; take the simplest setting within one standard error of the peak.

15Cheat sheet

Validation

Folds copy deployment: stratified (rare classes), grouped (new entities), forward in time with a gap as long as the label horizon (temporal). Tuned-on scores are optimistic (noise labels: best of 60 settings 0.578, nested 0.505, truth 0.50); use nested CV or an untouched test set. Fold sd / \(\sqrt k\) understates CV noise; repeat CV, compare paired.

Threshold metrics

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{precision} &= \frac{TP}{TP + FP}, \quad \text{recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}, \quad \text{FPR} = \frac{FP}{FP + TN} \\ \text{precision} &= \frac{\text{TPR}\,\pi}{\text{TPR}\,\pi + \text{FPR}\,(1 - \pi)} \\ F_\beta &= \frac{(1 + \beta^2)PR}{\beta^2 P + R}, \quad F_1 = \frac{2TP}{2TP + FP + FN} \\ \text{MCC} &= \frac{TP\cdot TN - FP\cdot FN}{\sqrt{(TP + FP)(TP + FN)(TN + FP)(TN + FN)}} \end{aligned} \]

Macro F1 = mean over classes; micro F1 = accuracy (single-label). Drill: TP 80, FP 120, FN 20, TN 9,780 → P 0.40, R 0.80, F1 0.533.

Threshold choice

\[ \begin{aligned} t^* &= \frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP} + C_{FN}} \\ t^*_{\text{general}} &= \frac{C_{FP} - C_{TN}}{(C_{FP} - C_{TN}) + (C_{FN} - C_{TP})} \\ \text{ROC slope at the cost optimum} &= \frac{1 - \pi}{\pi}\cdot\frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FN}} \end{aligned} \]

Youden = slope 1 = cost-optimal only if a miss costs \((1-\pi)/\pi\) false alarms. F1-optimal threshold = best F1 / 2 (calibrated). Capacity → precision@k.

ROC and AUC

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{AUC} &= P(s_+ \gt s_-) + \tfrac12 P(s_+ = s_-) = \frac{U}{n_+ n_-} \\ \text{AUC} &= \frac{W_+ - n_+(n_+ + 1)/2}{n_+ n_-} \\ \text{Gini} &= 2\,\text{AUC} - 1, \quad \text{KS} = \max_t(\text{TPR} - \text{FPR}) \\ \text{FPR}_{\max} &= \frac{\text{capacity}}{n_-} \end{aligned} \]

Eight rows: 13/16 = 0.8125, Gini 0.625, KS 0.75. Ignores calibration and (pure label shift) prevalence. Capacity 1% → only FPR 0–1% matters, under 1% of the area; same AUC 0.95, 231 vs 362 frauds caught. Partial AUC / 0.01 = mean recall in the slice.

PR and AP

\[ \text{AP} = \sum_k (R_k - R_{k-1})\,P_k = \frac{1}{n_+}\sum_{\text{positives}} \text{precision at its rank} \]

Baseline = prevalence. Eight rows: 0.804 (trapezoid 0.767). Moves with \(\pi\): 0.951, 0.662, 0.302 at 50%, 5%, 0.5%. 300 false alarms on top: AUC −0.003, AP 0.30 → 0.12.

Probabilities

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{LogLoss} &= -\frac1n\sum_i \big[y_i\log\hat p_i + (1 - y_i)\log(1 - \hat p_i)\big] \\ \text{Brier} &= \frac1n\sum_i(\hat p_i - y_i)^2, \quad \text{BSS} = 1 - \frac{\text{Brier}}{\pi(1 - \pi)} \\ \text{Brier} &= \text{REL} - \text{RES} + \text{UNC} \\ \text{ECE} &= \sum_b \frac{n_b}{n}\lvert \bar o_b - \bar p_b\rvert \end{aligned} \]

Proper: lowest only at the truth (absolute error isn't). 20-row drill: REL 0.025, RES 0.0225, UNC 0.2475, Brier 0.25; recalibrated 0.225. ECE: biased up by noise and bins, 0 for the base-rate predictor.

Calibration fixes

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Platt: } \hat p &= \sigma(a s + b) \\ \text{Temperature: } \hat p &= \operatorname{softmax}(z/T) \\ \text{odds}_{\text{true}} &= \text{odds}_{\text{model}}\cdot\frac{\pi/(1 - \pi)}{\pi_s/(1 - \pi_s)} \end{aligned} \]

Fit on held-out data. Isotonic = PAV, needs more data, ties can nudge AUC. Balanced-trained 0.9 → 0.083 at 1% prevalence. Overconfident: naive Bayes, deep trees, deep nets. Squeezed: forests, SVMs, boosted stumps.

Ranking

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{P@}k &= \frac{\text{relevant in top } k}{k}, \quad \text{R@}k = \frac{\text{relevant in top } k}{\text{all relevant}} \\ \text{MRR} &= \text{mean of } 1/\text{rank of first relevant} \\ \text{DCG@}k &= \sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{2^{\text{rel}_i} - 1}{\log_2(i + 1)}, \quad \text{nDCG@}k = \frac{\text{DCG@}k}{\text{IDCG@}k} \end{aligned} \]

Average per query. IDCG over every known relevant item. RAG retrieval: recall@k at the prompt's k; MRR for one answer; nDCG for graded search.

Regression

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{MAE} &= \tfrac1n\textstyle\sum \lvert e_i\rvert, \quad \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\tfrac1n\textstyle\sum e_i^2} \\ \text{MAPE} &= \tfrac{100\%}{n}\textstyle\sum \lvert e_i/y_i\rvert, \quad \text{WAPE} = \textstyle\sum\lvert e_i\rvert / \sum\lvert y_i\rvert \\ R^2 &= 1 - \text{SSE}/\text{SST}, \quad R^2_{\text{adj}} = 1 - (1 - R^2)\tfrac{n - 1}{n - p - 1} \\ \rho_\tau(e) &= \max\big(\tau e, (\tau - 1)e\big) \end{aligned} \]

RMSE → mean, MAE → median, pinball → quantile, MAPE → biased low (symmetric 20–100: best constant 40, mean 60). MAPE breaks at 0; sMAPE 200% at a zero; WAPE and MASE for demand.

Comparing models

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{SE}(\hat a) &= \sqrt{a(1 - a)/n} \\ \text{McNemar: } \chi^2 &= \frac{(\lvert b - c\rvert - 1)^2}{b + c} \\ \operatorname{Var}(\bar d) &\approx S^2\Big(\frac1J + \frac{n_{\text{test}}}{n_{\text{train}}}\Big) \end{aligned} \]

Accuracy 0.9 on 1,000 rows: ±1.9 points. Wilson for small counts (20/20 → [0.84, 1]). Paired bootstrap on the same rows (SE of an AUC gap 0.018 → 0.0075). McNemar 40 vs 60 → p 0.057. Naive t on 10×10 CV: about half false positives (57% here). Learning curve: low and meeting = bias; gap closing = variance, more data helps. Validation curve: simplest within one SE of the best.
