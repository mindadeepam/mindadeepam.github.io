Likelihood, MLE and MAP

The bridge from probability to machine learning: one formula read as a probability or as a likelihood, maximum likelihood derived for a coin, a Gaussian and the classic models, how precise an estimate is (Fisher information), how maximum likelihood fails, Bayes' rule for parameters, MAP and why priors are regularizers, conjugate updates, what a full posterior adds, and where each piece hides inside everyday ML.

About 50 minutes, plus 30 questions · one running example: 7 heads in 10 tosses · every number checked by code

In short

  1. Probability and likelihood are the same formula, \(p(\text{data} \mid \theta)\), read two ways. Fix \(\theta\) and vary the data: probabilities, which sum to 1 over the possible outcomes. Fix the data you saw and vary \(\theta\): the likelihood, a score for each \(\theta\) that only means something in ratios and doesn't integrate to 1 (for 7 heads in 10 it integrates to 1/11).
  2. Maximum likelihood (MLE) picks the \(\theta\) that makes the observed data most probable. Maximize the log: same answer, sums instead of products, no underflow. A coin gives \(k/n\); a Gaussian gives the sample mean and the variance divided by \(n\), which on average comes out at only \((n-1)/n\) of the true variance, because the mean was fitted to the same points. Least squares and log loss are MLE under Gaussian and Bernoulli noise.
  3. Near its peak the log-likelihood is a parabola, and its curvature is the Fisher information \(I\). The standard error is \(1/\sqrt{I}\): \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\) for a coin. MLE is invariant to reparameterization at any \(n\); it is also consistent, asymptotically normal and efficient, but those three are large-sample promises: 3 heads in 3 tosses gives \(\hat p = 1\), and separable classes send logistic weights to infinity.
  4. Bayes' rule for parameters: posterior ∝ likelihood × prior. The evidence \(p(\text{data})\) doesn't depend on \(\theta\), so MAP, the posterior's peak, ignores it. With a \(\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)\) prior a coin's MAP is \((k + \alpha - 1)/(n + \alpha + \beta - 2)\): the prior acts as extra imaginary tosses, and its pull fades as \(n\) grows.
  5. Regularization is a prior. A Gaussian prior \(\mathcal N(0, \tau^2)\) on the weights gives L2 with \(\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2\); a Laplace prior gives L1. A tighter prior is a bigger \(\lambda\). Ridge, lasso, weight decay, Laplace smoothing and smoothed target encoding are all MAP estimates or posterior means.
  6. Conjugate priors make updating arithmetic, \(\text{Beta}(2, 2)\) plus 7 heads and 3 tails is \(\text{Beta}(9, 5)\), and a full posterior gives more than its peak: a mean, credible intervals, and predictions that average over uncertainty. After 3 heads in 3, the next toss is a head with probability 0.8, not 1. Unlike MLE, MAP moves when you reparameterize.

01Probability and likelihood

Everything in this note grows from one formula. Toss a coin \(n = 10\) times. Its chance of heads, \(\theta\), is unknown. The number of heads \(k\) follows the binomial distribution:

\[ p(k \mid \theta) = \binom{n}{k}\,\theta^{k}\,(1 - \theta)^{n - k} \]

In words: any particular sequence with \(k\) heads has probability \(\theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k}\), and \(\binom{n}{k}\) counts how many such sequences there are.

The running example. You toss the coin and see 7 heads in 10. The same formula answers two different questions: “if the coin were fair, how likely is this outcome?” (a probability) and “having seen 7 heads, which values of \(\theta\) explain it well?” (a likelihood).

Why the likelihood is not a probability of \(\theta\)

  • It doesn't integrate to 1. The area under the curve is \(\int_0^1 \binom{10}{7}\theta^7(1-\theta)^3\,d\theta = 1/11\). (For a binomial count it's always \(1/(n+1)\).) A probability distribution over \(\theta\) would have area 1.
  • Each value is a probability of the same event under a different model. 0.267 is \(P(7 \text{ heads} \mid \theta = 0.7)\), not \(P(\theta = 0.7 \mid 7 \text{ heads})\). Turning it into a statement about \(\theta\) needs a prior and Bayes' rule (section 04).
  • With continuous data it can exceed 1. The likelihood is then a product of densities, and a density can be bigger than 1: a Gaussian with \(\sigma = 0.1\) has density 3.99 at its centre.
  • Only ratios carry meaning. \(L(0.7)/L(0.5) = 2.28\): the observed data are 2.28 times as probable under \(\theta = 0.7\) as under a fair coin. Factors that don't involve \(\theta\), like \(\binom{10}{7}\), cancel in every ratio and every maximization, so they're routinely dropped. Recording the exact sequence of tosses instead of the count changes the likelihood only by that constant, so it leads to the same conclusions about \(\theta\).

Trap

“\(\theta = 0.7\) has likelihood 0.267, so there's a 26.7% chance the coin's bias is 0.7.” No: if the bias were 0.7, the data would have probability 0.267. A probability for \(\theta\) is a posterior, and needs a prior.

Say it in an interview: “Probability and likelihood use the same function \(p(x \mid \theta)\). Probability fixes \(\theta\) and asks how likely different data are, so it sums to 1 over the data. Likelihood fixes the data I observed and asks how well each \(\theta\) explains them: it's a function of \(\theta\), it doesn't integrate to 1, and only ratios matter. For 7 heads in 10, \(\theta = 0.7\) has likelihood 0.267 and \(\theta = 0.5\) has 0.117, so the data favour 0.7 by a factor of 2.3.”

02Maximum likelihood estimation

Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) turns the likelihood into an estimator: pick the parameter value under which the observed data are most probable. With \(n\) observations that are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.), the probability of all of them is the product of each one's probability:

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} &= \arg\max_\theta \prod_{i=1}^{n} p(x_i \mid \theta) \\ &= \arg\max_\theta \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log p(x_i \mid \theta) \\ &= \arg\min_\theta \sum_{i=1}^{n} -\log p(x_i \mid \theta) \end{aligned} \]

In words: multiply the probabilities of the data points, take the log so the product becomes a sum, and find the \(\theta\) with the largest sum. The last line, minimizing the negative log-likelihood (NLL), is how the same thing is written as a loss to minimize. Write \(\ell(\theta) = \log L(\theta)\) for the log-likelihood.

Why take logs

  1. Same answer. The log is strictly increasing, so whatever \(\theta\) maximizes \(L\) also maximizes \(\log L\).
  2. Products become sums. The derivative of a sum is a sum of per-example derivatives, which is what lets SGD estimate it from a mini-batch.
  3. No underflow. The probability of one exact sequence of tosses shrinks exponentially with \(n\). At 70% heads:

  4. Common densities become simple. The Gaussian and Bernoulli are exponentials of simple expressions, so their logs are a square or a linear term, which is easy to differentiate.

The coin: deriving \(\hat\theta = k/n\)

Drop the constant \(\binom{n}{k}\), take the log, set the derivative to zero:

\[ \begin{aligned} \ell(\theta) &= k\log\theta + (n - k)\log(1 - \theta) \\ \frac{d\ell}{d\theta} &= \frac{k}{\theta} - \frac{n - k}{1 - \theta} = 0 \\ k(1 - \theta) &= (n - k)\,\theta \\ \hat\theta &= \frac{k}{n} \end{aligned} \]

In words: each head pushes the slope up by \(1/\theta\), each tail pushes it down by \(1/(1-\theta)\), and the top is where the pushes balance. That happens exactly at the observed share of heads.

  • The slope has a name, the score. At \(\theta = 0.5\) it is \(7/0.5 - 3/0.5 = 8\), positive, so raise \(\theta\). At 0.8 it is \(8.75 - 15 = -6.25\), so lower it. At 0.7 it is \(10 - 10 = 0\).
  • It's a maximum, and the only one. \(\ell''(\theta) = -k/\theta^2 - (n - k)/(1 - \theta)^2\) is negative everywhere, so \(\ell\) is concave: one peak, no other flat points.

The answer everyone guesses, 0.7, now has a reason: it's the \(\theta\) that makes what you saw most probable.

A Gaussian's mean and variance

Five measurements, \(x = 3, 5, 6, 8, 8\), assumed drawn independently from \(\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)\). The log of the product of Gaussian densities is:

\[ \ell(\mu, \sigma^2) = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \mu)^2 \]

In words: a penalty for a wide bell (the first term), plus a penalty for points far from the centre, measured in units of the variance.

Set each partial derivative to zero. The mean's solution doesn't depend on \(\sigma\), so find it first and plug it into the variance's equation:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mu} &= \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_i (x_i - \mu) = 0 \\ \hat\mu &= \bar x \\ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \sigma^2} &= -\frac{n}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{1}{2\sigma^4}\sum_i (x_i - \hat\mu)^2 = 0 \\ \hat\sigma^2 &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2 \end{aligned} \]

In words: the MLE of the mean is the sample mean, and the MLE of the variance is the average squared distance from it, dividing by \(n\).

With the numbers: \(\bar x = 30/5 = 6\). The deviations are −3, −1, 0, 2, 2, their squares 9, 1, 0, 4, 4, summing to 18. So \(\hat\sigma^2 = 18/5 = 3.6\). The familiar sample variance divides by \(n - 1\) instead: \(s^2 = 18/4 = 4.5\). Which one is “right” is the next question.

Why the variance MLE is biased, and why \(n - 1\) fixes it

The question: averaged over many samples, does \(\hat\sigma^2\) land on the true \(\sigma^2\)? Simulate it: draw 5 points from \(\mathcal N(6, 4)\) a million times and compute both estimates each time. The MLE averages 3.20; dividing by \(n - 1\) averages 4.00. The MLE is low by the factor \((n-1)/n = 0.8\).

Why: \(\hat\sigma^2\) measures the spread around \(\bar x\), not around the true \(\mu\), and \(\bar x\) was computed from these very points, so it sits closer to them than \(\mu\) does. In fact \(\bar x\) is the point that makes the sum of squared distances as small as possible. With the five points, if the true mean were 5:

\[ \begin{aligned} \textstyle\sum (x_i - 5)^2 &= 4 + 0 + 1 + 9 + 9 = 23 \\ \textstyle\sum (x_i - 6)^2 &= 9 + 1 + 0 + 4 + 4 = 18 \\ 23 - 18 &= 5 = n\,(\bar x - \mu)^2 = 5 \times 1^2 \end{aligned} \]

In words: measuring from the fitted centre always gives a smaller sum of squares than measuring from the true centre, and the shortfall is exactly \(n(\bar x - \mu)^2\). That identity holds for any data and any \(\mu\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2 &= \sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2 - n(\bar x - \mu)^2 \\ \mathbb E\Big[\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2\Big] &= n\sigma^2 - n \cdot \frac{\sigma^2}{n} = (n - 1)\,\sigma^2 \end{aligned} \]

In words: on average each \((x_i - \mu)^2\) is \(\sigma^2\), so the first sum averages \(n\sigma^2\); \(\bar x - \mu\) has variance \(\sigma^2/n\) (the variance of a mean), so the correction averages one \(\sigma^2\). Fitting the mean costs exactly one \(\sigma^2\) of the sum of squares, so divide by \(n - 1\) to get \(\sigma^2\) back on average. The simulation agrees: sums of squares averaged 20.0 around \(\mu\) and 16.0 around \(\bar x\). This is Bessel's correction; the usual phrase is “one degree of freedom is used up by the mean”: the deviations from \(\bar x\) must sum to 0, so only \(n - 1\) of them are free. (The same intuition, checked by listing every sample of a tiny population, is in hypothesis testing.)

Two related facts: \(s = \sqrt{s^2}\) is biased low even with \(n - 1\) (it averages 1.88 for a true \(\sigma\) of 2 at \(n = 5\)), because a square root is concave; and in regression the same story gives \(\text{RSS}/n\) for the MLE of the noise variance and \(\text{RSS}/(n - p)\) for the unbiased one, with \(p\) fitted coefficients (linear-logistic-regression).

Trap

“The MLE is unbiased.” Often it isn't, and that's fine: its guarantees (section 03) are about large samples. Bias is a small-sample property, and a little of it can buy a lower error.

Three more drills: Poisson, exponential, uniform

The same recipe (take the log, differentiate, set to zero) covers the other distributions interviewers like to ask about.

Poisson counts, such as calls per minute, with \(p(x \mid \lambda) = \lambda^x e^{-\lambda}/x!\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \ell(\lambda) &= \Big(\sum_i x_i\Big)\log\lambda - n\lambda - \sum_i \log x_i! \\ \frac{d\ell}{d\lambda} &= \frac{\sum_i x_i}{\lambda} - n = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \hat\lambda = \bar x \end{aligned} \]

In words: the MLE of a Poisson rate is the average count. Its Fisher information (next section) is \(n/\lambda\), so \(\text{SE} = \sqrt{\hat\lambda/n}\). For counts 3, 5, 4: \(\hat\lambda = 4\) and \(\text{SE} = \sqrt{4/3} = 1.15\).

  • Exponential waiting times, \(p(x \mid \lambda) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}\): \(\ell = n\log\lambda - \lambda\sum x_i\), so \(\hat\lambda = n/\sum x_i = 1/\bar x\). Waits of 2, 1 and 3 minutes give a rate of 0.5 per minute, and by invariance (section 03) a mean wait of 2. The rate's MLE is biased high at small \(n\): with 3 waits and a true rate of 0.5 it averages 0.75.
  • Uniform on \([0, \theta]\): \(L(\theta) = \theta^{-n}\) if \(\theta\) is at least every observation, and 0 otherwise. That only falls as \(\theta\) grows, so the maximum sits at the edge of the allowed region, \(\hat\theta = \max_i x_i\): 3 for the data 2, 1, 3. No derivative is zero there, so “set the derivative to zero” finds nothing. The estimate is always below the truth (on average \(n\theta/(n+1)\), so 3 when \(\theta = 4\) and \(n = 3\)).

Linear and logistic regression are MLE

Both maximize a conditional likelihood, \(p(y \mid x, w)\): the inputs are treated as fixed, and only the targets are modelled.

Linear regression. Assume each target is a linear function plus Gaussian noise, \(y_i = w^\top x_i + \varepsilon_i\) with \(\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)\). The NLL is the Gaussian's, with \(w^\top x_i\) as the mean:

\[ \begin{aligned} -\ell(w) &= \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i \big(y_i - w^\top x_i\big)^2 + \frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) \\ \hat w_{\text{MLE}} &= \arg\min_w \sum_i \big(y_i - w^\top x_i\big)^2 \end{aligned} \]

In words: with \(\sigma\) fixed, only the sum of squared residuals depends on \(w\), so maximizing the Gaussian likelihood is exactly least squares. Swap the noise model and the loss changes with it: Laplace noise gives absolute error, Bernoulli gives log loss, categorical gives cross-entropy. That family, and what each loss makes the model predict, is derived in loss functions; how OLS is solved is in linear and logistic regression.

Logistic regression. Each label is a coin toss with its own heads probability, \(y_i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p_i)\) with \(p_i = \sigma(w^\top x_i)\). The coin's log-likelihood, summed over rows, is minus the log loss:

\[ \begin{aligned} -\ell(w) &= -\sum_i \big[y_i \log p_i + (1 - y_i)\log(1 - p_i)\big] \\ \nabla_w(-\ell) &= \sum_i (p_i - y_i)\,x_i = X^\top(p - y) \end{aligned} \]

In words: minimizing log loss is maximizing the Bernoulli likelihood. Setting the gradient to zero has no closed-form solution, because \(p\) is a non-linear function of \(w\), but the NLL is convex, so any minimum that gradient descent or Newton's method (called IRLS here) finds is the global one. It's unique when the columns of \(X\) are linearly independent, and finite only if the classes aren't perfectly separable (section 03). Details are in linear and logistic regression. Training a neural network with cross-entropy is the same thing with a learned \(f(x)\) in place of \(w^\top x\) (loss functions); averaged over the data, it minimizes the forward KL divergence \(\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}} \Vert p_{\text{model}})\).

Say it in an interview: “MLE picks the parameters that make the observed data most probable. I maximize the log-likelihood: same argmax, a sum over i.i.d. examples instead of a product, and no underflow. For a coin the score equation gives \(k/n\). For a Gaussian it gives the sample mean and the variance with divisor \(n\), whose average is only \((n-1)/n\) of the true variance because the mean was fitted to the same data; \(n - 1\) corrects it, though the MLE has lower MSE. Least squares and log loss are just Gaussian and Bernoulli MLE.”

03How good is an MLE?

Four properties

The first three hold as \(n\) grows, under regularity conditions listed below the table. Invariance holds exactly, at any \(n\).

Fisher information and the standard error of an MLE

The question: how sure can you be that \(\theta\) is near 0.7? Look at the shape of the log-likelihood around its peak. A sharp peak means only a narrow range of \(\theta\) explains the data well; a flat one means many values fit almost as well. The sharpness is the curvature, the second derivative.

\[ \begin{aligned} I(\theta) &= -\,\mathbb E\Big[\frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial \theta^2}\Big] = \mathbb E\Big[\Big(\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \theta}\Big)^2\Big] \\ \operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta) &\approx \frac{1}{I(\theta)} \\ \text{SE}(\hat\theta) &\approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{I(\hat\theta)}} \end{aligned} \]

In words: the Fisher information is the expected curvature of the log-likelihood, how sharply it bends at \(\theta\), averaged over datasets the model could generate. Equivalently it's the variance of the score (the slope), because the score averages zero at the true \(\theta\). More information means a sharper peak and a smaller standard error. Here \(\ell\) is the log-likelihood of the whole sample, so \(I\) is the information in all \(n\) points; the information in one point is written \(I_1\) below. The observed information \(J(\hat\theta) = -\ell''(\hat\theta)\) is the curvature of your own dataset's curve at its peak; software uses it for standard errors.

Deriving it for a coin. One toss \(x \in \{0, 1\}\) has \(\log p(x \mid \theta) = x\log\theta + (1 - x)\log(1 - \theta)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}\log p(x \mid \theta) &= -\frac{x}{\theta^2} - \frac{1 - x}{(1 - \theta)^2} \\ I_1(\theta) &= \frac{\theta}{\theta^2} + \frac{1 - \theta}{(1 - \theta)^2} = \frac{1}{\theta(1 - \theta)} \\ I_n(\theta) &= \frac{n}{\theta(1 - \theta)} \\ \text{SE}(\hat\theta) &= \sqrt{\frac{\hat\theta(1 - \hat\theta)}{n}} \end{aligned} \]

In words: take minus the second derivative, average it using \(\mathbb E[x] = \theta\), and multiply by \(n\) because independent tosses add their information. One over the square root is the standard error. Per toss, a coin near 0 or 1 carries the most information: its outcomes vary least, so each toss pins \(\theta\) down more tightly.

  • The SE of a transformed estimate (the delta method). Invariance gives the estimate \(g(\hat\theta)\); its SE is about \(\lvert g'(\hat\theta) \rvert \times \text{SE}(\hat\theta)\). For the log-odds, \(g'(\theta) = 1/[\theta(1 - \theta)] = 4.76\) at 0.7, so the log-odds 0.85 has SE \(4.76 \times 0.145 = 0.69\).
  • Many parameters: \(I\) becomes a matrix, the expected Hessian of the NLL, and \(\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\theta) \approx I^{-1}\). The square roots of its diagonal are the coefficient standard errors a regression summary prints; for logistic regression \(I = X^\top W X\) with \(W = \operatorname{diag}\big(p_i(1 - p_i)\big)\) (linear-logistic-regression). It's the same Hessian Newton's method uses to take its steps.
  • Wald vs likelihood-ratio intervals. The Wald interval uses only the curvature at the peak, so it assumes the curve is a parabola. For 7 of 10 the curve is lopsided near 1, and the Wald upper end of 0.984 is too high. The likelihood-ratio interval uses the real curve: keep every \(\theta\) whose log-likelihood is within 1.92 of the peak. Why 1.92: when \(\theta_0\) is the true value, twice the drop, \(2[\ell(\hat\theta) - \ell(\theta_0)]\), approximately follows a \(\chi^2\) distribution with 1 degree of freedom (Wilks' theorem), whose 95% point is 3.84. That gives [0.39, 0.92] for 7 of 10 and [0.61, 0.78] for 70 of 100, where the curve is nearly a parabola and Wald agrees.
  • The likelihood-ratio test uses the same fact. Is the coin fair? Twice the drop from the peak to 0.5 is \(2\ln 2.28 = 1.65\), below 3.84, so a fair coin isn't rejected (\(p = 0.20\)); with 70 of 100 it's 16.5 (\(p = 0.00005\)). For nested models in general, \(2(\ell_{\text{big}} - \ell_{\text{small}})\) is approximately \(\chi^2\) with one degree of freedom per extra parameter; in regression output this is the drop in deviance.
  • Comparing models. Training likelihood always rises when parameters are added, so on its own it always picks the bigger model. Information criteria charge for each of the \(d\) parameters (lower is better): \(\text{AIC} = 2d - 2\hat\ell\) and \(\text{BIC} = d\ln n - 2\hat\ell\). For 7 of 10, the fair coin (\(d = 0\), \(\hat\ell = -6.93\)) scores 13.86 on both; the free coin (\(d = 1\), \(\hat\ell = -6.11\)) scores 14.22 and 14.52, so both prefer the fair coin. For 70 of 100 both prefer the free one (AIC 124.2 against 138.6). AIC estimates out-of-sample log-likelihood; BIC approximates the evidence of section 04, and its penalty grows with \(n\), so it picks smaller models.

Trap

Writing the standard error as \(p(1 - p)/\sqrt n\). The square root covers the whole variance: \(\sqrt{p(1 - p)/n}\). And Fisher information grows with \(n\) (it adds over independent data), while the standard error shrinks like \(1/\sqrt n\).

When MLE goes wrong

  • Little data, extreme outcomes. 3 heads in 3 tosses gives \(\hat\theta = 1\): the model now says a tail is impossible. The standard-error formula returns \(\sqrt{1 \times 0/3} = 0\), total certainty, and the first tail costs an infinite log loss. Zero counts do the same in Naive Bayes, where a word never seen with a class gets probability 0 and vetoes that class (classical NLP).
  • Separation. If some combination of features splits the classes perfectly, logistic regression's likelihood keeps rising as the weights grow, so no finite MLE exists. On four points \(x = -2, -1, 1, 2\) with labels 0, 0, 1, 1, gradient descent on the NLL (learning rate 0.5, intercept included) gives a weight of 3.4 after 100 steps, 5.5 after 1,000, 7.8 after 10,000 and 10.1 after 100,000, still climbing while the loss creeps towards 0 (linear-logistic-regression).
  • Too many parameters for the data. The MLE fits noise as eagerly as signal. A degree-4 polynomial goes exactly through 5 points: zero residuals, an estimated noise variance of 0, an infinite likelihood. A Gaussian mixture can park one component on a single point and shrink its variance to 0, again with infinite likelihood (unsupervised learning). A full covariance matrix in \(d\) dimensions estimated from \(n \le d\) points is singular.
  • The wrong noise model. The MLE is only as good as the assumed distribution: Gaussian noise when the real noise has heavy tails lets a few outliers drag the fit (loss functions).

The first three have one cure: add information that isn't in the data, which is what a prior does. That's MAP, section 05.

Say it in an interview: “The MLE is invariant to reparameterization at any \(n\). Under regularity conditions it's also consistent, asymptotically normal with variance one over the Fisher information, and efficient, because it reaches the Cramér–Rao bound. Fisher information is the expected curvature of the log-likelihood; for a coin it's \(n/[p(1-p)]\), so the SE is \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\). Those are large-sample promises: with 3 heads in 3 the MLE says \(p = 1\), separable data send logistic weights to infinity, and many parameters fit noise. A prior fixes all three.”

04Bayes' rule for parameters

MLE treats \(\theta\) as a fixed unknown and asks which value explains the data best. The Bayesian view treats \(\theta\) as uncertain, with a distribution of its own, and uses Bayes' rule to update that distribution with the data. (Bayes' rule for events belongs to a planned probability note; here it's applied to parameters.)

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\theta \mid D) &= \frac{p(D \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)}{p(D)} \\ p(D) &= \int p(D \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)\,d\theta \end{aligned} \]

In words: posterior = likelihood × prior / evidence. The prior \(p(\theta)\) is what you believed before the data, the likelihood is how well each \(\theta\) explains the data, and the posterior is the updated belief. The evidence or marginal likelihood \(p(D)\) is the probability of the data averaged over the prior: one number, the same for every \(\theta\).

Worked, with a flat prior \(p(\theta) = 1\) on \([0, 1]\) and 7 heads in 10:

\[ \begin{aligned} p(D) &= \int_0^1 120\,\theta^7(1 - \theta)^3\,d\theta = \frac{1}{11} \\ p(\theta \mid D) &= 11 \times 120\,\theta^7(1 - \theta)^3 = 1320\,\theta^7(1 - \theta)^3 \end{aligned} \]

In words: the evidence is the area under the likelihood curve from section 01, and dividing by it rescales the curve to area 1. The result is the \(\text{Beta}(8, 4)\) distribution, whose normalizing constant is indeed 1320. With a flat prior the posterior has the likelihood's shape, peak at 0.7 included.

Why MAP can ignore the evidence. \(p(D)\) doesn't depend on \(\theta\), so dividing by it can't move the peak. For finding the most probable \(\theta\) it's enough that posterior ∝ likelihood × prior.

When the evidence matters:

  • For the full posterior. Means, intervals and predictions need a distribution with area 1.
  • For comparing models. The evidence scores a whole model by how well it predicted the data, averaging over its prior. A fair-coin model (\(\theta = 0.5\) exactly) gives the data probability \(p(D) = 0.117\); the flexible flat-prior model gives \(1/11 = 0.091\). Their ratio, the Bayes factor, is 1.29 in favour of the fair coin, even though \(\theta = 0.7\) fits better. The flexible model spread its prior over many values of \(\theta\) that predict 7 of 10 badly, and pays for it. That built-in Occam's razor is why approximations of the evidence, such as BIC (section 03), are used to choose model size.

Trap

“The posterior is the normalized likelihood.” Only with a flat prior, and “flat” depends on how the parameter is written: flat in \(\theta\) is not flat in the log-odds (section 08).

Say it in an interview: “posterior ∝ likelihood × prior. The evidence \(p(D)\) normalizes it and doesn't depend on \(\theta\), so it drops out of any argmax. I need it only to get a proper posterior, for intervals and predictions, or to compare models, where it automatically penalizes flexible ones.”

05MAP estimation

Maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation returns the peak of the posterior:

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} &= \arg\max_\theta\ p(\theta \mid D) \\ &= \arg\max_\theta\ \big[\log p(D \mid \theta) + \log p(\theta)\big] \\ &= \arg\min_\theta\ \big[\underbrace{-\log p(D \mid \theta)}_{\text{data loss (NLL)}}\ \underbrace{-\ \log p(\theta)}_{\text{penalty}}\big] \end{aligned} \]

In words: maximize the log-likelihood plus the log-prior. As a loss, that's the usual NLL plus a penalty, minus the log-prior, that charges for parameter values the prior finds implausible. With a flat prior the penalty is a constant and MAP equals MLE.

The coin with a Beta prior

The natural prior for a probability is the Beta distribution, \(\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)\), with density proportional to \(\theta^{\alpha - 1}(1 - \theta)^{\beta - 1}\) on \([0, 1]\). Its mean is \(\alpha/(\alpha + \beta)\) and, for \(\alpha, \beta \gt 1\), its peak is at \((\alpha - 1)/(\alpha + \beta - 2)\). \(\text{Beta}(1, 1)\) is flat; \(\text{Beta}(2, 2)\) is a gentle hump at 0.5; \(\text{Beta}(5, 5)\) says “probably close to fair” more firmly.

\[ \begin{aligned} \log p(\theta \mid D) &= (k + \alpha - 1)\log\theta \\ &\quad + (n - k + \beta - 1)\log(1 - \theta) + \text{const} \\ \frac{d}{d\theta}: \quad 0 &= \frac{k + \alpha - 1}{\theta} - \frac{n - k + \beta - 1}{1 - \theta} \\ \hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} &= \frac{k + \alpha - 1}{n + \alpha + \beta - 2} \end{aligned} \]

In words: the log-prior has the same shape as a coin's log-likelihood, so it simply adds to the head and tail counts. The derivative is the coin MLE's, with \(k + \alpha - 1\) heads out of \(n + \alpha + \beta - 2\) tosses.

The prior as pseudo-counts

  • Imaginary tosses.   • A weighted average. The MAP is a blend of the MLE and the prior's peak, weighted by real against imaginary tosses: with \(\text{Beta}(5, 5)\), the data get weight \(10/18 = 0.556\), so \(0.556 \times 0.7 + 0.444 \times 0.5 = 0.611\). Pulling an estimate towards a prior value like this is called shrinkage. Smoothed target encoding is the same blend, with the overall mean counting as \(m\) extra rows (data preparation).
  • Choosing the prior's strength is choosing how many tosses of evidence your belief is worth. Past experiments can set it (estimating the prior from data is called empirical Bayes); otherwise cross-validation can.

06Priors as regularizers

The version of the last section interviewers ask for: a regularization penalty is minus a log-prior, so a regularized fit is a MAP estimate. The setup is linear regression with Gaussian noise, \(y_i = w^\top x_i + \varepsilon_i\), \(\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)\), plus a prior on each weight.

A Gaussian prior gives L2 (ridge, weight decay)

Put an independent zero-mean Gaussian prior on each weight, \(w_j \sim \mathcal N(0, \tau^2)\): “weights are probably small, and large ones are increasingly implausible”. Step by step:

  1. The log-prior. \(-\log p(w) = \sum_j w_j^2/(2\tau^2) + \text{const}\).
  2. The negative log-posterior is minus the log-likelihood plus minus the log-prior, up to constants.
  3. Multiply by \(2\sigma^2\). Scaling by a positive constant doesn't move the minimum.
\[ \begin{aligned} -\log p(w \mid D) &= \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i \big(y_i - w^\top x_i\big)^2 \\ &\quad + \frac{1}{2\tau^2}\sum_j w_j^2 + \text{const} \\ \hat w_{\text{MAP}} &= \arg\min_w\ \sum_i \big(y_i - w^\top x_i\big)^2 \\ &\qquad\qquad + \frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}\lVert w \rVert_2^2 \\ \lambda &= \frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2} \end{aligned} \]

In words: ridge regression is the MAP estimate under a zero-mean Gaussian prior, and its \(\lambda\) is the noise variance divided by the prior variance. A tight prior (small \(\tau^2\)) or noisy data (large \(\sigma^2\)) means a strong penalty; a flat prior (\(\tau^2 \to \infty\)) gives \(\lambda = 0\), plain least squares.

Worked, one weight and no intercept. \(x = 1, 2, 3, 4\), \(y = 1, 3, 2, 5\), \(\sigma^2 = 1\). Then \(\sum xy = 33\) and \(\sum x^2 = 30\). The MLE is \(33/30 = 1.1\), and setting the derivative of the penalized loss to zero gives \(\hat w = \sum xy/(\sum x^2 + \lambda)\):

  • Logistic regression has no \(\sigma\): the objective is the log loss plus \(\lVert w \rVert^2/(2\tau^2)\). scikit-learn's LogisticRegression minimizes \(\tfrac12\lVert w \rVert^2 + C \sum \text{log loss}\); dividing by \(C\) shows that \(C\) is the prior variance \(\tau^2\). A numerical MAP fit and scikit-learn give the same coefficients.
  • Weight decay in a neural network is the same prior. With plain SGD, the step \(w \leftarrow w - \eta(\nabla \text{NLL} + \lambda w) = (1 - \eta\lambda)\,w - \eta\nabla\text{NLL}\) shrinks every weight by a constant factor each step, hence the name. With Adam, adding \(\lambda w\) to the gradient is not the same as decaying the weights. The L2 version still has the MAP as its fixed point, but along the way Adam divides its pull by each weight's gradient scale, so weights with large gradients are barely regularized. AdamW applies the decay to the weights directly (deep learning basics): it regularizes more evenly in practice, but with no penalty term in its loss it isn't exactly a Gaussian prior any more; how it's used alongside dropout and early stopping is in deep learning basics.

A Laplace prior gives L1 (lasso)

Now give each weight a Laplace prior, \(p(w_j) = \frac{1}{2b}\exp(-\lvert w_j \rvert/b)\): a sharp peak at 0 with exponential tails (variance \(2b^2\)). The same three steps:

\[ \begin{aligned} -\log p(w \mid D) &= \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i \big(y_i - w^\top x_i\big)^2 \\ &\quad + \frac{1}{b}\sum_j \lvert w_j \rvert + \text{const} \\ \hat w_{\text{MAP}} &= \arg\min_w\ \sum_i \big(y_i - w^\top x_i\big)^2 \\ &\qquad\qquad + \frac{2\sigma^2}{b}\lVert w \rVert_1 \\ \lambda &= \frac{2\sigma^2}{b} \end{aligned} \]

In words: lasso is the MAP estimate under a Laplace prior, with \(\lambda\) again growing as the prior narrows.

Worked, same data. For \(w \gt 0\) the derivative of \(\sum (y - wx)^2 + \lambda w\) is \(-2(33 - 30w) + \lambda\), so \(\hat w = (33 - \lambda/2)/30\), and once \(\lambda/2 \ge 33\) the best value is exactly 0:

The data's loss \(\sum(y - wx)^2\) has slope \(-2\sum xy = -66\) there, pulling \(w\) up. The L2 penalty's slope, \(2\lambda w\), is 0 at \(w = 0\), so it can never cancel that pull: it shrinks the weight but never all the way. Trap

“A Laplace prior makes weights exactly zero.” Only its peak does. In the example with \(b = 0.025\), the posterior mode is exactly 0, but the posterior mean is 0.075 and the posterior gives 88% probability to \(w \gt 0\). Lasso's sparsity comes from taking the mode of a kinked distribution, not from believing the weight is zero. Bayesian models that really want “exactly zero or not” use spike-and-slab priors; the horseshoe is a continuous cousin that shrinks small weights hard without making them exactly zero.

What the correspondence tells you in practice

  • Scale the features first. \(\mathcal N(0, \tau^2 I)\) puts the same belief on every weight, which only makes sense if the features are on comparable scales; otherwise the penalty hits each feature according to its units.
  • Don't penalize the intercept. It gets a flat prior. Penalizing it would pull every prediction towards 0 instead of towards the average target. (scikit-learn's default solver leaves it unpenalized; its liblinear solver penalizes it.)
  • The same prior has different \(\lambda\)'s in different libraries. With a summed loss the prior's weight is fixed and the data swamp it as \(n\) grows (MAP → MLE). Libraries that average the loss over rows rescale \(\lambda\): scikit-learn's Lasso minimizes \(\text{RSS}/(2n) + \alpha\lVert w \rVert_1\), so \(\alpha = \sigma^2/(nb)\), while Ridge minimizes \(\text{RSS} + \alpha\lVert w \rVert_2^2\), so \(\alpha = \sigma^2/\tau^2\). Both were checked numerically against a direct MAP fit.
  • Choosing \(\lambda\) is choosing a prior. Cross-validation picks it empirically; empirical Bayes picks the \(\tau^2\) that maximizes the evidence.
  • A prior needn't be centred on 0. \(\mathcal N(w_0, \tau^2)\) penalizes \(\lVert w - w_0 \rVert^2\): shrink towards a previous model's weights, for example to keep a fine-tuned model close to its pretrained starting point.

Say it in an interview: “A Gaussian prior \(\mathcal N(0, \tau^2)\) on the weights adds \(\lVert w \rVert^2/(2\tau^2)\) to the negative log-likelihood; multiplying through by \(2\sigma^2\) gives ridge with \(\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2\). A Laplace prior adds \(\lVert w \rVert_1/b\), which is lasso with \(\lambda = 2\sigma^2/b\). A tighter prior means a bigger \(\lambda\). L1 gives exact zeros because the MAP is the mode of a prior with a kink at 0; the posterior mean under the same prior isn't sparse.”

07Conjugate priors

A prior is conjugate to a likelihood when the posterior lands in the same family as the prior. Then updating never needs an integral: you just change the prior's parameters, usually by adding counts or sums from the data.

Beta–Binomial: add heads to \(\alpha\), tails to \(\beta\)

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\theta \mid D) &\propto \underbrace{\theta^{\alpha - 1}(1 - \theta)^{\beta - 1}}_{\text{prior}} \cdot \underbrace{\theta^{k}(1 - \theta)^{n - k}}_{\text{likelihood}} \\ &= \theta^{\alpha + k - 1}(1 - \theta)^{\beta + n - k - 1} \\ \theta \mid D &\sim \text{Beta}(\alpha + k,\ \beta + n - k) \end{aligned} \]

In words: the prior and the likelihood are both powers of \(\theta\) and \(1 - \theta\), so multiplying them adds the exponents. The result is again a Beta, with the heads added to \(\alpha\) and the tails to \(\beta\).

Gaussian–Gaussian, and the other common pairs

For the mean \(\mu\) of Gaussian data with a known noise variance \(\sigma^2\), a Gaussian prior \(\mu \sim \mathcal N(\mu_0, \tau_0^2)\) gives a Gaussian posterior. With \(n\) points of mean \(\bar x\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{1}{\tau_n^2} &= \frac{1}{\tau_0^2} + \frac{n}{\sigma^2} \\ \mu_n &= \tau_n^2\Big(\frac{\mu_0}{\tau_0^2} + \frac{n\,\bar x}{\sigma^2}\Big) \end{aligned} \]

In words: precisions add (precision is one over variance: the prior's plus the data's), and the posterior mean is a precision-weighted average of the prior mean and the sample mean. The prior is worth \(n_0 = \sigma^2/\tau_0^2\) observations sitting at \(\mu_0\). And \(n/\sigma^2\) is exactly the Fisher information about \(\mu\) in the data, so “precisions add” says: posterior information = prior information + Fisher information.

Worked, with the five measurements from section 02 (\(\bar x = 6\), \(n = 5\)), a known \(\sigma^2 = 4\) and a prior \(\mathcal N(5, 1)\):

  • Precision: \(1/1 + 5/4 = 2.25\), so the posterior variance is 0.444 (standard deviation 0.667).
  • Mean: \((5/1 + 5 \times 6/4)/2.25 = 12.5/2.25 = 5.556\). The same thing as pseudo-observations: the prior is worth \(4/1 = 4\) points at 5, so \((4 \times 5 + 5 \times 6)/9 = 5.556\).
  • 95% credible interval: \(5.556 \pm 1.96 \times 0.667 = [4.25, 6.86]\). The MLE alone would be \(6 \pm 1.96 \times 0.894\), with \(\text{SE} = \sqrt{4/5} = 0.894\).
  • More data: with 50 points of the same mean, the data's weight rises from 0.56 to 0.93 and the posterior mean to 5.93.

Why conjugacy matters

  • Closed form. The posterior, its mean, its intervals and its predictions all have formulas: no integrals, no sampling.
  • Streaming updates. Keep two numbers per item (per ad, per arm, per merchant) and add counts as data arrive. Thompson sampling in bandits does exactly this.
  • Readable priors. A conjugate prior is pseudo-data, so you can say “this prior is worth 20 users”, and defend it.
  • The limit: conjugacy is a convenience, not a reason to believe a prior; choose the prior for its content. Most models, logistic regression and neural networks included, have no conjugate prior, so their posteriors need sampling (MCMC), variational inference or the Laplace approximation (section 08).

Say it in an interview: “A conjugate prior keeps the posterior in the prior's family, so updating is arithmetic: \(\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)\) plus \(k\) successes in \(n\) becomes \(\text{Beta}(\alpha + k, \beta + n - k)\). \(\text{Beta}(2, 2)\) with 7 of 10 gives \(\text{Beta}(9, 5)\), mean 0.64. For a Gaussian mean with known variance, precisions add and the posterior mean is a precision-weighted average. It matters because it's closed-form and streams; without conjugacy you need sampling or an approximation.”

08MAP vs the full posterior

MAP keeps one point of the posterior. A full Bayesian analysis keeps the whole distribution, and the difference shows up in five places. The running example: a flat prior and 7 heads in 10, so the posterior is \(\text{Beta}(8, 4)\).

Mean, median or mode

\(\text{Beta}(8, 4)\) has mode 0.700 (here MAP equals MLE, because the prior is flat), median 0.676 and mean 0.667. Each is the best single summary under a different cost of being wrong: the mean minimizes the expected squared error, the median the expected absolute error, the mode the 0–1 loss (in the limit). They differ when the posterior is skewed, which happens with little data or near 0 and 1, and merge as \(n\) grows.

The posterior predictive

The question: what's the probability that the next toss is a head? The plug-in answer uses one estimate, \(p(x_{\text{new}} \mid \hat\theta)\). The Bayesian answer averages the prediction over every \(\theta\), weighted by the posterior:

\[ \begin{aligned} p(x_{\text{new}} \mid D) &= \int p(x_{\text{new}} \mid \theta)\,p(\theta \mid D)\,d\theta \\ P(\text{head} \mid D) &= \mathbb E[\theta \mid D] = \frac{k + \alpha}{n + \alpha + \beta} \end{aligned} \]

In words: the predictive probability of a head is the posterior mean of \(\theta\). With a flat prior that's \((k + 1)/(n + 2)\), Laplace's rule of succession, which is “add one imaginary head and one imaginary tail”.

MAP depends on how you write the parameter; MLE doesn't

Same flat prior on \(\theta\), same 7 of 10, so the MAP is 0.7. Now estimate the log-odds \(z = \log\frac{\theta}{1 - \theta}\) instead, as logistic regression does. The belief is unchanged, but a density changes when you change variables: it gets multiplied by \(\lvert d\theta/dz \rvert = \theta(1 - \theta)\), so a prior that's flat in \(\theta\) isn't flat in \(z\):

\[ \begin{aligned} p(z \mid D) &\propto \theta^7(1 - \theta)^3 \cdot \theta(1 - \theta) = \theta^8(1 - \theta)^4 \\ \text{peak at } \theta &= \frac{8}{12} = 0.667 \end{aligned} \]

In words: the same belief and the same data give a MAP of 0.700 in one coordinate system and 0.667 in another. The likelihood is a function, not a density, so it has no such factor: maximizing over \(z\) gives \(\hat z = \log(7/3) = 0.847\), which maps back to \(\theta = 0.7\). That is the MLE's invariance from section 03.

The posterior median is invariant too, since quantiles survive any increasing transform (the median of \(z\) is 0.736, the log-odds of 0.676). The posterior mean isn't: \(\mathbb E[z] = 0.760\), while the log-odds of \(\mathbb E[\theta] = 0.667\) is 0.693. So a MAP estimate is a property of the belief plus a coordinate system, another reason to see it as regularized MLE rather than as a Bayesian summary.

Which prior is “flat”? The example shows that “uninformative” depends on the coordinates. The Jeffreys prior, proportional to \(\sqrt{I(\theta)}\), is the standard answer: choose it in \(\theta\) or in the log-odds and you get the same belief. For a coin it's \(\sqrt{1/[\theta(1 - \theta)]}\), the \(\text{Beta}(\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\) distribution; with 7 of 10 its posterior is \(\text{Beta}(7.5, 3.5)\), mean 0.682. It settles which prior to call flat; the MAP still moves between coordinate systems.

The Laplace approximation

When the posterior has no closed form, approximate it with a Gaussian centred at the MAP, with variance from the curvature there:

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\theta \mid D) &\approx \mathcal N\big(\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}},\ H^{-1}\big) \\ H &= -\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}\log p(\theta \mid D)\,\Big|_{\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}} \end{aligned} \]

In words: near its peak the log-posterior is roughly a parabola (a second-order Taylor expansion), and the exponential of a parabola is a Gaussian. Its variance is one over the curvature, the same idea as the Fisher standard error.

  • On \(\text{Beta}(8, 4)\): peak 0.7, curvature 47.6, so a standard deviation of 0.145, against the true 0.131. The Gaussian also puts 1.9% of its probability above \(\theta = 1\), which is impossible, because the true posterior is skewed against the boundary. With 70 of 100 the two standard deviations are 0.0458 and 0.0453: nearly exact.
  • With a flat prior \(H\) is the observed Fisher information, so the Laplace interval is the Wald interval.
  • Uses: Bayesian logistic regression, approximating the evidence (BIC is a cruder version of it), and cheap uncertainty for neural networks by fitting a Laplace approximation to the last layer.

Say it in an interview: “MAP keeps the peak. The full posterior also gives the mean, credible intervals with the probability reading people want, and a predictive distribution that averages over \(\theta\): after 3 heads in 3, the plug-in says the next toss is a head with probability 1, the flat-prior predictive says 0.8. MAP also isn't invariant to reparameterization, because a density picks up a Jacobian; the MLE is. When there's no closed form, the Laplace approximation fits a Gaussian at the MAP with the inverse Hessian as its covariance.”

09Hidden data: EM as maximum likelihood

Sometimes part of each data point is never observed: which cluster a point came from, which of two coins was tossed. The likelihood then has to sum over the hidden values \(z\):

\[ \ell(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log \sum_{z} p(x_i, z \mid \theta) \]

In words: each point's probability is the total over every way the hidden part could have been. The log of a sum doesn't split into simple terms, so there's usually no closed-form MLE.

The expectation–maximization (EM) algorithm alternates two easy steps. The E-step uses the current \(\theta\) to compute each point's posterior over \(z\), a soft assignment (the “responsibilities”). The M-step then does ordinary MLE as if those soft assignments had been observed: weighted counts, weighted means, weighted variances. Each round never lowers the likelihood, and the loop converges to a local maximum (strictly, a stationary point of the likelihood), so the starting point matters and restarts help. Adding a log-prior to the M-step turns the same loop into a MAP search, which also stops a mixture component from collapsing onto a single point. Gaussian mixtures, and k-means as their limit with hard assignments and equal spherical variances shrinking to 0, are worked through in unsupervised learning.

Say it in an interview: “EM is maximum likelihood when some variables are hidden. The E-step fills them in with their posterior under the current parameters, the M-step does weighted MLE as if they were observed, and the likelihood never decreases, but it only finds a local optimum.”

Numerical MLE by gradient ascent on the log-likelihood

def gaussian_mle(x, lr=0.1, steps=2000):
    mu, s = 0.0, 0.0            # s = log sigma keeps sigma > 0
    n = len(x)
    for _ in range(steps):
        var = exp(2 * s)
        r = x - mu
        g_mu = sum(r) / var               # d loglik / d mu
        g_s = sum(r**2) / var - n         # d loglik / d s
        mu += lr * g_mu / n               # ascent: + gradient
        s += lr * g_s / n
    return mu, exp(2 * s)

# x = [3, 5, 6, 8, 8] → mu 6.000, var 3.600 (divides by n)
# log-likelihood at the optimum: -10.297

Optimizing \(\log\sigma\) rather than \(\sigma\) keeps the variance positive without constraints, the same trick a network uses when it predicts a variance. Gradient ascent on \(\ell\) is gradient descent on the NLL.

A Beta–Binomial updater

class BetaBinomial:
    def __init__(self, a=1.0, b=1.0):  # Beta(1,1) = flat
        self.a, self.b = a, b

    def update(self, heads, n):
        self.a += heads
        self.b += n - heads

    def mean(self):                    # = P(next is a head)
        return self.a / (self.a + self.b)

    def mode(self):                    # = MAP, for a, b > 1
        return (self.a - 1) / (self.a + self.b - 2)

    def interval(self, mass=0.95):
        lo = (1 - mass) / 2
        return beta_ppf([lo, 1 - lo], self.a, self.b)

# BetaBinomial(2, 2), update(7, 10) → Beta(9, 5):
#   mean 0.643, mode 0.667, interval [0.386, 0.861]
# then update(1, 5) → Beta(10, 9): mean 0.526,
#   mode 0.529; one toss at a time gives the same

MAP logistic regression

def map_logreg(X, y, tau2=None, lr=0.5, steps=1000):
    n, d = X.shape
    w, b = zeros(d), 0.0
    for _ in range(steps):
        p = sigmoid(X @ w + b)
        g_w = X.T @ (p - y)          # gradient of the NLL
        g_b = sum(p - y)
        if tau2 is not None:
            g_w += w / tau2          # prior N(0, tau2)
        w -= lr * g_w / n            # bias: flat prior
        b -= lr * g_b / n
    return w, b

# X = [[-2], [-1], [1], [2]], y = [0, 0, 1, 1]:
# tau2=None (MLE): w 3.4, 5.5, 7.8, 10.1 after
#   1e2, 1e3, 1e4, 1e5 steps: never converges
# tau2=1 (MAP): w 1.007 from 100 steps on
# tau2 = 0.1, 1, 10, 100 → w 0.24, 1.01, 2.28, 3.95

The prior term \(w/\tau^2\) is the derivative of \(\lVert w \rVert^2/(2\tau^2)\). It's what gives the separable data a finite answer: the NLL keeps falling as \(w\) grows, but the penalty eventually rises faster. In scikit-learn terms this is LogisticRegression(C=tau2).

13Interview questions

Answer out loud first, then open the answer. Each answer stands on its own: the short answer, the mechanism, the why, then the likely follow-up. Questions 4, 5, 10, 11, 16, 22 and 23 are hand drills; do them with a pen.

1What's the difference between probability and likelihood?

The same function \(p(x \mid \theta)\), read two ways: probability fixes \(\theta\) and varies the data; likelihood fixes the observed data and varies \(\theta\).

  • Probability: for a fair coin and 10 tosses, \(P(7 \text{ heads}) = 0.117\), and the probabilities of all 11 outcomes sum to 1.
  • Likelihood: having seen 7 heads, \(L(0.5) = 0.117\), \(L(0.7) = 0.267\), \(L(0.3) = 0.009\). It scores how well each \(\theta\) explains what happened.
  • Not a distribution over \(\theta\): it doesn't integrate to 1 (here 1/11), so only ratios matter: the data favour 0.7 over 0.5 by 2.28 to 1.
  • Its use: maximize it (MLE), or multiply it by a prior to get a posterior (Bayes).

Follow-up: “so is \(L(0.7) = 0.267\) the probability that \(\theta = 0.7\)?” No; that needs a prior. (01)

2Is the likelihood a probability distribution over the parameters? Can it be bigger than 1?

No, and yes.

  • It doesn't integrate to 1 over \(\theta\): for 7 heads in 10, \(\int_0^1 120\,\theta^7(1-\theta)^3\,d\theta = 1/11\). Each value is the probability of the same data under a different model, not of different \(\theta\)'s.
  • It can exceed 1 for continuous data, where it's a product of densities: a Gaussian with \(\sigma = 0.1\) has density 3.99 at its centre.
  • Making it a distribution over \(\theta\) takes a prior and normalizing by the evidence: with a flat prior, the 7-of-10 likelihood becomes \(\text{Beta}(8, 4)\).

(01, 04)

3Why do we maximize the log-likelihood instead of the likelihood?

Same maximizer, much easier to work with.

  • Same answer: log is strictly increasing, so the argmax doesn't move.
  • Sums, not products: i.i.d. data give a product; the log makes it a sum of per-example terms, so derivatives are sums and SGD can use mini-batches.
  • Numerics: 10,000 tosses at 70% heads have sequence probability around \(10^{-2653}\), which is 0 in float64 (the smallest positive value is about \(5 \times 10^{-324}\)). The log-likelihood is −6,108.6, a normal number.
  • Algebra: Gaussian and Bernoulli densities are exponentials, so their logs are squares and linear terms.

Follow-up: minimizing the negative log-likelihood is how this appears as a loss. (02)

4Hand drill: derive the MLE of a coin's heads probability. What is it for 7 heads in 10?

\(\hat\theta = k/n = 0.7\).

  • Log-likelihood (dropping the constant \(\binom{n}{k}\)): \(\ell(\theta) = k\log\theta + (n-k)\log(1-\theta)\).
  • Derivative: \(k/\theta - (n-k)/(1-\theta) = 0\), so \(k(1-\theta) = (n-k)\theta\) and \(\theta = k/n\).
  • Check it's a maximum: \(\ell'' = -k/\theta^2 - (n-k)/(1-\theta)^2 \lt 0\) everywhere, so \(\ell\) is concave.
  • Numbers: the slope (score) is \(+8\) at 0.5, 0 at 0.7, \(-6.25\) at 0.8.

Follow-up: its standard error is \(\sqrt{0.7 \times 0.3/10} = 0.145\) (question 10). The same recipe gives the other classics: Poisson counts 3, 5, 4 → \(\hat\lambda = \bar x = 4\) (SE \(\sqrt{4/3} = 1.15\)); exponential waits 2, 1, 3 → rate \(1/\bar x = 0.5\); Uniform\((0, \theta)\) → \(\hat\theta = \max x_i = 3\), found at the edge of the allowed region, not by a zero derivative. (02, 02)

5Hand drill: derive the MLE of a Gaussian's mean and variance, and apply it to 3, 5, 6, 8, 8.

\(\hat\mu = \bar x = 6\) and \(\hat\sigma^2 = \frac1n\sum(x_i - \bar x)^2 = 3.6\).

  • Log-likelihood: \(\ell = -\frac n2\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum(x_i - \mu)^2\).
  • Mean: \(\partial\ell/\partial\mu = \sum(x_i - \mu)/\sigma^2 = 0\) gives \(\bar x\), whatever \(\sigma\) is.
  • Variance: \(\partial\ell/\partial\sigma^2 = -n/(2\sigma^2) + \sum(x_i - \bar x)^2/(2\sigma^4) = 0\) gives the average squared deviation.
  • Numbers: deviations −3, −1, 0, 2, 2; squares sum to 18; \(18/5 = 3.6\). The unbiased version is \(18/4 = 4.5\). By invariance \(\hat\sigma = \sqrt{3.6} = 1.90\).

Follow-up: why does it divide by \(n\) and is that a problem? Question 6. (02)

6Why is the MLE of the variance biased? Why does dividing by n − 1 fix it?

Because it measures spread around \(\bar x\), which was fitted to the same points and so sits closer to them than the true mean does. On average it comes out low by exactly one \(\sigma^2\) in the sum of squares.

  • The identity: \(\sum(x_i - \bar x)^2 = \sum(x_i - \mu)^2 - n(\bar x - \mu)^2\).
  • Take expectations: \(n\sigma^2 - n \cdot \sigma^2/n = (n - 1)\sigma^2\), since \(\bar x\) has variance \(\sigma^2/n\). So \(\mathbb E[\hat\sigma^2_{\text{MLE}}] = \frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2\), and dividing by \(n - 1\) instead is unbiased.
  • Numbers: for 3, 5, 6, 8, 8, the squares around 6 sum to 18 but around 5 to 23, and \(23 - 18 = 5(6-5)^2\). Simulating samples of 5 from \(\mathcal N(6, 4)\): the MLE averages 3.20, the \(n - 1\) version 4.00.
  • Degrees of freedom: the deviations from \(\bar x\) sum to 0, so only \(n - 1\) are free.
  • It fades: the ratio is 0.8 at \(n = 5\), 0.99 at \(n = 100\): biased but consistent.

Follow-up: “so is unbiased better?” Not necessarily: error is variance plus bias squared. For \(n = 5\), \(\sigma^2 = 4\), dividing by 4 (unbiased) has MSE 8.00, by 5 (the MLE) 5.76, by 6 only 5.33. Ridge and lasso are deliberately biased for the same reason. And \(s = \sqrt{s^2}\) is biased low anyway (1.88 on average for a true \(\sigma\) of 2). (02)

7Show that least squares is maximum likelihood. What's the assumption, and what if it's wrong?

Least squares is the MLE when the targets are the model's prediction plus independent Gaussian noise of constant variance.

  • Model: \(y_i = w^\top x_i + \varepsilon_i\), \(\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)\).
  • NLL: \(\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum(y_i - w^\top x_i)^2 + \frac n2\log(2\pi\sigma^2)\). Only the sum of squares depends on \(w\), so the MLE of \(w\) minimizes it. The MLE of \(\sigma^2\) is \(\text{RSS}/n\) (unbiased: \(\text{RSS}/(n - p)\)).
  • Other noise, other loss: Laplace noise gives absolute error (the median), Bernoulli gives log loss.
  • If the noise has heavy tails, least squares still estimates the conditional mean (and is the best linear unbiased estimator if the errors are uncorrelated with constant variance), but outliers drag it and its small-sample intervals become unreliable; with infinite-variance noise, such as Cauchy, even large samples don't save it. A heavier-tailed likelihood (Laplace, Huber, Student-t) is more robust.

Full family: loss functions. (02)

8Logistic regression as MLE: what's the likelihood, why is there no closed form, and is the optimum unique?

Each label is a Bernoulli with \(p_i = \sigma(w^\top x_i)\); the NLL is the log loss; it's convex but non-linear in \(w\), so it's solved iteratively.

  • NLL: \(-\sum[y_i\log p_i + (1 - y_i)\log(1 - p_i)]\), gradient \(X^\top(p - y)\).
  • No closed form: setting \(X^\top(p - y) = 0\) can't be solved for \(w\) algebraically because \(p\) is a sigmoid of \(w\).
  • The optimum: the NLL is convex (its Hessian \(X^\top W X\) with \(W = \operatorname{diag}(p_i(1 - p_i))\) is positive semi-definite), so any minimum gradient descent or Newton's method (IRLS) finds is global. It's unique when \(X\) has full column rank, and finite only if the classes aren't separable.
  • When it doesn't: perfectly separable data (question 13).

Follow-up: the same Hessian, inverted, gives the coefficients' standard errors. (02, linear-logistic-regression)

9What are the main properties of the MLE? When do they fail?

Invariant at any \(n\); and for large \(n\), under regularity conditions, consistent, asymptotically normal with variance \(1/I(\theta)\), and efficient.

  • Consistent: converges to the true value.
  • Asymptotically normal: \(\hat\theta \approx \mathcal N(\theta, 1/I(\theta))\), which gives standard errors and intervals.
  • Efficient: reaches the Cramér–Rao bound, the lowest variance any unbiased estimator can have.
  • Invariant: the MLE of \(g(\theta)\) is \(g(\hat\theta)\), exactly and at any \(n\); the odds' MLE for 7 of 10 is \(0.7/0.3 = 2.33\).
  • Failures: small samples (it can be biased, like the variance, or extreme, like \(\hat p = 1\)); the truth on the boundary or a support that depends on \(\theta\) (Uniform(0, θ)); parameters growing with \(n\); a misspecified model, where it converges to the KL-closest wrong model and needs robust standard errors.

(03)

10Hand drill: what is Fisher information? Use it to get the standard error of a coin's MLE for 7 of 10 and 70 of 100.

The expected curvature of the log-likelihood, \(I(\theta) = -\mathbb E[\ell''(\theta)]\); the MLE's variance is about \(1/I\). For a coin \(I = n/[\theta(1-\theta)]\), so \(\text{SE} = \sqrt{\hat\theta(1-\hat\theta)/n}\).

  • One toss: \(\partial^2/\partial\theta^2[x\log\theta + (1-x)\log(1-\theta)] = -x/\theta^2 - (1-x)/(1-\theta)^2\). Average with \(\mathbb E[x] = \theta\): \(1/\theta + 1/(1-\theta) = 1/[\theta(1-\theta)]\). Multiply by \(n\).
  • 7 of 10: \(I = 10/0.21 = 47.6\), \(\text{SE} = 0.145\), Wald interval [0.416, 0.984].
  • 70 of 100: \(I = 476\), \(\text{SE} = 0.046\), interval [0.610, 0.790]. Ten times the data, ten times the information, \(\sqrt{10}\) times smaller SE.
  • Intuition: a sharp peak means few \(\theta\) explain the data well. Equivalently, \(I\) is the variance of the score.
  • Same as the A/B formula \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\), there the variance of an average of 0/1 values.

Follow-up: observed information \(-\ell''(\hat\theta)\) is what software uses; with many parameters, invert the information matrix. (03)

11Hand drill: how do you compare two models by their likelihoods? Is the running example's coin fair?

Not by raw training likelihood, which always prefers the bigger model. Use a likelihood-ratio test for nested models, or an information criterion that charges for parameters.

  • Likelihood-ratio test: for nested models, \(2(\ell_{\text{big}} - \ell_{\text{small}})\) is approximately \(\chi^2\) with one degree of freedom per extra parameter (Wilks' theorem). Fair vs free coin, 7 of 10: \(\ell = -6.931\) vs \(-6.109\), so \(2 \times 0.823 = 1.65 \lt 3.84\) and \(p = 0.20\): fair isn't rejected. With 70 of 100 it's 16.5, \(p = 0.00005\).
  • AIC \(= 2d - 2\hat\ell\) and BIC \(= d\ln n - 2\hat\ell\) for \(d\) parameters; lower is better. 7 of 10: fair scores 13.86 on both, free 14.22 (AIC) and 14.52 (BIC), so both prefer fair. 70 of 100: free wins (AIC 124.2 vs 138.6).
  • Where they come from: AIC estimates out-of-sample log-likelihood; BIC approximates the log evidence, so it leans the same way as the Bayes factor of 1.29 for the fair coin. BIC's penalty grows with \(n\), so it picks smaller models.
  • The same 3.84 gives the likelihood-ratio interval: every \(\theta\) whose log-likelihood is within 1.92 of the peak, [0.39, 0.92] here.

(03, 04)

12When does maximum likelihood go wrong? Give concrete examples.

When the data are too few or too clean for the model's flexibility: it trusts the sample completely.

  • Extreme small samples: 3 heads in 3 gives \(\hat p = 1\), an SE of 0, and infinite log loss on the first tail. Zero word counts veto a class in Naive Bayes.
  • Separation: logistic weights grow without bound.
  • Too many parameters: a degree-4 polynomial through 5 points has zero residuals; a mixture component can collapse onto one point with infinite likelihood; covariance estimates are singular when \(n \le d\).
  • Wrong noise model: Gaussian likelihood with outliers.
  • The fix for the first three: a prior (MAP or a full posterior), or equivalently regularization.

(03)

13Your logistic regression's coefficients keep growing and the solver warns it didn't converge. What's happening?

Probably (quasi-)perfect separation: some feature or combination splits the classes, so the likelihood keeps increasing as the weights grow and there is no finite MLE.

  • Mechanism: on separable data every point can be pushed further to its correct side by scaling \(w\) up, which sends each \(p_i\) towards its label and the NLL towards 0.
  • Numbers: on \(x = -2, -1, 1, 2\), labels 0, 0, 1, 1, the weight is 3.4, 5.5, 7.8, 10.1 after \(10^2\) to \(10^5\) steps; the NLL at \(w = 10\) is 0.0001.
  • Fixes: an L2 prior (with \(\tau^2 = 1\), \(w\) settles at 1.007), Firth's correction (MAP with the Jeffreys prior), or removing a leaky feature (a separating feature is often leakage).
  • Symptoms: huge coefficients and standard errors, predicted probabilities of exactly 0 and 1.

(03, linear-logistic-regression)

14Write Bayes' rule for parameters. What's the evidence, why can MAP ignore it, and when can't you?

\(p(\theta \mid D) = p(D \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)/p(D)\): posterior = likelihood × prior / evidence.

  • Evidence: \(p(D) = \int p(D \mid \theta)p(\theta)\,d\theta\), the probability of the data averaged over the prior. With a flat prior and 7 of 10 it's 1/11, and the posterior is \(\text{Beta}(8, 4)\).
  • MAP ignores it: it doesn't depend on \(\theta\), so it can't move the argmax.
  • You need it to normalize the posterior (for means, intervals, predictions) and to compare models: a fair-coin model gives 7 of 10 probability 0.117, the flat-prior model 0.091, a Bayes factor of 1.29 for the simpler model. The evidence penalizes flexibility automatically.

(04)

15MLE vs MAP: what's the difference, and when does it matter?

MLE treats \(\theta\) as a fixed unknown and maximizes the likelihood. MAP gives \(\theta\) a prior and maximizes likelihood × prior, which is MLE plus a penalty equal to minus the log-prior. Both return a single point.

  • With a flat prior they're equal.
  • It matters with little data: 3 heads in 3 gives an MLE of 1.0; MAP with a \(\text{Beta}(2, 2)\) prior gives 0.8.
  • It fades with lots of data: at 700 of 1,000 the \(\text{Beta}(5, 5)\) MAP is 0.698 against an MLE of 0.700.
  • When to prefer MAP (or the posterior mean): small counts and new items, such as a new ad's click rate after a handful of impressions, with a Beta prior estimated from past ads (empirical Bayes); and whenever the MLE degenerates (separation, zero counts, more parameters than data).
  • The risk: a strong, wrong prior biases MAP until the data swamp it, and forever if it gives the truth zero probability.
  • In ML terms: unregularized training is MLE; L2 or weight decay is MAP with a Gaussian prior; L1 is MAP with a Laplace prior.
  • Neither is fully Bayesian: both discard the posterior's spread. MAP also moves when you reparameterize, while the MLE doesn't.

16Hand drill: MAP of a coin's bias with a Beta(2, 2) prior, for 7 of 10 and for 3 of 3. And with Beta(5, 5)?

\(\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} = (k + \alpha - 1)/(n + \alpha + \beta - 2)\).

  • Derivation: log-posterior \((k + \alpha - 1)\log\theta + (n - k + \beta - 1)\log(1-\theta)\); set the derivative to zero, exactly as for the MLE.
  • \(\text{Beta}(2, 2)\): 7 of 10 → \(8/12 = 0.667\); 3 of 3 → \(4/5 = 0.8\).
  • \(\text{Beta}(5, 5)\): 7 of 10 → \(11/18 = 0.611\); 3 of 3 → \(7/11 = 0.636\).
  • As a blend: with \(\text{Beta}(5, 5)\) the data's weight is \(10/18\), so \(0.556 \times 0.7 + 0.444 \times 0.5 = 0.611\).

(05)

17How does a prior act like pseudo-counts, and what happens to MAP as n grows?

A \(\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)\) prior adds \(\alpha - 1\) imaginary heads and \(\beta - 1\) imaginary tails to the MAP (\(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) to the posterior mean). The real data grow while the imaginary ones don't, so MAP → MLE.

  • Numbers at 70% heads: \(\text{Beta}(5, 5)\) gives 0.611, 0.685, 0.698 at \(n = 10, 100, 1000\); \(\text{Beta}(20, 20)\) gives 0.542, 0.645, 0.693.
  • In log terms: one log-prior term against \(n\) log-likelihood terms.
  • Same idea elsewhere: smoothed target encoding (the overall mean counts as \(m\) rows), add-one smoothing, Bayesian A/B priors “worth 1,000 users”.
  • Exception: zero prior probability on a region can never be overturned.

(05, 05)

18Derive L2 regularization from a Gaussian prior. What is λ in terms of the prior?

A \(\mathcal N(0, \tau^2)\) prior on each weight gives ridge with \(\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2\).

  • Log-prior: \(-\log p(w) = \sum_j w_j^2/(2\tau^2) + \text{const}\).
  • Negative log-posterior: \(\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum(y_i - w^\top x_i)^2 + \frac{1}{2\tau^2}\lVert w\rVert^2 + \text{const}\).
  • Multiply by \(2\sigma^2\): \(\sum(y_i - w^\top x_i)^2 + (\sigma^2/\tau^2)\lVert w\rVert^2\). Tight prior or noisy data → big \(\lambda\); flat prior → OLS.
  • Numbers: one weight with \(\sum xy = 33\), \(\sum x^2 = 30\), \(\sigma^2 = 1\): \(\tau^2 = 0.1\) gives \(\lambda = 10\) and \(\hat w = 33/40 = 0.825\) instead of 1.1.
  • Logistic regression: NLL \(+ \lVert w\rVert^2/(2\tau^2)\); scikit-learn's \(C\) is exactly \(\tau^2\).

Follow-up: don't penalize the intercept (flat prior), and standardize features so one prior fits all weights. (06)

19Derive L1 from a Laplace prior. Why does it give exact zeros? Is the Bayesian posterior sparse?

A Laplace prior \(\propto \exp(-\lvert w_j\rvert/b)\) adds \(\lVert w\rVert_1/b\) to the NLL; times \(2\sigma^2\), that's lasso with \(\lambda = 2\sigma^2/b\). Only the MAP is sparse.

  • Why zeros: the prior has a kink at 0, so the L1 penalty adds a slope of \(\lambda\) however small the weight; if the data's slope at 0 (here \(-2\sum xy = -66\)) is weaker than that, the optimum is exactly 0. The L2 penalty's slope \(2\lambda w\) vanishes at 0, so it never gets there.
  • Numbers: one weight, \(\hat w = (33 - \lambda/2)/30\): \(b = 0.1\) (\(\lambda = 20\)) gives 0.767; \(b = 0.025\) (\(\lambda = 80\)) gives exactly 0.
  • Posterior not sparse: with \(b = 0.025\) the posterior mode is 0 but its mean is 0.075, and \(P(w \gt 0) = 0.88\). Sparsity is a property of taking the peak.

Follow-up: elastic net = Gaussian × Laplace prior. Mechanics: linear-logistic-regression. (06)

20Is weight decay the same as L2 regularization?

For plain SGD, yes. For Adam, no: the training paths differ, and AdamW's decoupled decay works better in practice.

  • SGD: \(w \leftarrow w - \eta(\nabla\text{NLL} + \lambda w) = (1 - \eta\lambda)w - \eta\nabla\text{NLL}\): adding the L2 gradient is the same as shrinking the weights by a constant factor each step. Both are a Gaussian prior, i.e. MAP.
  • Adam + L2: the loss is still NLL \(+ \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\lVert w \rVert^2\), so its fixed point is the Gaussian-prior MAP. But on the way, the L2 gradient \(\lambda w\) is divided by Adam's per-weight scale, so weights with a large gradient history are barely regularized.
  • AdamW: applies the decay to the weights directly, outside the adaptive step, so every weight decays at the same rate. It regularizes more evenly, but its loss has no penalty term, so it isn't exactly any fixed Gaussian prior.

(06, deep-learning-basics)

21What is a conjugate prior? Give examples, and say why it matters.

A prior whose posterior stays in the same family for a given likelihood, so updating is just changing parameters.

  • Beta–Binomial: \(\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta) \to \text{Beta}(\alpha + k, \beta + n - k)\).
  • Dirichlet–multinomial: add counts per category. Gamma–Poisson: \(\text{Gamma}(a + \sum x_i, b + n)\).
  • Gaussian–Gaussian (known variance): precisions add, the mean is precision-weighted.
  • Why it matters: closed-form posteriors, means, intervals and predictions; streaming updates (Thompson sampling keeps two numbers per arm); priors readable as pseudo-data.
  • Limit: most models (logistic regression, neural nets) have none; use MCMC, variational inference or the Laplace approximation.

22Hand drill: prior Beta(2, 2). You see 7 heads in 10, then 1 head in 5 more. What's the posterior, its mean and its mode after each step?

\(\text{Beta}(9, 5)\), then \(\text{Beta}(10, 9)\).

  • Step 1: \(\alpha = 2 + 7 = 9\), \(\beta = 2 + 3 = 5\). Mean \(9/14 = 0.643\), mode \(8/12 = 0.667\), 95% interval [0.39, 0.86].
  • Step 2: \(\alpha = 9 + 1 = 10\), \(\beta = 5 + 4 = 9\). Mean \(10/19 = 0.526\), mode \(9/17 = 0.529\), interval [0.31, 0.74].
  • Check: one update with 8 heads in 15 gives the same \(\text{Beta}(10, 9)\); order doesn't matter.
  • Next-toss probability is the posterior mean: 0.526.

(07)

23Hand drill: a mean has prior N(5, 1). You observe 5 points with mean 6 and known noise variance 4. What's the posterior?

\(\mathcal N(5.556, 0.444)\).

  • Precision: \(1/1 + 5/4 = 2.25\); variance \(1/2.25 = 0.444\), standard deviation 0.667.
  • Mean: \((5 \times 1 + 6 \times 1.25)/2.25 = 12.5/2.25 = 5.556\), a precision-weighted average.
  • As pseudo-data: the prior is worth \(\sigma^2/\tau_0^2 = 4\) points at 5: \((4 \times 5 + 5 \times 6)/9 = 5.556\).
  • Compare: the MLE is 6 with SE \(\sqrt{4/5} = 0.894\); the posterior is pulled towards 5 and is narrower.

(07)

24What does a full posterior give you that a MAP estimate doesn't?

Uncertainty, and predictions that account for it.

  • Other summaries: the mean and median, which can differ from the mode: \(\text{Beta}(8, 4)\) has mode 0.700, median 0.676, mean 0.667.
  • Credible intervals with a direct probability reading.
  • The posterior predictive: average the prediction over \(\theta\). After 3 heads in 3, plugging in the MLE says the next toss is a head with probability 1 (infinite log loss on a tail); the flat-prior predictive says \(4/5 = 0.8\). After 7 of 10, two more heads: plug-in 0.49, predictive 0.462.
  • Invariance: the posterior itself doesn't depend on how you write \(\theta\); its MAP does.
  • Cost: integrals, which conjugacy, sampling or approximations handle.

(08)

25What's the difference between a credible interval and a confidence interval?

A credible interval is a probability statement about \(\theta\) given the data and prior; a confidence interval is a statement about the procedure's long-run coverage.

  • Credible, 95%: \(P(\theta \in [a, b] \mid D) = 0.95\). For 7 of 10 with a flat prior: [0.390, 0.891] equal-tailed, [0.412, 0.907] highest-density.
  • Confidence, 95%: 95% of intervals built this way contain the true \(\theta\); this one does or doesn't. For 7 of 10: Wald [0.416, 0.984], Wilson [0.397, 0.892], Clopper–Pearson [0.348, 0.933].
  • In practice: with a flat prior and moderate \(n\) they nearly agree (Wilson vs credible here); they differ with small \(n\), near 0 or 1, or with an informative prior.

(08)

26Is the MAP estimate invariant to reparameterization? Show with an example.

No. A density picks up a Jacobian factor when you change variables, which moves its peak. The MLE is invariant because the likelihood is a function, not a density.

  • Example: flat prior on \(\theta\), 7 of 10: MAP in \(\theta\) is 0.7.
  • In log-odds \(z\): the flat prior becomes \(p(z) \propto \theta(1 - \theta)\), so the posterior is \(\propto \theta^8(1-\theta)^4\), with its peak at \(\theta = 8/12 = 0.667\).
  • MLE in \(z\): \(\hat z = \log(7/3) = 0.847\), which is \(\theta = 0.7\) again.
  • Posterior median is invariant; the posterior mean isn't.

Follow-up: this is why MAP is best seen as regularized MLE rather than a Bayesian summary. (08)

27What is the Laplace approximation, and where is it used?

Approximating the posterior by a Gaussian centred at the MAP, with covariance equal to the inverse Hessian of the negative log-posterior there.

  • Why it works: a second-order Taylor expansion of the log-posterior is a parabola, and \(\exp\) of a parabola is a Gaussian.
  • Example: \(\text{Beta}(8, 4)\): mode 0.7, curvature 47.6, so sd 0.145 against a true 0.131; it even puts 1.9% of its mass above 1. With 70 of 100: 0.0458 vs 0.0453, nearly exact.
  • Uses: Bayesian logistic regression, approximating the evidence (BIC), last-layer uncertainty in neural networks.
  • Limits: poor for skewed or multi-modal posteriors and near boundaries.

(08)

28What is EM, and why is it needed?

An algorithm for maximum likelihood when some variables are hidden, such as cluster labels.

  • The problem: \(\ell(\theta) = \sum_i\log\sum_z p(x_i, z \mid \theta)\); the log of a sum has no closed-form maximizer.
  • E-step: with the current \(\theta\), compute each point's posterior over \(z\) (soft assignments).
  • M-step: ordinary MLE with those weights (weighted counts, means, variances).
  • Guarantees: the likelihood never decreases; it converges to a local maximum (strictly, a stationary point), so use several starts. A prior in the M-step gives MAP-EM and prevents collapsed components.
  • k-means is EM for Gaussian mixtures in the limit of hard assignments and equal spherical variances shrinking to 0.

(09, unsupervised-learning)

29Which everyday ML techniques are secretly MLE or MAP?

Most losses are negative log-likelihoods, and most penalties and smoothing tricks are log-priors or posterior means.

  • MLE: MSE (Gaussian), MAE (Laplace), log loss (Bernoulli), cross-entropy (categorical), language-model training (next-token categorical).
  • MAP: ridge and weight decay (Gaussian prior), lasso (Laplace), elastic net (both).
  • Posterior means: add-\(\alpha\) smoothing in Naive Bayes (\(\text{Dirichlet}(\alpha)\) prior; as a MAP it's \(\text{Dirichlet}(\alpha + 1)\)), smoothed target encoding (a prior worth \(m\) rows).
  • Not a weight prior: label smoothing is \((1 - \varepsilon)\) × NLL + \(\varepsilon\) × cross-entropy to uniform, a pull on the outputs.
  • EM: Gaussian mixtures, k-means.

(10)

30Write pseudocode to fit a model by numerical maximum likelihood, and to turn it into MAP logistic regression.

Gradient ascent on the log-likelihood (or descent on the NLL); for MAP, add the log-prior's gradient.

  • Gaussian MLE: parameters \(\mu\) and \(s = \log\sigma\); gradients \(\sum r/\sigma^2\) and \(\sum r^2/\sigma^2 - n\) with \(r = x - \mu\); step uphill. On 3, 5, 6, 8, 8 it reaches \(\mu = 6\), \(\sigma^2 = 3.6\).
  • Logistic MLE: \(p = \sigma(Xw + b)\); gradient of the NLL \(X^\top(p - y)\); step downhill.
  • MAP: add \(w/\tau^2\) to the weight gradient (Gaussian prior), not to the bias. On separable data this turns a diverging weight into 1.007 for \(\tau^2 = 1\).
  • Details: optimize \(\log\sigma\) so the variance stays positive; average gradients over \(n\); stop when the gradient is near 0.

14Cheat sheet

Likelihood

\[ \begin{aligned} L(\theta) &= p(\text{observed data} \mid \theta) \\ \ell(\theta) &= \log L(\theta) = \sum_i \log p(x_i \mid \theta) \end{aligned} \]

Same formula as a probability, read with the data fixed. Doesn't integrate to 1 over \(\theta\) (7 of 10: 1/11); only ratios matter. 7 of 10: \(L(0.5) = 0.117\), \(L(0.7) = 0.267\).

MLE

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat\theta_{\text{coin}} &= k/n \\ \hat\lambda_{\text{Poisson}} &= \bar x \\ \hat\mu &= \bar x \\ \hat\sigma^2_{\text{MLE}} &= \tfrac1n\textstyle\sum(x_i - \bar x)^2 \\ \mathbb E[\hat\sigma^2_{\text{MLE}}] &= \tfrac{n-1}{n}\,\sigma^2 \end{aligned} \]

3, 5, 6, 8, 8: mean 6, MLE variance 3.6, unbiased 4.5. Exponential rate \(1/\bar x\); Uniform\((0, \theta)\): \(\max x_i\). Gaussian noise → MSE; Laplace → MAE; Bernoulli → log loss; categorical → cross-entropy. Logistic: gradient \(X^\top(p - y)\), convex, no closed form.

Fisher information

\[ \begin{aligned} I(\theta) &= -\mathbb E[\ell''(\theta)] = \operatorname{Var}(\ell'(\theta)) \\ \operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta) &\approx 1/I(\theta) \\ I_{\text{coin}} &= \frac{n}{\theta(1 - \theta)} \\ \text{SE}_{\text{coin}} &= \sqrt{\hat\theta(1 - \hat\theta)/n} \\ \text{AIC} &= 2d - 2\hat\ell \\ \text{BIC} &= d\ln n - 2\hat\ell \end{aligned} \]

7 of 10: SE 0.145, Wald [0.416, 0.984]; 70 of 100: SE 0.046. LR interval: \(\ell\) within 1.92 of its peak; LR test: \(2(\ell_{\text{big}} - \ell_{\text{small}}) \approx \chi^2\) (7 of 10 vs fair: 1.65, \(p = 0.20\)). Delta method: \(\text{SE}(g(\hat\theta)) \approx \lvert g'(\hat\theta)\rvert\,\text{SE}(\hat\theta)\). MLE: invariant at any \(n\); consistent, asymptotically normal, efficient (Cramér–Rao) for large \(n\). Fails on 3 of 3, separation, too many parameters.

Bayes and MAP

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\theta \mid D) &= \frac{p(D \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)}{p(D)} \\ \hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} &= \arg\max_\theta\,\big[\log p(D \mid \theta) + \log p(\theta)\big] \\ \hat\theta_{\text{MAP, coin}} &= \frac{k + \alpha - 1}{n + \alpha + \beta - 2} \end{aligned} \]

Evidence drops out of the argmax; needed for normalizing and model comparison. \(\text{Beta}(2, 2)\): 7 of 10 → 0.667, 3 of 3 → 0.8. MAP → MLE as \(n\) grows.

Priors are regularizers

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat w_{\mathcal N(0, \tau^2)} &= \arg\min_w\ \text{RSS} + \tfrac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}\lVert w \rVert_2^2 \\ \hat w_{\text{Laplace}(0, b)} &= \arg\min_w\ \text{RSS} + \tfrac{2\sigma^2}{b}\lVert w \rVert_1 \end{aligned} \]

Tighter prior = bigger \(\lambda\). scikit-learn: logistic \(C = \tau^2\); Ridge \(\alpha = \sigma^2/\tau^2\); Lasso \(\alpha = \sigma^2/(nb)\). Only the MAP of a Laplace prior is sparse. Weight decay = L2 for SGD; under Adam they differ, and AdamW's decoupled decay isn't exactly a prior.

Conjugate updates

\[ \begin{aligned} \theta \mid D &\sim \text{Beta}(\alpha + k,\ \beta + n - k) \\ \tfrac{1}{\tau_n^2} &= \tfrac{1}{\tau_0^2} + \tfrac{n}{\sigma^2} \\ \mu_n &= \tau_n^2\Big(\tfrac{\mu_0}{\tau_0^2} + \tfrac{n\bar x}{\sigma^2}\Big) \end{aligned} \]

\(\text{Beta}(2, 2)\) + 7 of 10 = \(\text{Beta}(9, 5)\), mean 0.643. Dirichlet–multinomial adds counts; Gamma–Poisson: \(\text{Gamma}(a + \sum x, b + n)\).

Full posterior

\[ \begin{aligned} P(\text{head} \mid D) &= \mathbb E[\theta \mid D] = \frac{k + \alpha}{n + \alpha + \beta} \\ p(\theta \mid D) &\approx \mathcal N\big(\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}},\ H^{-1}\big) \end{aligned} \]

3 of 3, flat prior: predictive 0.8, not 1. \(\text{Beta}(8, 4)\): mode 0.700, median 0.676, mean 0.667. MAP isn't invariant to reparameterization (7 of 10 in log-odds: 0.667); MLE is. EM = MLE with hidden variables, local optima.
