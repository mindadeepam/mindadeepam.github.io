Loss functions

Where each loss comes from, what it makes a model predict, its gradient, and how it fails: regression, classification, imbalance, calibration, ranking, contrastive, segmentation and generative losses, with the follow-up questions that test real understanding.

About 40 minutes · likelihood first, then gradients, then the practical traps

In short

  1. A loss is a negative log-likelihood under an assumed noise model. Gaussian noise gives MSE, which predicts the conditional mean; Laplace gives MAE (the median); Bernoulli gives binary cross-entropy; categorical gives cross-entropy. The assumption decides what the model predicts and how hard outliers pull.
  2. Sigmoid with BCE and softmax with cross-entropy both give the gradient \(p - y\) at the logits: bounded, largest when the model is confidently wrong, never saturating. MSE through a sigmoid multiplies that by \(p(1 - p)\) and stalls exactly there.
  3. Compute losses from logits with log-sum-exp (CrossEntropyLoss, BCEWithLogitsLoss). Taking the log of a softmax lets a probability underflow to 0, so \(\log 0 = -\infty\) and the gradient turns into NaN; clamping probabilities silently zeroes the gradient of the worst examples.
  4. Cross-entropy is entropy plus KL: \(H(p, q) = H(p) + \mathrm{KL}(p \Vert q)\). Minimizing it is maximum likelihood and minimizes the forward KL, which is mass-covering.
  5. Class weights and resampling multiply the predicted odds by a constant; focal loss pulls probabilities towards 0.5. All of them change the ranking little and the calibration a lot, so correct the probabilities afterwards, or just move the threshold.
  6. Label smoothing caps the logit gap and fights overconfidence; focal loss reweights examples by difficulty; temperature scaling fixes calibration after training without changing accuracy.
  7. Ranking and contrastive losses are cross-entropy over pairs or candidates. InfoNCE is softmax cross-entropy over a batch's similarities: its temperature sets how hard it focuses on hard negatives, and its value is tied to \(\log N\), so more negatives help.
  8. You can't train on accuracy, F1 or NDCG, because their gradient is zero almost everywhere. Train a smooth surrogate, report the metric, and check the starting loss (\(\log K\) for \(K\) classes) before anything else.

01Where losses come from

A model doesn't just output a number. Implicitly it outputs the parameters of a distribution over the target. Choose that distribution's family, and the loss follows: it's minus the log of the probability the model gives to what actually happened.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat\theta &= \arg\max_\theta \prod_{i=1}^{N} p(y_i \mid x_i; \theta) \\ &= \arg\min_\theta \sum_{i=1}^{N} -\log p(y_i \mid x_i; \theta) \end{aligned} \]

Where the product comes from: the examples are assumed independent given their inputs, and drawn from the same distribution (i.i.d.). The probability of the whole dataset then factorizes into one term per example. Correlated examples (repeated measurements of one user, neighbouring time steps) break this, and the summed loss then over-counts how much evidence you have.

Why the log: it's monotonic, so the best \(\theta\) doesn't move. It turns a product of many small numbers (which underflows) into a sum of per-example terms, which is what mini-batch SGD needs. And for exponential-family distributions it cancels the \(\exp\), leaving a term linear in the natural parameter (the mean for a Gaussian, the logit for a Bernoulli or categorical) minus a convex log-normalizer: a square, a softplus or a log-sum-exp.

Gaussian noise gives MSE

Assume additive Gaussian noise around the model's prediction \(\hat y = f(x)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} y &= f(x) + \varepsilon \\ \varepsilon &\sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2) \\ y \mid x &\sim \mathcal N(\hat y, \sigma^2) \end{aligned} \]

The density and its negative log are:

\[ \begin{aligned} p(y \mid x) &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma}\exp\Big(-\frac{(y - \hat y)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big) \\ -\log p(y \mid x) &= \frac{(y - \hat y)^2}{2\sigma^2} + \log\sigma + \tfrac12\log 2\pi \end{aligned} \]

With \(\sigma\) fixed, only the first term depends on the model, and \(1/2\sigma^2\) just rescales it. So maximizing the Gaussian likelihood is minimizing squared error. The Gaussian's tails fall off like \(e^{-r^2}\): a big residual is nearly impossible under the model, so the loss charges for it quadratically.

Laplace noise gives MAE

\[ \begin{aligned} p(y \mid x) &= \frac{1}{2b}\exp\Big(-\frac{|y - \hat y|}{b}\Big) \\ -\log p(y \mid x) &= \frac{|y - \hat y|}{b} + \log 2b \end{aligned} \]

Laplace tails are only exponential in \(|r|\): a big residual is unlikely rather than impossible, so it's charged linearly.

Bernoulli and categorical give cross-entropy

For a 0/1 label with \(p = P(y = 1 \mid x)\), the Bernoulli likelihood is \(p^y (1 - p)^{1 - y}\), so

\[ -\log p(y \mid x) = -\big[y\log p + (1 - y)\log(1 - p)\big] \]

For \(K\) classes with a one-hot \(y\) and true class \(c\), the categorical likelihood is \(\prod_k p_k^{y_k}\), so

\[ \begin{aligned} -\log p(y \mid x) &= -\sum_{k=1}^{K} y_k \log p_k \\ &= -\log p_c \end{aligned} \]

The same recipe, from classic models to networks

Every classic model is “pick a distribution for \(y \mid x\), connect its mean to \(w^\top x\) through a link, minimize the negative log-likelihood”. These are the generalized linear models; a neural net keeps the same output layer and loss, and replaces \(w^\top x\) with a learned \(f(x)\).

If the classes are perfectly separable the likelihood keeps rising as \(\lVert w\rVert \to \infty\), and only regularization gives a finite answer.

Training a neural network is maximum likelihood

A network's last layer outputs the parameters of a distribution over the target, the loss is that distribution's negative log-likelihood, and SGD minimizes its average over mini-batches. So “which loss?” is really “which distribution?”, and the output activation is the link that keeps those parameters valid (a probability in \([0, 1]\), a positive variance).

\[ \begin{aligned} \theta^\star &= \arg\min_\theta\ \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} -\log p_\theta(y_i \mid x_i) \\ &\approx \arg\min_\theta\ \mathbb E_{(x, y) \sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[-\log p_\theta(y \mid x)\big] \\ &= \arg\min_\theta\ \mathbb E_{x}\Big[\mathrm{KL}\big(p_{\text{data}}(\cdot \mid x)\ \Vert\ p_\theta(\cdot \mid x)\big)\Big] \end{aligned} \]

In words: the average NLL estimates the cross-entropy between the data and the model, which is the data's own entropy (fixed) plus the KL from data to model. So MLE pulls the model's distribution towards the data's. A mini-batch average is an unbiased estimate of the same expectation, which is why SGD on mini-batches optimizes the right thing.

What the assumptions mean for a network:

  • Independent examples. The loss is a sum over examples, and shuffled mini-batches rely on it. Near-duplicates or many rows per user over-weight what they share.
  • Independent outputs given the input. Per-pixel MSE treats every pixel as its own Gaussian, so the optimum is the per-pixel average of all plausible images, which is a blur whenever the answer is uncertain (future frames, super-resolution). Multi-label BCE likewise ignores how labels co-occur. The fixes model the joint distribution: an autoregressive factorization (how language models avoid this), a mixture, or a non-likelihood loss (perceptual, adversarial).
  • Fixed variance. MSE assumes the same noise everywhere. Predicting \(\sigma^2(x)\) as a second output lets the network say “unsure here” and down-weight those examples (see “What the assumptions mean in practice” below).
  • The prior. Weight decay makes training MAP rather than MLE: a Gaussian prior on the weights (see “Regularization is a prior” below). The full objective is a negative log posterior.

Not everything in deep learning is maximum likelihood:

  • Exact likelihood: autoregressive models and normalizing flows. A bound on it: VAEs maximize the ELBO, a lower bound on \(\log p(x)\), and diffusion models train a weighted version of a similar bound.
  • No likelihood at all: GANs. The generator minimizes a divergence that a discriminator estimates (Jensen–Shannon for the original GAN), so it can produce sharp samples but gives no \(p(x)\).
  • Margin and ranking objectives: hinge, triplet and contrastive losses. InfoNCE sits in between: it is the categorical NLL of picking the positive out of the candidates, and a lower bound on mutual information.
  • Reward, not likelihood: RL fine-tuning (REINFORCE, PPO) maximizes expected reward. Supervised fine-tuning of an LLM, by contrast, is plain MLE on the demonstrations.

Where MLE shows its limits in deep nets: the NLL keeps rewarding larger logits on training data, so big networks become overconfident (label smoothing and temperature scaling, section 08); it memorizes noisy labels (also section 08); and forward KL is mode-covering, so a language model spreads some probability onto poor continuations, which is one reason decoding uses top-\(k\) or top-\(p\) truncation.

Where the central limit theorem fits

The CLT doesn't derive any loss; maximum likelihood under an assumed distribution does. The CLT does two other jobs:

  • It motivates Gaussian noise. If a target's noise is the sum of many small, independent effects, the CLT says that sum is roughly Gaussian, which is the case for MSE. (The Gaussian is also the maximum-entropy distribution for a given mean and variance: the least-committal choice.)
  • It describes mini-batch gradients. A mini-batch gradient is an average of \(B\) per-example gradients, so by the CLT it is roughly Gaussian around the full-data gradient, with covariance shrinking as \(1/B\). That is the “gradient noise” behind the linear learning-rate scaling rule and arguments about SGD finding flat minima.

What the optimum predicts

With a flexible enough model, the loss is minimized separately at each \(x\): pick the prediction \(\hat y\) that minimizes the expected loss over \(y \mid x\). For log loss, write \(\pi = P(y = 1 \mid x)\) and let \(q\) be the predicted probability.

In each case below, the first line is the derivative of the expected loss, and setting it to zero gives the second.

Squared error:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial \hat y}\,\mathbb E\big[(y - \hat y)^2\big] &= -2\,\mathbb E[y - \hat y] \\ \hat y^* &= \mathbb E[y \mid x] \end{aligned} \]

Absolute error:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial \hat y}\,\mathbb E\,|y - \hat y| &= P(y < \hat y) - P(y > \hat y) \\ \hat y^* &= \operatorname{median}(y \mid x) \end{aligned} \]

Log loss:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial q}\,\mathbb E\big[\text{log loss}\big] &= -\frac{\pi}{q} + \frac{1 - \pi}{1 - q} \\ q^* &= \pi \end{aligned} \]

MAE's derivative is a vote: each point below \(\hat y\) pulls it down, towards itself, with force 1; each point above pulls it up with force 1; they balance at the median. Log loss is minimized only by the true probability, which makes it a proper scoring rule.

MSE doesn't need Gaussian noise to target the mean. The mean minimizes expected squared error for any distribution. The Gaussian story tells you when MSE is the most efficient choice, what its uncertainty means, and how much outliers will pull.

What the assumptions mean in practice

  • Outliers. Under MSE a residual \(k\) times larger costs \(k^2\) times more and pulls \(k\) times harder, so a handful of bad points can move the whole fit. Heavier-tailed noise models (Laplace, Huber, Student-t) cap that pull.
  • Heteroscedasticity. MSE assumes one \(\sigma\) for every example. If the noise grows with \(x\) (spend grows with income, say), MSE still targets the mean, but the noisy region dominates the gradient and its residuals say nothing reliable about uncertainty. One fix is to predict the variance too, with a second output \(s = \log\sigma^2(x)\) and the full Gaussian NLL:
    \[ \begin{aligned} L &= \frac{(y - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} + \tfrac12\log\sigma^2 \\ &= \tfrac12\,e^{-s}\,(y - \mu)^2 + \tfrac12\,s \end{aligned} \]
    The model can down-weight examples it declares noisy, but pays \(\tfrac12 s\) for each claim Predicting \(s\) rather than \(\sigma\) keeps the variance positive and training stable. PyTorch's GaussianNLLLoss takes the variance itself, so pass \(e^{s}\) (or a softplus output), not \(s\). The other fixes: a log transform, or a GLM whose variance is tied to its mean (Poisson, Gamma, Tweedie).
  • Support. A Gaussian lives on the whole real line, so MSE on counts or prices can predict negative values. A log link (Poisson, Gamma, Tweedie) can't.

Trap: log-transformed targets

Train MSE on \(\log y\), exponentiate, and you predict \(\exp(\mathbb E[\log y])\), the geometric mean. By Jensen's inequality that's below \(\mathbb E[y]\) (under lognormal noise it's the median). If the business sums predictions (revenue, demand), correct it (multiply by \(e^{\sigma^2/2}\) for lognormal noise, or use a smearing estimate), or train a log-link loss such as Poisson, Gamma or Tweedie on the raw target.

Regularization is a prior

Maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation multiplies the likelihood by a prior over the weights, and the prior's negative log becomes a penalty term:

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} &= \arg\max_\theta\; p(\theta)\prod_i p(y_i \mid x_i; \theta) \\ &= \arg\min_\theta\; \sum_i -\log p(y_i \mid x_i; \theta) - \log p(\theta) \end{aligned} \]
  • Gaussian prior \(\theta_j \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma_\theta^2)\) gives \(-\log p(\theta) = \lVert\theta\rVert_2^2 / 2\sigma_\theta^2 + \text{const}\): L2 regularization (weight decay). A tighter prior, smaller \(\sigma_\theta\), is a larger \(\lambda\).
  • Laplace prior \(\theta_j \sim \text{Laplace}(0, b_\theta)\) gives \(-\log p(\theta) = \lVert\theta\rVert_1 / b_\theta + \text{const}\): L1. Its sharp peak at zero is why L1 sets weights exactly to zero.
  • "Loss plus \(\lambda\) times a penalty" is still a negative log-probability, of the weights and data together. \(\lambda\) is relative to the data term's scale, so it must change if you switch between mean and sum reduction (section 12).

02Regression losses

Write the residual as \(r = \hat y - y\). Each loss below is a different answer to one question: how hard should a point pull on the fit as its residual grows?

MSE

\(L = \tfrac12 r^2\) with gradient \(r\). The pull is proportional to the error: fast convergence, a smooth bowl, and a constant second derivative, which Newton-style methods and gradient boosting like. The cost is that the gradient sum is dominated by the largest residuals. Its units are the target's squared, so standardize targets to keep the initial loss and gradients at a sane scale.

MAE

\(L = |r|\) with gradient \(\operatorname{sign}(r)\). Every point pulls with the same force, so outliers can't dominate (the median tolerates up to half the data being corrupted). Three costs:

  • The gradient doesn't shrink near the optimum, so SGD with a fixed learning rate keeps bouncing around it. It needs a decaying learning rate.
  • It's not differentiable at 0, and its second derivative is 0 everywhere else. Second-order gradient boosting can't use it directly; LightGBM's L1 objective re-fits each leaf to the median of its residuals.
  • It predicts the median, which is wrong when predictions get summed.

Huber and smooth L1

\[ \begin{aligned} L_\delta(r) &= \begin{cases} \tfrac12 r^2 & |r| \le \delta \\ \delta\big(|r| - \tfrac12\delta\big) & |r| > \delta \end{cases} \\ \frac{\partial L_\delta}{\partial \hat y} &= \operatorname{clip}(r, -\delta, \delta) \end{aligned} \]
  • Huber is MSE with its gradient clipped at \(\delta\). Quadratic near zero, so the gradient still shrinks as you approach the optimum; linear in the tails, so the pull of any one point is capped. Value and slope are continuous at \(\pm\delta\).
  • \(\delta\) is in target units: roughly the residual size beyond which you stop trusting a point. Set it from the residual spread (a multiple of the median absolute deviation) or tune it. As \(\delta \to 0\) Huber's optimum tends to the median and as \(\delta \to \infty\) to the mean; for symmetric noise all three coincide.
  • Smooth L1 (Fast R-CNN) is Huber divided by its threshold \(\beta\). Its tail slope is \(\pm 1\) whatever \(\beta\) is: L1 with a rounded tip.
    \[ \text{smooth-L1}_\beta(r) = \begin{cases} \tfrac12 r^2/\beta & |r| < \beta \\ |r| - \tfrac12\beta & |r| \ge \beta \end{cases} \]
  • Where it's used. Bounding-box regression in Fast and Faster R-CNN: early on, badly matched proposals produce huge offsets whose L2 gradients would blow up training. DQN: the TD error is a moving, noisy target, and the Huber loss is exactly "clip the TD-error gradient to \([-1, 1]\)".

Log-cosh

\[ \begin{aligned} L(r) &= \log\cosh r \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat y} &= \tanh r \\ \frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial \hat y^2} &= 1 - \tanh^2 r \end{aligned} \]

It behaves like \(\tfrac12 r^2\) for small residuals and like \(|r| - \log 2\) for large ones: a smooth Huber with \(\delta \approx 1\) and no kink. Being twice differentiable everywhere is why it appears with gradient boosting. The catch: for large residuals the second derivative goes to 0, so Newton-style steps \(g/h\) become huge there, and the switch-over scale is fixed at 1 in target units (so rescale the target).

Quantile (pinball) loss

\[ L_\tau(y, \hat y) = \begin{cases} \tau\,(y - \hat y) & y \ge \hat y \\ (1 - \tau)\,(\hat y - y) & y < \hat y \end{cases} \]

Under-predicting (top line) costs \(\tau\) per unit, over-predicting (bottom line) \(1 - \tau\). The gradient with respect to \(\hat y\) is \(-\tau\) or \(1 - \tau\), and its expectation is zero exactly at the \(\tau\)-quantile (\(F\) is the CDF of \(y \mid x\)):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial \hat y}\,\mathbb E\big[L_\tau\big] &= -\tau\,P(y > \hat y) + (1 - \tau)\,P(y < \hat y) \\ &= F(\hat y) - \tau \\ \hat y^* &= F^{-1}(\tau) \end{aligned} \]
  • \(\tau = 0.5\) is half the MAE. It's the likelihood of an asymmetric Laplace distribution.
  • Prediction intervals: train heads at \(\tau = 0.05\) and \(0.95\) (or one multi-output head with the pinball losses summed) for a 90% interval. Also used for P90 delivery times, and for inventory, where the best stock level is the quantile \(\tau = c_{\text{under}} / (c_{\text{under}} + c_{\text{over}})\).
  • Quantile crossing: separately trained quantiles can cross. Sort the outputs, or predict the lowest quantile plus non-negative increments.
  • Coverage: a nominal 90% interval often covers less on new data. Check coverage on a holdout and correct it with conformalized quantile regression (widen the interval by the right quantile of the holdout errors).
  • The gradient has constant size, as with MAE, so the same optimization notes apply.

Poisson and Tweedie: counts and amounts

Poisson for counts, with a log link \(\mu = e^{f}\) so the mean stays positive and features act multiplicatively:

\[ \begin{aligned} -\log p(y \mid x) &= \mu - y\log\mu + \log y! \\ L(f) &= e^{f} - y f \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial f} &= \mu - y \\ \frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial f^2} &= \mu \end{aligned} \]

The second line drops \(\log y!\), which doesn't depend on the model. The gradient is the same "prediction minus target" as cross-entropy, on the log scale. It predicts the mean count and assumes the variance equals the mean. Real counts are usually overdispersed (variance above the mean): the Poisson mean is still consistent, but its uncertainty is too narrow, so use a negative binomial if you need intervals. For rates, add \(\log(\text{exposure})\) to \(f\) as a fixed offset. MSE on counts fails on all three fronts: skew, variance that grows with the mean, and negative predictions.

Tweedie with power \(1 < p < 2\) is a compound Poisson–Gamma distribution: a point mass at exactly zero plus a continuous positive part. It fits zero-inflated amounts such as insurance claims, spend per customer and item-level sales. Its variance is \(\phi\,\mu^{p}\); \(p = 0\) is Gaussian, \(p = 1\) Poisson and \(p = 2\) Gamma. The loss is the negative quasi-likelihood: integrate "residual over variance" from \(y\) to the prediction. With a log link:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu &= e^{f} \\ L(\mu) &= -\int_{y}^{\mu} \frac{y - t}{t^{p}}\,dt \\ &= -\frac{y\,\mu^{1-p}}{1 - p} + \frac{\mu^{2-p}}{2 - p} + \text{const} \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mu} &= \frac{\mu - y}{\mu^{p}} \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial f} &= \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mu}\cdot\mu \\ &= \mu^{1-p}\,(\mu - y) \end{aligned} \]

Again "\(\mu - y\)", now multiplied by \(\mu^{1-p}\), which shrinks for large predictions. Big-ticket rows have big variance, so they're trusted less: the variance assumption shows up as a per-example weight. The power \(p\) is a hyperparameter. Don't pick it by validation deviance computed at each candidate \(p\), since those deviances are on different scales; compare candidates on one fixed metric (deviance at a single fixed power, MAE, or Gini and lift), or by profile likelihood.

03Sigmoid and binary cross-entropy

The model outputs a logit \(z\) (the log-odds), turned into a probability by the sigmoid:

\[ \begin{aligned} p &= \sigma(z) \\ \sigma(z) &= \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} \\ \sigma'(z) &= p\,(1 - p) \end{aligned} \]

Written on the logit, binary cross-entropy simplifies to softplus minus a linear term:

\[ \begin{aligned} L &= -\big[y\log p + (1 - y)\log(1 - p)\big] \\ &= y\log\big(1 + e^{-z}\big) + (1 - y)\log\big(1 + e^{z}\big) \\ &= \log\big(1 + e^{z}\big) - y\,z \end{aligned} \]

The middle line uses the first two identities below, and the last line uses the third. This logit form is what frameworks actually compute (section 05).

\[ \begin{aligned} \log\sigma(z) &= -\log\big(1 + e^{-z}\big) \\ 1 - \sigma(z) &= \sigma(-z) \\ \log\big(1 + e^{-z}\big) &= \log\big(1 + e^{z}\big) - z \end{aligned} \]

The gradient is \(p - y\)

Through the probability, by the chain rule:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial p} &= -\frac{y}{p} + \frac{1 - y}{1 - p} \\ &= \frac{p - y}{p(1 - p)} \\ \frac{\partial p}{\partial z} &= p(1 - p) \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial z} &= \frac{p - y}{p(1 - p)}\cdot p(1 - p) \\ &= p - y \end{aligned} \]

Or in one step from the logit form: the derivative of \(\log(1 + e^{z})\) is \(\sigma(z)\), and of \(y z\) is \(y\).

  • Why it's such a good gradient: the sigmoid's slope \(p(1 - p)\), which vanishes when it saturates, cancels exactly against the \(1/(p(1 - p))\) from the log. What's left is bounded in \((-1, 1)\), largest when the model is confidently wrong, and zero only when it's confidently right.
  • Convex: the second derivative is \(p(1 - p) \ge 0\). For logistic regression \(z = w^\top x\) is linear in \(w\), so the loss is convex in \(w\), with gradient \((p - y)\,x\) and Hessian \(X^\top \operatorname{diag}\big(p(1 - p)\big) X\), which Newton's method uses (as iteratively reweighted least squares, IRLS). On separable data the weights run off to infinity, so regularize.
  • Labels as \(\pm 1\): the same loss is \(\log(1 + e^{-yz})\), a function of the margin \(m = yz\) only. That form lines it up against hinge and exponential losses (section 09).

Why not MSE for classification

Put squared error on the probability, \(L = \tfrac12(p - y)^2\) with \(p = \sigma(z)\):

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial z} = (p - y)\,p\,(1 - p) \]
  • Saturation. The extra factor \(p(1 - p)\) goes to zero at both ends. When \(y = 1\) and \(z \to -\infty\) (confidently wrong), \(p \to 0\) and so does the gradient: the worst examples barely learn. Cross-entropy's gradient there is close to \(-1\).
  • Not convex in the logit. For \(y = 1\) the curvature changes sign at \(p = 1/3\), so the loss has flat, concave regions; for logistic regression this can mean plateaus and local minima in \(w\). Cross-entropy is convex.
    \[ \frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial z^2} = -\,p\,(1 - p)^2\,(1 - 3p) \]
  • Bounded penalty. The most squared error can charge for a confident mistake is a constant; cross-entropy charges \(-\log p\), which is unbounded. That's the likelihood saying a confident mistake is very costly.
  • Wrong likelihood. MSE is a Gaussian likelihood, and a 0/1 label is not Gaussian.

The Brier score (squared error on probabilities) is still a proper scoring rule and a good metric for calibration. The problem is training through the sigmoid with it, not the score itself.

04Softmax and cross-entropy

For \(K\) classes the model outputs logits \(z \in \mathbb R^K\) and

\[ p_k = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} e^{z_j}} \]
  • Every \(p_k\) is positive and they sum to 1. Adding the same constant to every logit changes nothing, so only differences between logits matter: the logits have one redundant direction.
  • Why the exponential: it makes the logits log-probabilities up to a shared constant (the categorical distribution in exponential-family form), so the log in the loss undoes it and the log-likelihood is linear in \(z\) minus a log-sum-exp.
  • With two classes, \(p_1 = \sigma(z_1 - z_0)\): softmax is the sigmoid of the logit difference.

Cross-entropy with true class \(c\):

\[ \begin{aligned} L &= -\sum_{k} y_k \log p_k \\ &= -\log p_c \\ &= -z_c + \log\sum_{j} e^{z_j} \end{aligned} \]

Push the true logit up and push the log-sum-exp, a soft maximum over all logits, down.

The softmax Jacobian

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial p_i}{\partial z_j} &= p_i\,(\delta_{ij} - p_j) \\ J &= \operatorname{diag}(p) - p\,p^\top \end{aligned} \]
  • The diagonal is \(p_i(1 - p_i)\): raising your own logit raises your probability, with a sigmoid-like slope. Off the diagonal, \(-p_i p_j\): raising someone else's logit lowers yours.
  • \(J\) is \(K \times K\), symmetric, positive semi-definite and of rank \(K - 1\). \(J\mathbf 1 = 0\) is the shift invariance: moving all logits together does nothing.
  • It saturates like a sigmoid: when one \(p_k\) is near 1, the whole of \(J\) is near 0. Hence the same danger as with MSE if you put the wrong loss on top.

Deriving \(\partial L / \partial z = p - y\)

For any target vector \(y\) with \(\sum_i y_i = 1\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_j} &= -\sum_i \frac{y_i}{p_i}\,\frac{\partial p_i}{\partial z_j} \\ &= -\sum_i \frac{y_i}{p_i}\,p_i\,(\delta_{ij} - p_j) \\ &= -y_j + p_j\sum_i y_i \\ &= p_j - y_j \end{aligned} \]

Or from the log-sum-exp form, since the gradient of \(\log\sum_j e^{z_j}\) is the softmax itself:

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial z_j}\Big(-z_c + \log\sum_k e^{z_k}\Big) = -\mathbf 1[j = c] + p_j \]
  • Why it's \(p - y\): the \(1/p_i\) from the log cancels the \(p_i\) in the Jacobian, just as in the binary case. Softmax is the canonical link of the categorical distribution, and every GLM with its canonical link has gradient "prediction minus target": Gaussian with identity, Bernoulli with logit, Poisson with log.
  • The gradient sums to zero over classes. The true logit goes up by \(1 - p_c\); each wrong logit goes down by the probability it took. Confident wrong classes are pushed hardest.
  • Soft targets work unchanged (label smoothing, distillation, mixup): the derivation only used \(\sum_i y_i = 1\).
  • Convex in the logits: the Hessian with respect to \(z\) is \(J\), which is positive semi-definite. In a deep network the loss is not convex in the weights.

Temperature

Divide the logits by a temperature \(T\) before the softmax. As \(T \to 0\) it becomes a one-hot argmax; as \(T \to \infty\) it becomes uniform; the argmax never changes. The gradient picks up a factor \(1/T\):

\[ \begin{aligned} p &= \operatorname{softmax}(z / T) \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial z} &= \frac{p - y}{T} \end{aligned} \]

Temperature appears in calibration, distillation, sampling from language models and contrastive losses, all below.

Trap: what if you forget the softmax, or apply it twice?

  • Probabilities into CrossEntropyLoss, which applies log-softmax itself: a double softmax. The "logits" now live in \([0, 1]\), so the best possible probability for the true class is \(e/(e + K - 1)\). The loss has a floor far above 0, and the gradient passes through two softmax Jacobians, so it's tiny. Training crawls; the argmax may still look fine, but the probabilities are meaningless.
  • Raw logits into NLLLoss (forgot the log-softmax): the loss is just \(-z_c\), unbounded below. The model drives the true logit to infinity and the loss goes negative and diverges.
  • Softmax, then log, then NLL: correct on paper, but a probability that underflows to 0 gives \(\log 0 = -\infty\) and then NaN. Use log_softmax.

05Numerical stability

\(e^{z}\) overflows float32 for \(z\) above about 89 and float16 above about 11. In the other direction, a probability that underflows to exactly 0 makes \(\log p = -\infty\), and the backward pass then produces NaN (\(\infty - \infty\) or \(0 \cdot \infty\)). Both are fixed by never forming the probability you're about to take the log of.

Log-sum-exp

With \(m = \max_j z_j\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \log\sum_j e^{z_j} &= m + \log\sum_j e^{z_j - m} \\ \log p_k &= z_k - m - \log\sum_j e^{z_j - m} \end{aligned} \]

Every exponent is now at most 0, so nothing overflows. The largest term is \(e^0 = 1\), so the sum is at least 1 and its log is never \(-\infty\). Subtracting \(m\) is free because softmax ignores shifts. And \(\log p_k\) is computed directly, never through a probability that could round to zero.

Binary cross-entropy from a logit

\[ \begin{aligned} \log\big(1 + e^{z}\big) &= \max(z, 0) + \log\big(1 + e^{-|z|}\big) \\ L &= \max(z, 0) - y\,z + \log\big(1 + e^{-|z|}\big) \end{aligned} \]

The same idea: \(e^{-|z|} \le 1\) always. Use log1p for accuracy when \(e^{-|z|}\) is tiny. Compute the sigmoid itself as \(1/(1 + e^{-z})\) when \(z \ge 0\) and \(e^{z}/(1 + e^{z})\) when \(z < 0\), so you never exponentiate a large positive number.

Why frameworks take logits

  • CrossEntropyLoss is log-softmax plus NLLLoss, fused; BCEWithLogitsLoss is sigmoid plus BCE, fused. Both use the forms above, and their backward pass is exactly \(p - y\), with no division by \(p(1 - p)\) that could become \(0/0\).
  • So the model's last layer outputs raw logits, with no softmax or sigmoid inside the model. Apply softmax only when you need probabilities to report.
  • Under mixed precision, PyTorch's autocast runs these losses in float32, and refuses BCELoss on probabilities outright, pointing you to BCEWithLogitsLoss.

The clamping trap

A common "fix" when computing BCE from probabilities is \(p \leftarrow \operatorname{clamp}(p, \varepsilon, 1 - \varepsilon)\) before the log. But clamp has zero derivative outside \([\varepsilon, 1 - \varepsilon]\). A confidently wrong example (\(y = 1\), \(p < \varepsilon\)) gets clamped, and its gradient becomes exactly zero: the worst examples silently stop learning, the opposite of what cross-entropy is for. Without clamping it's no better: in float32, \(\sigma(z)\) rounds to exactly 1 for \(z\) above about 17, so \(\log(1 - p) = -\infty\).

Adding \(\varepsilon\) inside the log keeps a gradient with respect to \(p\), but the gradient still passes through the sigmoid's slope. For \(y = 1\):

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\Big[-\log(p + \varepsilon)\Big] = -\frac{p\,(1 - p)}{p + \varepsilon} \]

This goes to 0 once \(p \ll \varepsilon\), whereas the logit form gives \(p - 1 \approx -1\). The rule: if you have logits, never compute a loss from probabilities.

06Entropy, cross-entropy and KL

Entropy is the average surprise, where the surprise of an outcome is \(-\log\) of its probability. It's 0 for a certain outcome and at most \(\log K\), reached by the uniform distribution. In bits (\(\log_2\)) it's the shortest possible average code length.

\[ H(p) = -\sum_k p_k \log p_k \]

Cross-entropy is the average surprise when outcomes come from \(p\) but you score them with your model \(q\). It splits into the entropy plus the extra cost of using the wrong distribution:

\[ \begin{aligned} H(p, q) &= -\sum_k p_k \log q_k \\ &= -\sum_k p_k \log p_k + \sum_k p_k \log\frac{p_k}{q_k} \\ &= H(p) + \mathrm{KL}(p \Vert q) \end{aligned} \]

KL divergence is never negative, and it's zero only when \(q = p\) (Gibbs' inequality). The proof is Jensen's inequality, since \(\log\) is concave:

\[ \begin{aligned} -\mathrm{KL}(p \Vert q) &= \sum_k p_k \log\frac{q_k}{p_k} \\ &\le \log\sum_k p_k\,\frac{q_k}{p_k} \\ &= \log\sum_{k:\,p_k > 0} q_k \\ &\le 0 \end{aligned} \]

The sums run over the classes where \(p_k > 0\), so the last sum of \(q_k\) is at most 1.

So the cross-entropy is never below the entropy: no model beats the true distribution.

Why cross-entropy training is maximum likelihood

The average negative log-likelihood over the data is the cross-entropy between the empirical distribution \(\hat p\) and the model:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} -\log q_\theta(y_i \mid x_i) &= H(\hat p, q_\theta) \\ &= H(\hat p) + \mathrm{KL}(\hat p \Vert q_\theta) \end{aligned} \]

\(H(\hat p)\) doesn't depend on \(\theta\), so minimizing cross-entropy, maximizing likelihood and minimizing the forward KL from data to model are the same thing. With one-hot targets each example's entropy is 0, so its cross-entropy equals its KL and the per-example floor is 0. With soft targets (label smoothing, distillation) the floor is the target's entropy, so the loss never reaches 0; that's why distillation code often reports KL instead.

Forward vs reverse KL

  • Forward, \(\mathrm{KL}(p \Vert q) = \mathbb E_p[\log(p/q)]\): the average is over the data. Wherever the data has mass and \(q\) is near 0, the log ratio blows up, so \(q\) must cover every region the data visits. It's mass-covering (mean-seeking). This is maximum likelihood and cross-entropy training.
  • Reverse, \(\mathrm{KL}(q \Vert p) = \mathbb E_q[\log(q/p)]\): the average is over the model's own samples. \(q\) is punished for putting mass where \(p\) is small, but not for missing parts of \(p\). It's mode-seeking (zero-forcing). This is variational inference (maximizing the evidence lower bound, the ELBO; section 11), and the KL penalty that keeps an RLHF policy close to its reference model, \(\mathrm{KL}(\pi \Vert \pi_{\text{ref}})\), runs in this direction.
  • KL is asymmetric and has no triangle inequality, so it's not a distance. Jensen–Shannon divergence is a symmetric, bounded alternative (the original GAN objective).
  • In generative models, forward-KL training tends to over-cover (blurry or generic samples), while mode-seeking objectives give sharp but less diverse samples.

Perplexity

\[ \mathrm{PPL} = \exp\Big(-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log q\big(w_t \mid w_{<t}\big)\Big) \]

The exponential of the mean per-token cross-entropy (natural log): the effective number of equally likely choices at each step. A uniform model over a vocabulary of \(V\) tokens has perplexity \(V\). It's per token, so it's only comparable between models with the same tokenizer; bits per byte or character avoid that.

Distillation with temperature

A student with logits \(z\) learns from a teacher with logits \(v\). Both are softened with the same temperature \(T > 1\):

\[ \begin{aligned} p^{T} &= \operatorname{softmax}(v / T) \\ q^{T} &= \operatorname{softmax}(z / T) \\ L &= \alpha\,\mathrm{CE}\big(y, \operatorname{softmax}(z)\big) \\ &\quad + (1 - \alpha)\,T^2\,\mathrm{KL}\big(p^{T} \Vert q^{T}\big) \end{aligned} \]
  • Why soften: at \(T = 1\) a good teacher puts almost all its mass on the right class, so its output carries little more than the hard label. The useful information, which wrong classes look like the right one (the "dark knowledge"), is in the relative sizes of the tiny probabilities. Dividing by \(T\) raises them enough to carry gradient.
  • The gradient of the soft term with respect to a student logit is the softmax gradient with an extra \(1/T\). For large \(T\), use \(e^{z_i/T} \approx 1 + z_i/T\); with zero-mean logits the softmax denominator is about \(K\):
    \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\,\mathrm{KL}(p^{T} \Vert q^{T})}{\partial z_i} &= \frac{1}{T}\big(q^{T}_i - p^{T}_i\big) \\ q^{T}_i &\approx \frac{1 + z_i/T}{K} \\ p^{T}_i &\approx \frac{1 + v_i/T}{K} \\ \frac{\partial\,\mathrm{KL}}{\partial z_i} &\approx \frac{1}{K T^2}\,(z_i - v_i) \end{aligned} \]
    So at high temperature, distillation becomes matching logits with squared error.
  • Why the \(T^2\): the soft term's gradients shrink like \(1/T^2\). Multiplying by \(T^2\) keeps the balance between the two terms fixed when you change \(T\).
  • The student uses \(T = 1\) at inference. A teacher trained with label smoothing was reported to make a worse teacher, because smoothing erases the similarity structure the student is meant to learn, though later work (Shen et al., 2021) disputed how general that is.

07Class imbalance and focal loss

With a proper loss like cross-entropy, imbalance doesn't bias the probabilities: the model learns that the rare class is rare, which is correct. What goes wrong is different:

  • the gradient is dominated by the many easy majority examples, so the minority boundary gets few, small updates;
  • a neural network can settle early on predicting the prior for everything;
  • the default 0.5 threshold is wrong for the decision you actually need.

Many "imbalance problems" are threshold problems. Predict positive when the expected cost of a miss exceeds that of a false alarm, which with calibrated probabilities means \(p > C_{FP}/(C_{FP} + C_{FN})\).

Class weights and pos_weight

Each example's loss, averaged over the batch as usual:

\[ \ell_i = -\,w_1\,y_i\log p_i - w_0\,(1 - y_i)\log(1 - p_i) \]
  • BCEWithLogitsLoss(pos_weight=w) multiplies only the positive term, typically with \(w = n_{\text{neg}}/n_{\text{pos}}\). CrossEntropyLoss(weight=...) takes one weight per class, and with mean reduction it divides by the sum of the weights of the targets in the batch, not by \(N\).
  • What it does to the optimum. Take a group of examples the model can't tell apart, with \(n_1\) positives and \(n_0\) negatives, and let it pick one \(p\) for all of them:
    \[ \begin{aligned} 0 &= \frac{\partial}{\partial p}\Big[-w\,n_1\log p - n_0\log(1 - p)\Big] \\ &= -\frac{w\,n_1}{p} + \frac{n_0}{1 - p} \\ \frac{p}{1 - p} &= w\cdot\frac{n_1}{n_0} \end{aligned} \]
    The predicted odds are multiplied by \(w\). So weighting is a threshold shift in disguise: thresholding the weighted model at 0.5 is (for a well-specified model) thresholding the unweighted one at \(1/(1 + w)\). The ranking, and so the AUC, barely changes; the probabilities are inflated.
  • In gradient terms, each positive's pull \(p - 1\) is multiplied by \(w_1\).

Resampling

  • Oversampling the minority \(k\) times matches weight \(k\) in expectation, but exact copies are easy to memorize for high-capacity models. Augmentation, or SMOTE-style interpolation, adds variety (SMOTE rarely beats simple weights with boosted trees on tabular data).
  • Undersampling the majority, keeping a fraction \(\rho\) of the negatives and every positive, is cheaper and throws information away. Negatives become \(\rho\) times rarer in training, so the odds the model learns are multiplied by \(1/\rho\).
  • Undoing the shift (the prior correction): the learned log-odds are too high by \(\log(1/\rho)\), so add \(\log\rho\) back. With \(p_s\) the model's output, and \(\rho = 1/w\) for class weights:
    \[ \operatorname{logit} p = \operatorname{logit} p_s + \log\rho \]
  • Weights vs resampling under SGD: the same expected gradient, with different variance. A few rare examples carrying large weights make the gradient spiky (a batch that happens to contain one positive takes a huge step). Balanced sampling shows the minority more often with normal-sized steps, which is usually steadier for neural networks. For trees and full-batch models, weights are simplest.
  • Logit adjustment for long-tailed multi-class problems: instead of reweighting, add the log class prior \(\log\pi_y\), times a scale \(\tau\) (usually 1), to the logits inside the loss, or subtract \(\tau\log\pi\) from the logits at prediction time. This targets balanced accuracy while keeping the loss a plain cross-entropy:
    \[ L = -\log\frac{e^{z_y + \tau\log\pi_y}}{\sum_j e^{z_j + \tau\log\pi_j}} \]

Focal loss

Let \(p_t\) be the probability the model gives to the true class (\(p\) if \(y = 1\), \(1 - p\) if \(y = 0\)), and \(\alpha_t\) a class weight defined the same way (\(\alpha\) for positives, \(1 - \alpha\) for negatives):

\[ \mathrm{FL}(p_t) = -\,\alpha_t\,(1 - p_t)^{\gamma}\,\log p_t \]
  • What it down-weights: well-classified examples, whatever their class. The factor \((1 - p_t)^\gamma\) goes to 0 as \(p_t \to 1\), so an example the model already gets right confidently contributes almost nothing, while hard and misclassified examples keep nearly their full cross-entropy. \(\gamma = 0\) is plain cross-entropy; \(\gamma = 2\) is the usual default.
  • Why it was invented (RetinaNet, one-stage object detection): about 100,000 anchor boxes per image, almost all easy background. Each has a small loss, but together they swamp the loss and its gradient. Two-stage detectors avoid this by sampling proposals; focal loss does it inside the loss.
  • \(\alpha\) is an ordinary class weight. RetinaNet's best setting with \(\gamma = 2\) was \(\alpha = 0.25\) for the rare foreground, i.e. less weight on the rare class, because \(\gamma\) had already removed most of the easy negatives' loss. The two interact, so tune them together.
  • The gradient with respect to the true-class logit \(z_t\) (\(z\) for positives, \(-z\) for negatives), using \(\partial p_t/\partial z_t = p_t(1 - p_t)\):
    \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\,\mathrm{FL}}{\partial p_t} &= \alpha_t\Big[\gamma\,(1 - p_t)^{\gamma - 1}\log p_t - \frac{(1 - p_t)^{\gamma}}{p_t}\Big] \\ \frac{\partial\,\mathrm{FL}}{\partial z_t} &= \frac{\partial\,\mathrm{FL}}{\partial p_t}\cdot p_t(1 - p_t) \\ &= -\,\alpha_t\,(1 - p_t)^{\gamma}\,\Big[(1 - p_t) - \gamma\,p_t\log p_t\Big] \end{aligned} \]
    With \(\gamma = 0\) and \(\alpha_t = 1\) this is \(p_t - 1\), the cross-entropy gradient. The factor shrinks the gradient of easy examples, not just their loss.
  • Normalization: RetinaNet divides the summed loss by the number of positive anchors, not by all anchors, since the easy negatives now contribute almost nothing.
  • Calibration: focal loss is not a proper scoring rule. Without \(\alpha\) (or with \(\alpha = 0.5\)), its optimum lies between the true probability and 0.5, so on paper it's under-confident; any other \(\alpha\) also rescales the odds, as a class weight does. Deep networks trained with cross-entropy are often over-confident, so in practice focal loss can even improve calibration. Either way, measure calibration before using its outputs as probabilities.
  • When to use it: huge numbers of easy negatives (dense detection, segmentation of small objects, extreme-imbalance neural nets). For moderate imbalance, weights, or nothing plus a tuned threshold.

Default recipe

Train unweighted with cross-entropy, check calibration, and set the threshold from costs or capacity. Reach for weights, resampling or focal loss when the model collapses to the majority class, when you need to subsample for speed (then apply the prior correction), or when a fix measurably improves the validation metric you care about. Judge with PR-AUC or cost, not accuracy.

08Label smoothing and calibration

Label smoothing

Replace the one-hot target with a mix of it and the uniform distribution \(u\):

\[ y^{\text{LS}} = (1 - \varepsilon)\,y + \frac{\varepsilon}{K}\,\mathbf 1 \]

The loss splits into the usual cross-entropy plus a pull towards uniform:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{CE}\big(y^{\text{LS}}, q\big) &= (1 - \varepsilon)\,\mathrm{CE}(y, q) + \varepsilon\,\mathrm{CE}(u, q) \\ \mathrm{CE}(u, q) &= \mathrm{KL}(u \Vert q) + \log K \end{aligned} \]
  • What it does to the logits. With one-hot targets the loss is zero only at \(p_c = 1\), which needs \(z_c - z_k \to \infty\): logits and weights grow without bound and the model becomes over-confident. With smoothing, the gradient is \(q - y^{\text{LS}}\) and the optimum is \(q = y^{\text{LS}}\), which fixes a finite gap between the true logit and each other logit: Since \(q_c/q_k = e^{z_c - z_k}\), the gap is the log of the ratio of the two smoothed targets:
    \[ \begin{aligned} z_c - z_k &= \log\frac{y^{\text{LS}}_c}{y^{\text{LS}}_k} \\ &= \log\frac{1 - \varepsilon + \varepsilon/K}{\varepsilon/K} \\ &= \log\Big(1 + \frac{K(1 - \varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}\Big) \end{aligned} \]
  • What it does to representations: the penultimate-layer features of each class form tight, evenly spaced clusters. This often improves generalization and calibration. \(\varepsilon = 0.1\) is the common default (Inception, the original Transformer).
  • When it hurts:
    • distillation, since a smoothed teacher has lost the information about which wrong classes are similar;
    • when downstream use needs real confidence or the ranking among the wrong classes (uncertainty estimates, top-k retrieval);
    • when thresholds near 1 matter: the true class's probability is capped at \(1 - \varepsilon + \varepsilon/K\);
    • with heavy imbalance, where the uniform mass inflates rare classes.

Noisy labels

Cross-entropy is unforgiving of wrong labels. A mislabelled example gets a tiny \(p_t\) for its (wrong) label, so its loss \(-\log p_t\) is huge and its gradient as large as any example's. A big network eventually memorizes it (deep networks can fit even random labels); clean patterns tend to be learned first, so early stopping helps. The robust alternatives limit how hard a low-confidence example can pull:

  • MAE on probabilities, \(2(1 - p_t)\), is symmetric: its sum over all \(K\) possible labels is a constant, which makes it provably robust to uniform label noise. The price is slow, under-fitting training: its gradient is the cross-entropy gradient times \(2p_t\), so hard examples barely move it.
  • Generalized cross-entropy interpolates between the two, with \(0 < \kappa \le 1\). As \(\kappa \to 0\) it becomes \(-\log p_t\); at \(\kappa = 1\) it's \(1 - p_t\). Its gradient is the cross-entropy gradient times \(p_t^{\kappa}\), so low-confidence, possibly mislabelled examples are down-weighted.
    \[ L_\kappa = \frac{1 - p_t^{\kappa}}{\kappa} \]
  • Bootstrapping blends the given label with the model's own prediction, \(\beta y + (1 - \beta)\hat p\) (or with its argmax), so a confident model can partly overrule a wrong label. Label smoothing is the same blend with the uniform distribution instead of the model, which is why it also buys some robustness to noise.
  • Other common fixes: train only on the small-loss examples (co-teaching), or estimate the label-noise transition matrix and correct the loss with it.

Calibration

A model is calibrated when \(P(y = 1 \mid \hat p = p) = p\): of the cases it scores at 0.8, 80% are positive. A reliability diagram bins predictions by confidence and plots each bin's accuracy against its mean confidence; perfect calibration is the diagonal. The expected calibration error is the weighted average gap:

\[ \mathrm{ECE} = \sum_{b=1}^{M} \frac{|B_b|}{N}\,\Big|\operatorname{acc}(B_b) - \operatorname{conf}(B_b)\Big| \]
  • For multi-class it's usually computed on the top-1 confidence. It depends on the binning (equal-width or equal-count), is biased when bins are small, and can be gamed: always predicting the base rate is perfectly calibrated and useless. Report it next to log loss or the Brier score, which measure calibration and sharpness together.
  • Why modern networks are over-confident: once the training set is classified correctly, cross-entropy keeps falling by growing the logits. The validation NLL starts overfitting before the accuracy does. Depth, width, BatchNorm and less weight decay all made it worse (Guo et al., 2017).
  • Proper scoring rules (log loss, Brier) are minimized in expectation only by the true probability, so they reward honest probabilities. Accuracy, hinge and focal loss are not proper.

Temperature scaling

After training, freeze the network and fit one scalar \(T > 0\) on held-out data by minimizing the NLL of the rescaled logits:

\[ T^* = \arg\min_{T} \; -\sum_{i \in \text{val}} \log\operatorname{softmax}\big(z_i / T\big)_{y_i} \]
  • Every logit is divided by the same \(T\), so the argmax, and hence accuracy, is unchanged. \(T > 1\) softens an over-confident model.
  • One parameter can barely overfit the validation set, and it works remarkably well for neural networks.
  • Relatives: Platt scaling (binary, \(\sigma(a z + b)\)); vector and matrix scaling (per-class, more parameters); isotonic regression (non-parametric and monotone, needs more data, can create ties).
  • Calibration is fitted to one distribution; recheck it after any shift. And if the model was trained with weights or resampling, apply the prior correction first (or let the calibrator absorb it).

Hinge loss

  • Once a point is beyond the margin (\(m \ge 1\)) its loss and gradient are exactly zero, so it stops influencing the model. The SVM solution depends only on the points on or inside the margin, the support vectors. The logistic loss keeps pushing every point a little.
  • Its population minimizer is \(\operatorname{sign}(2p - 1)\): it estimates the best decision, not the probability. That's why SVM scores aren't probabilities and need Platt scaling.
  • Hinge plus \(\lambda\lVert w\rVert^2\) is the soft-margin SVM; the margin width is \(2/\lVert w\rVert\).
  • Multi-class version (Weston–Watkins): the true class must beat every other class by a margin \(\Delta\):
    \[ L = \sum_{j \ne c} \max\big(0,\ \Delta + z_j - z_c\big) \]

Ranking: pointwise, pairwise, listwise

Ranking is usually judged by NDCG: a gain for each item, discounted by the log of its rank and normalized by the best possible ordering's score (IDCG), so it lies in \([0, 1]\). With \(\text{rel}_i\) the relevance of the item at rank \(i\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{DCG@}k &= \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{2^{\text{rel}_i} - 1}{\log_2(1 + i)} \\ \mathrm{NDCG@}k &= \frac{\mathrm{DCG@}k}{\mathrm{IDCG@}k} \end{aligned} \]
  • Pointwise: predict each item's relevance on its own, with MSE or BCE. Simple, but it ignores that only the order within a query matters.
  • Pairwise (RankNet): for a pair where item \(i\) should rank above \(j\), model \(P(i \succ j) = \sigma(s_i - s_j)\) and train with BCE:
    \[ \begin{aligned} L_{ij} &= \log\Big(1 + e^{-(s_i - s_j)}\Big) \\ \frac{\partial L_{ij}}{\partial s_i} &= -\,\sigma\big(-(s_i - s_j)\big) \\ \frac{\partial L_{ij}}{\partial s_j} &= +\,\sigma\big(-(s_i - s_j)\big) \end{aligned} \]
    The same loss with a sampled negative item is BPR (Bayesian personalized ranking), the standard implicit-feedback recommender loss. Its weakness: a mis-ordered pair costs the same at rank 50 as at rank 1, but users only look at the top.
  • Reward models and DPO use the same pairwise logistic loss. An RLHF reward model uses the Bradley–Terry model, \(P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\big(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)\big)\) for a preferred response \(y_w\) and a rejected one \(y_l\), and minimizes \(-\log\sigma(r_w - r_l)\): RankNet on (chosen, rejected) pairs. Direct preference optimization (DPO) drops the reward model and uses the policy's log-ratio to a frozen reference model as an implicit reward:
    \[ \begin{aligned} \hat r(x, y) &= \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} \\ L_{\text{DPO}} &= -\log\sigma\big(\hat r(x, y_w) - \hat r(x, y_l)\big) \end{aligned} \]
    Its gradient raises the chosen response's log-probability and lowers the rejected one's, weighted by \(\sigma(\hat r_l - \hat r_w)\): by how wrongly the implicit reward orders the pair now. \(\beta\) plays the role of the KL penalty's strength; a smaller \(\beta\) lets the policy drift further from the reference.
  • LambdaRank skips writing a loss and defines the gradient directly: take RankNet's pair gradient and scale it by how much NDCG would change if the two items swapped places. A document's total gradient is the sum of its \(\lambda\)s over all its pairs. Pairs near the top get large gradients; swaps deep in the list hardly count. LambdaMART is these gradients fed to gradient-boosted trees (lambdarank in LightGBM; rank:ndcg in XGBoost, which also has rank:pairwise and rank:map).
    \[ |\lambda_{ij}| = \sigma\big(-(s_i - s_j)\big)\,\big|\Delta\mathrm{NDCG}_{ij}\big| \]
  • Listwise: score the whole list at once. ListNet uses cross-entropy between the softmax of the true relevances and the softmax of the scores; ListMLE maximizes the likelihood of the correct order under a Plackett–Luce model. Softmax cross-entropy over a list with one relevant item and sampled negatives is the sampled softmax, which is InfoNCE (next section). When negatives are sampled (in-batch, or by popularity) rather than drawn from the whole catalogue, frequently sampled items get pushed down too often and the softmax is biased against popular items. The logQ correction subtracts \(\log Q(j)\), the log-probability that item \(j\) is sampled, from each candidate's logit before the softmax.
  • NDCG itself depends on a sort, so it's piecewise constant in the scores and its gradient is zero almost everywhere. That's why all of the above exist.

10Metric learning and contrastive losses

The goal is an embedding \(f(x)\) where similar items are close and dissimilar ones far apart: retrieval, face verification, recommendation, self-supervised pretraining, CLIP. Embeddings are usually L2-normalized. Then the similarity of two embeddings \(e_1, e_2\) is the cosine \(s = e_1^\top e_2 \in [-1, 1]\), their squared distance is \(2 - 2s\), and the trivial win of scaling every embedding up is gone.

Contrastive (pairwise) loss

For a pair at distance \(d\), labelled \(y = 1\) (similar) or \(y = 0\) (dissimilar), with margin \(m\):

\[ L = y\,d^2 + (1 - y)\,\max(0,\ m - d)^2 \]

Positive pairs are always pulled together. Negative pairs are pushed apart only until they're \(m\) apart; beyond that they're far enough and get no gradient. Without the margin, the negative term would push everything to infinity. Its weakness is one absolute threshold for every pair.

Triplet loss

An anchor \(a\), a positive \(p\) (same identity) and a negative \(n\). The negative must be farther than the positive by at least \(m\), a relative constraint (FaceNet):

\[ L = \max\big(0,\ d(a, p) - d(a, n) + m\big) \]

Triplets fall into three kinds by where the negative sits:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{easy:}\quad & d(a, n) > d(a, p) + m \\ \text{semi-hard:}\quad & d(a, p) < d(a, n) < d(a, p) + m \\ \text{hard:}\quad & d(a, n) < d(a, p) \end{aligned} \]

Easy triplets have zero loss and teach nothing. Semi-hard ones are in the right order but inside the margin. Hard ones are in the wrong order.

  • The margin stops the model from being satisfied with a negative that's only barely farther away, and with normalized embeddings it sets the scale of the separation.
  • Mining matters: most random triplets are easy and contribute nothing. Mine negatives inside each batch (online), using batches built with several examples per identity so positives exist.
  • The hardest negatives can hurt: early in training they push the model towards a collapsed solution (every embedding at one point gives a constant loss of \(m\)), and the very hardest are often label noise. FaceNet used semi-hard negatives; "batch-hard" with a soft margin is a common later variant.

InfoNCE and NT-Xent

A query \(q\) (the anchor) has one positive key \(k^{+}\) among \(N\) candidate keys \(k_j\), scored by similarity \(s\) and a temperature \(\tau\). It's softmax cross-entropy where the "classes" are the candidates and the label is the positive:

\[ L = -\log\frac{\exp\big(s(q, k^{+})/\tau\big)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp\big(s(q, k_j)/\tau\big)} \]
  • In-batch negatives: with \(B\) pairs \((q_i, k_i)\), the labels are \(0, \dots, B - 1\) and the loss is cross-entropy on the rows of \(S\). CLIP averages the row loss (image to text) and the column loss (text to image). In recommendation, popular items show up as in-batch negatives far more often than rare ones, so apply the logQ correction (section 09).
  • NT-Xent (SimCLR): two augmented views of each of \(B\) images give \(2B\) embeddings. Each one's positive is its other view; the other \(2B - 2\) are negatives. The self-similarity on the diagonal is masked out.
  • SigLIP replaces the row softmax with an independent sigmoid BCE on every cell of the \(B \times B\) matrix (diagonal positive, the rest negative), with a learned temperature and bias. Each pair's loss no longer depends on the rest of the batch, which makes very large batches cheaper to shard and works well at small batch sizes too.

Why the temperature matters

As with any cross-entropy, the gradient with respect to each similarity is a softmax weight minus the label, scaled by \(1/\tau\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial s_j} &= \frac{1}{\tau}\Big(\pi_j - \mathbf 1[j = +]\Big) \\ \pi &= \operatorname{softmax}(s / \tau) \end{aligned} \]

So each negative is pushed away in proportion to its softmax weight \(\pi_j\), and \(\tau\) decides how that push is shared out:

  • Small \(\tau\): a sharp softmax, so nearly all the push goes to the hardest negatives (the most similar ones). Embeddings spread out more uniformly, but training is less stable and more hurt by false negatives: items that look like negatives but are really the same class.
  • Large \(\tau\): every negative is pushed about equally, so the loss stops caring which ones are confusing, and the embedding stays looser.
  • Scale: cosine similarities lie in \([-1, 1]\), so without a temperature the logits span only 2 units and the softmax can never become confident; the loss stays high. Typical values are 0.05 to 0.1, or learned: CLIP learns \(\log(1/\tau)\) and caps \(1/\tau\) at 100.

Why the batch size matters

  • More negatives make the task harder and put more hard negatives in every batch, which gives better representations. SimCLR used batches of thousands.
  • InfoNCE bounds the mutual information between query and key: \(I(q; k) \ge \log N - L\). The bound can't exceed \(\log N\), so with few negatives the objective saturates. At initialization the loss is about \(\log N\).
  • Getting many negatives cheaply: MoCo keeps a queue of past keys from a slowly updated (momentum) encoder; multi-GPU training gathers embeddings from all devices before building \(S\) (otherwise each device only sees its own slice); memory banks store old embeddings. BYOL and SimSiam drop negatives entirely and avoid collapse with an asymmetric predictor and a stop-gradient.
  • False negatives grow with the batch (duplicates, same-class items). Supervised contrastive learning treats every same-label item as a positive; debiased variants correct the denominator.

11Segmentation, detection and generative losses

Segmentation: Dice, IoU and Tversky

Per-pixel cross-entropy treats every pixel independently, so a small foreground object is outvoted by the background. Overlap losses score the predicted region as a whole. With predicted probabilities \(p_i\) and 0/1 ground truth \(g_i\) over the pixels, soft counts replace hard ones:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{TP} &= \sum_i p_i g_i \\ \mathrm{FP} &= \sum_i p_i (1 - g_i) \\ \mathrm{FN} &= \sum_i (1 - p_i)\, g_i \\ L_{\text{Dice}} &= 1 - \frac{2\,\mathrm{TP}}{2\,\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP} + \mathrm{FN}} \\ L_{\text{Tversky}} &= 1 - \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + a\,\mathrm{FP} + b\,\mathrm{FN}} \end{aligned} \]
  • Dice is a soft F1 (the denominator is \(\sum_i p_i + \sum_i g_i\)). It's normalized by the region's size, so a small object counts as much as a large one, and it optimizes the evaluation metric directly. Add a small constant to the numerator and denominator so empty masks don't divide by zero. Soft IoU (Jaccard) is \(\mathrm{TP}/(\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP} + \mathrm{FN})\).
  • Tversky weights the two error types separately: \(b > a\) penalizes missed pixels more and favours recall; setting both \(a\) and \(b\) to 0.5 gives Dice.
  • Dice plus cross-entropy is the usual default (nnU-Net). Dice's gradient is noisy when the region is tiny or empty and early in training; cross-entropy gives smooth per-pixel gradients, and Dice handles the imbalance.

Boxes: IoU-based losses

L1 or smooth L1 on box offsets treats the four coordinates independently and isn't scale-invariant, while detectors are judged by IoU. Plain \(1 - \mathrm{IoU}\) is scale-invariant but has zero gradient when the boxes don't overlap. With predicted box \(A\), target \(B\), the smallest box \(C\) enclosing both, \(\rho\) the distance between the two centres and \(c\) the diagonal of \(C\):

\[ \begin{aligned} L_{\text{GIoU}} &= 1 - \mathrm{IoU} + \frac{|C \setminus (A \cup B)|}{|C|} \\ L_{\text{DIoU}} &= 1 - \mathrm{IoU} + \frac{\rho^2}{c^2} \end{aligned} \]

GIoU's extra term still has a gradient for disjoint boxes, pulling the prediction towards the target. DIoU pulls the centres together directly and converges faster; CIoU adds an aspect-ratio term.

GAN losses

The discriminator outputs a logit \(d\) and \(D = \sigma(d)\): a binary classifier trained with BCE, real as 1 and generated as 0. The original minimax game, with \(G\) the generator and \(n\) its noise input:

\[ \min_G \max_D \; \mathbb E_x\big[\log D(x)\big] + \mathbb E_n\big[\log\big(1 - D(G(n))\big)\big] \]
  • The minimax generator loss saturates, the same story as MSE through a sigmoid in section 03. Early on the discriminator rejects fakes easily, so for a fake \(d \to -\infty\), and the generator's gradient with respect to \(d\) vanishes. The non-saturating loss has the generator maximize \(\log D(G(n))\) instead: the same fixed point, but a gradient near \(-1\) exactly when the generator is worst. Minimax first, non-saturating second, as \(d \to -\infty\):
    \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial d}\log\big(1 - \sigma(d)\big) &= -\sigma(d) \to 0 \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial d}\Big[-\log\sigma(d)\Big] &= \sigma(d) - 1 \to -1 \end{aligned} \]
  • With the optimal discriminator, the minimax objective is the Jensen–Shannon divergence between real and generated data, up to constants.
  • Wasserstein GAN: a critic with no sigmoid maximizes \(\mathbb E[f(\text{real})] - \mathbb E[f(\text{fake})]\) under a 1-Lipschitz constraint (weight clipping or a gradient penalty). It gives useful gradients even when the two distributions don't overlap, and its value tracks sample quality.
  • Hinge GAN (SAGAN, BigGAN): the discriminator minimizes \(\mathbb E[\max(0, 1 - d_{\text{real}})] + \mathbb E[\max(0, 1 + d_{\text{fake}})]\) and the generator minimizes \(-\mathbb E[d_{\text{fake}}]\).

The VAE loss

A VAE maximizes the evidence lower bound (ELBO) on \(\log p(x)\), with an encoder \(q_\phi(h \mid x)\) over a latent code \(h\), a decoder \(p_\theta(x \mid h)\) and a prior \(p(h) = \mathcal N(0, I)\). The loss is minus the ELBO: a reconstruction term plus a KL term.

\[ \begin{aligned} L &= -\mathbb E_{q_\phi(h \mid x)}\big[\log p_\theta(x \mid h)\big] \\ &\quad + \mathrm{KL}\big(q_\phi(h \mid x) \Vert p(h)\big) \\ \mathrm{KL} &= \tfrac12\sum_j\big(\mu_j^2 + \sigma_j^2 - \log\sigma_j^2 - 1\big) \end{aligned} \]
  • The reconstruction term is MSE for a Gaussian decoder and per-pixel BCE for a Bernoulli one (section 01). The KL has the closed form above for a diagonal Gaussian encoder \(\mathcal N(\mu, \operatorname{diag}\sigma^2)\).
  • Reparameterization: sampling isn't differentiable, so write \(h = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon\) with \(\epsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, I)\). The randomness moves into \(\epsilon\) and gradients flow through \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\).
  • Weighting the KL by \(\beta\) trades reconstruction against a smoother latent space (β-VAE). A strong decoder can ignore \(h\) and drive the KL to 0 (posterior collapse); KL annealing or a minimum KL per dimension ("free bits") counters it.

12Reduction, masking and multi-task

Mean vs sum

\[ \begin{aligned} L_{\text{mean}} &= \frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B} \ell_i \\ L_{\text{sum}} &= \sum_{i=1}^{B} \ell_i \\ &= B\,L_{\text{mean}} \end{aligned} \]
  • With SGD the step is \(\eta\nabla L\). Under sum reduction the step grows with \(B\), so changing the batch size silently changes the effective learning rate. Mean reduction keeps the gradient scale independent of \(B\). The usual linear scaling rule (multiply \(B\) by \(k\), try \(\eta \times k\) with warmup) is about the gradient becoming less noisy, and assumes mean reduction.
  • Adam is nearly invariant to a constant loss scale, since it divides by \(\sqrt{v}\). Mean vs sum matters much less, except through \(\epsilon\) and through any L2 penalty added to the loss, whose relative strength changes with the scale of the data term.
  • Gradient accumulation over \(A\) micro-batches: divide each micro-batch loss by \(A\), or the effective learning rate is \(A\) times too big.

Masking and ignore_index

Padding, unlabelled positions and prompt tokens shouldn't count. Set their targets to a sentinel (PyTorch's ignore_index, \(-100\) by default): they get zero loss and zero gradient, and the mean divides by the number of real targets. By hand that's \(\sum_i m_i \ell_i / \sum_i m_i\) for a 0/1 mask \(m\). The trap is averaging over every position, padding included, which makes the loss depend on how much padding each batch had.

Sequence losses

A language model is trained with token-level cross-entropy on the next token: logits of shape \(B \times T \times V\), targets equal to the inputs shifted by one, flattened to \((BT) \times V\). With a mask \(m\):

\[ L = -\frac{1}{\sum_{i,t} m_{it}}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\sum_{t=1}^{T} m_{it}\,\log q\big(y_{it} \mid y_{i,<t}\big) \]
  • Per-token average (above): every token counts equally, so long sequences dominate. It's the standard language-modelling objective and matches perplexity.
  • Per-sequence average: average within each sequence, then across sequences. Every sequence counts equally, so the tokens of short sequences weigh more.
  • The accumulation trap: averaging per micro-batch and then over micro-batches isn't the per-token mean when micro-batches hold different numbers of tokens. Divide the summed loss by the total token count across all micro-batches. This bug has shipped in popular training libraries.
  • Supervised fine-tuning masks the prompt so only response tokens are trained. Seq2seq with teacher forcing feeds the gold previous token and applies cross-entropy at every target position. When the alignment between input and output is unknown (speech, handwriting), CTC sums the probability of every valid alignment with dynamic programming.
# logits: (B, T, V); targets: (B, T), pad and prompt positions = -100
logits, targets = logits[:, :-1], targets[:, 1:]  # predict the next token
logP = log_softmax(logits, axis=-1)               # (B, T-1, V)
mask = targets != -100                            # (B, T-1)
safe = where(mask, targets, 0)                    # any valid id; masked below
nll  = -take_along_axis(logP, safe[..., None], axis=-1)[..., 0]  # (B, T-1)
per_token = (nll * mask).sum() / mask.sum()       # = ignore_index mean
n_seq = mask.sum(1)                               # real tokens per row
keep  = n_seq > 0                                 # skip fully masked rows
per_seq = ((nll * mask).sum(1)[keep] / n_seq[keep]).mean()

Multi-task losses

\(L = \sum_k w_k L_k\). The task losses have different units and scales (cross-entropy in nats, MSE in squared target units) and different gradient sizes on the shared layers, so equal weights let one task dominate, and grid-searching the weights is expensive.

  • Uncertainty weighting (Kendall et al., 2018) treats each task as having a learned noise level \(\sigma_k\), from the Gaussian NLL in section 01:
    \[ \begin{aligned} L &= \sum_k \Big(\frac{1}{2\sigma_k^2}\,L_k + \log\sigma_k\Big) \\ &= \sum_k \Big(\tfrac12\,e^{-s_k}\,L_k + \tfrac12\,s_k\Big) \end{aligned} \]
    Here \(s_k = \log\sigma_k^2\) is what's actually learned. A task with a large loss gets a large \(\sigma_k\) and so less weight; the \(\log\sigma_k\) term stops \(\sigma_k\) from growing forever. For fixed \(L_k\) the optimum is \(\sigma_k^2 = L_k\), so each task's effective weight is about \(1/L_k\): the method balances tasks by their loss scale. Learning \(s_k\) rather than \(\sigma_k\) keeps it stable.
  • GradNorm tunes the weights so each task's gradient norm on the last shared layer moves towards a common target, raised for tasks that are training more slowly.
  • PCGrad ("gradient surgery"): when two tasks' gradients conflict (negative cosine), project one onto the plane normal to the other before summing.
  • Strong simple baselines: divide each loss by its initial value or a running mean, then tune a few weights.

Auxiliary losses

Extra terms that help the main task train and are usually dropped at inference:

  • deep supervision (Inception's side classifiers, extra outputs in U-Net++), which injects gradient into middle layers;
  • the load-balancing loss in mixture-of-experts models, which pushes the router to spread tokens across experts;
  • regularizers written as losses: weight decay, the VAE's KL to the prior, consistency terms between two dropout passes;
  • self-supervised side tasks (masked tokens, next-state prediction in RL) next to a supervised head.

Keep their weights small enough that the main loss's gradient dominates, and check they don't conflict with it.

13Losses vs metrics

Two different jobs

  • The loss is what the optimizer minimizes: differentiable, ideally proper, matched to the noise. The metric is what you report and decide on: accuracy, F1, AUC, NDCG, cost. They're often different, and that's fine.
  • They can disagree. Validation loss rising while validation accuracy still rises is common: the model flips more borderline cases to correct while becoming very confident on the ones it still gets wrong, whose \(-\log p\) explodes. Early-stop on the metric that matters, and recalibrate if you need probabilities.
  • Match the loss to the metric where you can: an MAE metric wants an MAE or Huber loss (MSE predicts the mean, not the median); a quantile metric wants pinball loss; a ranking metric wants a pairwise or listwise loss; probability quality wants log loss.

Why you can't train on accuracy

Accuracy, \(\frac1N\sum_i \mathbf 1[\arg\max_k z_{ik} = y_i]\), is piecewise constant in the weights: a tiny change almost never flips an argmax, so the gradient is zero almost everywhere and undefined where it jumps. Gradient descent gets no signal. F1, precision at \(k\), AUC and NDCG have the same problem, because they depend on thresholds or sort orders. The options:

  • Surrogate losses: a smooth proxy or upper bound. Cross-entropy for accuracy (the margin figure above). For AUC, which is \(P(s^{+} > s^{-})\) for a random positive and negative, there are two routes. Replacing the indicator \(\mathbf 1[s^{+} > s^{-}]\) with \(\sigma(s^{+} - s^{-})\) gives a soft AUC to maximize, but it's non-convex and saturates like MSE on a sigmoid. Minimizing \(-\log\sigma(s^{+} - s^{-})\) instead is the pairwise logistic (RankNet) loss, a convex upper bound on the mis-ordering indicator (in base 2). "Soft F1" computes true and false positives from probabilities instead of hard predictions. LambdaRank for NDCG.
  • Post-hoc tuning: train with cross-entropy, then choose the threshold that maximizes F1 on validation data. This captures most of the gain.
  • Policy gradient (REINFORCE): treat the output as a sampled action and the metric \(R\) as a reward:
    \[ \nabla_\theta\,\mathbb E\big[R(\hat y)\big] = \mathbb E\Big[\big(R(\hat y) - b\big)\,\nabla_\theta\log q_\theta(\hat y)\Big] \]
    Both expectations are over outputs sampled from the model, \(\hat y \sim q_\theta\). It works for any black-box reward (BLEU or CIDEr in self-critical sequence training, a learned reward model in RLHF), but its variance is high: it needs a baseline \(b\) (the greedy output's reward, or a learned value) and usually cross-entropy pretraining first.
  • Relaxations such as Gumbel-softmax or differentiable sorting are another route when a discrete step sits inside the model.

Softmax cross-entropy, forward and backward

def softmax_ce(Z, y):
    B = Z.shape[0]
    m    = Z.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)                # (B, 1) row max
    lse  = m + log(exp(Z - m).sum(axis=1, keepdims=True))  # (B, 1)
    logP = Z - lse                                     # (B, K) log-probs
    loss = -logP[arange(B), y].mean()                  # mean over the batch

    P  = exp(logP)                                     # softmax, no overflow
    dZ = P.copy()
    dZ[arange(B), y] -= 1                              # P - Y, Y one-hot
    dZ /= B                                            # from the mean
    return loss, dZ                                    # dZ: (B, K)

Binary cross-entropy with logits

# z: (B,) logits   y: (B,) in {0, 1}   pw: weight on positives
def bce_with_logits(z, y, pw=1.0):
    sp_pos = maximum(z, 0) + log1p(exp(-abs(z)))  # softplus(z)  = -log(1 - p)
    sp_neg = sp_pos - z                            # softplus(-z) = -log p
    loss = (pw * y * sp_neg + (1 - y) * sp_pos).mean()

    p  = sigmoid(z)
    dz = (pw * y * (p - 1) + (1 - y) * p) / len(z)  # (p - y)/B if pw = 1
    return loss, dz

def sigmoid(z):                     # never exponentiates a large positive
    e = exp(-abs(z))
    return where(z >= 0, 1 / (1 + e), e / (1 + e))

Focal loss (binary, from logits)

# z: (N,) logits   y: (N,) in {0, 1}
def focal_loss(z, y, gamma=2.0, alpha=0.25):
    p   = sigmoid(z)
    ce  = maximum(z, 0) - z * y + log1p(exp(-abs(z)))  # BCE per example
    p_t = y * p + (1 - y) * (1 - p)        # prob. of the true class
    a_t = y * alpha + (1 - y) * (1 - alpha)
    loss = a_t * (1 - p_t) ** gamma * ce   # easy examples -> about 0
    return loss.sum() / max(1, y.sum())    # RetinaNet: per positive

# backward: autograd, or per example in the true-class logit z_t:
#   -a_t * (1 - p_t)**gamma * ((1 - p_t) + gamma * p_t * ce)   (log p_t = -ce)

# Zq: (B, d) queries (anchors)   Zk: (B, d) keys; row i of Zq matches row i of Zk
def info_nce(Zq, Zk, tau=0.07, symmetric=True):
    Zq = Zq / norm(Zq, axis=1, keepdims=True)  # unit vectors: dot = cosine
    Zk = Zk / norm(Zk, axis=1, keepdims=True)

    labels = arange(len(Zq))                 # row i's positive is column i
    loss = cross_entropy(S, labels)          # each row: a B-way softmax
    if symmetric:                            # CLIP: columns too
        loss = (loss + cross_entropy(S.T, labels)) / 2
    return loss                              # about log B at init

15Interview questions

Answer out loud first, then open the answer.

1Derive MSE from maximum likelihood. What does it assume, and what does the model end up predicting? What about MAE?

Assume \(y \mid x \sim \mathcal N(\hat y, \sigma^2)\) with fixed \(\sigma\). Then:

\[ \begin{aligned} -\log p(y \mid x) &= \frac{(y - \hat y)^2}{2\sigma^2} + \text{const} \\ \arg\min_{\hat y}\ \mathbb E\big[(y - \hat y)^2\big] &= \mathbb E[y \mid x] \end{aligned} \]

It assumes symmetric, light-tailed noise with one variance everywhere. It predicts the conditional mean, and that holds for any noise distribution; Gaussianity only makes it the most efficient choice. MAE is the Laplace likelihood and predicts the conditional median. Pinball loss at level \(\tau\) predicts the \(\tau\)-quantile. (01)

2Your regression target has outliers. What do you do?

First decide whether they're errors (fix or drop them) or real (keep them). For real heavy tails, use a loss whose pull is capped: Huber (MSE with its gradient clipped at \(\delta\), with \(\delta\) set from the residual scale), MAE if the median is what you want, or log-cosh if you need a smooth second derivative. A log transform also tames skewed targets, but then MSE predicts the geometric mean, which is below the mean, so correct it if predictions get summed. If the real question is a percentile, use pinball loss. (02)

3What loss for daily order counts? For spend per customer, where most customers spend nothing?

Counts: Poisson with a log link, \(L = e^{f} - y f\), gradient \(\mu - y\). It keeps predictions positive, makes effects multiplicative and assumes variance equals mean; use negative binomial if the data is overdispersed and you need intervals, and an exposure offset for rates. Zero-inflated spend: Tweedie with \(1 < p < 2\), a point mass at zero plus a continuous positive part, with gradient \(\mu^{1-p}(\mu - y)\). Plain MSE would predict negatives, ignore that variance grows with the mean, and be dominated by big spenders. (02)

4Why does softmax with cross-entropy give \(p - y\) at the logits?

The softmax Jacobian is \(\partial p_i/\partial z_j = p_i(\delta_{ij} - p_j)\). The loss contributes \(-y_i/p_i\), so the \(p_i\) cancels:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_j} &= -\sum_i \frac{y_i}{p_i}\,p_i\,(\delta_{ij} - p_j) \\ &= -y_j + p_j\sum_i y_i \\ &= p_j - y_j \end{aligned} \]

Equivalently, \(L = -z_c + \log\sum_k e^{z_k}\), and the gradient of log-sum-exp is the softmax. The deeper reason is that softmax is the canonical link of the categorical distribution; every GLM with its canonical link has "prediction minus target" as its gradient. It works for any soft target that sums to 1. (04)

5Why not use MSE for classification?

Through a sigmoid, MSE's gradient is \((p - y)\,p(1 - p)\). The \(p(1 - p)\) factor vanishes when the output saturates, so a confidently wrong example gets almost no gradient, exactly where learning matters most; cross-entropy's \(p - y\) is near \(\pm 1\) there. MSE is also non-convex in the logit (the curvature flips sign at \(p = 1/3\) for \(y = 1\)), its penalty for a confident mistake is bounded, and it's the wrong likelihood for a 0/1 label. As a metric on probabilities (the Brier score) it's fine. (03)

6What goes wrong if you forget the softmax, apply it twice, or compute the loss from clamped probabilities?
  • Probabilities into CrossEntropyLoss: a double softmax. The inputs lie in \([0, 1]\), so the true class can reach at most \(e/(e + K - 1)\); the loss has a high floor and the gradients are tiny. Slow training, meaningless probabilities.
  • Logits into NLLLoss with no log-softmax: the loss is \(-z_c\), unbounded below, so it diverges to minus infinity.
  • Softmax, then log: \(\log 0\) gives \(-\infty\) and then NaN. Use log-softmax (log-sum-exp).
  • Clamping \(p\) to \([\varepsilon, 1 - \varepsilon]\): clamp has zero derivative outside the range, so the most confidently wrong examples get exactly zero gradient. Frameworks take logits so the loss is computed stably and the gradient is exactly \(p - y\). (05)
7Multi-class vs multi-label: what output layer and loss?

Exactly one label: softmax over \(K\) with cross-entropy; probabilities compete and sum to 1; predict with argmax. Any subset: \(K\) independent sigmoids with a BCE per label; each probability is separate, there's an implicit "none", and each label gets its own threshold. Mutually exclusive groups within a multi-label problem get one softmax per group. (04)

8How are entropy, cross-entropy and KL related? Forward vs reverse KL?
\[ \begin{aligned} H(p, q) &= -\sum_k p_k\log q_k \\ &= H(p) + \mathrm{KL}(p \Vert q) \end{aligned} \]

The entropy of the data doesn't depend on the model, so minimizing cross-entropy is maximum likelihood is minimizing \(\mathrm{KL}(\text{data} \Vert \text{model})\). Forward KL averages over the data, so the model must cover every mode: mass-covering. Reverse KL averages over the model, so it avoids regions where the data is thin and locks onto a mode: mode-seeking, as in variational inference and the KL penalty in RLHF. KL isn't symmetric, so it isn't a distance. (06)

9What loss should a \(K\)-class classifier show at step 0, and what does it mean if it's far higher?

With small random weights the logits are near zero, the softmax is uniform, and the loss is \(-\log(1/K) = \log K\) (perplexity \(K\)). Much higher means the initial logits are large, so the model starts confidently wrong and wastes steps undoing it: scale down the last layer's init. For binary problems, set the output bias to the log-odds of the base rate so the starting loss is the base rate's entropy instead of \(\log 2\). The floor isn't 0 either: label noise sets it at \(H(y \mid x)\). (13)

10You have a 1-in-1,000 positive rate. How do you handle it in the loss?

Cross-entropy doesn't bias the probabilities under imbalance; the real issues are a gradient dominated by easy negatives and a wrong default threshold. Options:

  • Class weights or pos_weight: they multiply the predicted odds by \(w\), which is a threshold shift; ranking is nearly unchanged.
  • Resampling: keeping a fraction \(\rho\) of the negatives multiplies the odds by \(1/\rho\); correct with \(\operatorname{logit} p = \operatorname{logit} p_s + \log\rho\). Balanced batches are steadier than huge weights for neural nets.
  • Focal loss when there are huge numbers of easy negatives.

In practice: often train unweighted, calibrate, pick the threshold from costs, and evaluate with PR-AUC or cost rather than accuracy. (07)

11Explain focal loss. What do \(\gamma\) and \(\alpha\) do?
\[ \mathrm{FL} = -\,\alpha_t\,(1 - p_t)^{\gamma}\log p_t \]

\((1 - p_t)^\gamma\) down-weights well-classified examples (\(p_t\) near 1) of either class, so the loss and gradient concentrate on hard and misclassified ones; \(\gamma = 0\) is cross-entropy and 2 is typical. It was built for one-stage detection, where around 100,000 easy background anchors would swamp the loss. \(\alpha\) is a class weight; with \(\gamma = 2\), RetinaNet's best was 0.25 on the rare class, because \(\gamma\) had already removed most of the negatives' loss. It's not a proper scoring rule, so check calibration. (07)

12Label smoothing vs focal loss?

Label smoothing changes the target to \((1 - \varepsilon)y + \varepsilon/K\) for every example. It caps the logit gap at \(\log\big(1 + K(1 - \varepsilon)/\varepsilon\big)\), stops logits growing forever, reduces over-confidence and tightens class clusters. Focal loss changes each example's weight by difficulty, so easy examples nearly vanish; it's for an easy majority swamping the gradient. Smoothing hurts distillation and anything needing real confidence; focal loss pulls probabilities towards 0.5. Both lower confidence, and neither replaces measuring calibration. (08)

13What is calibration, how do you measure it, and how does temperature scaling fix it without changing accuracy?

Calibrated means that of the cases scored \(p\), a fraction \(p\) are positive. Measure it with a reliability diagram and ECE, the count-weighted gap between accuracy and confidence across confidence bins, alongside log loss or Brier. Deep networks are often over-confident because cross-entropy keeps rewarding larger logits. Temperature scaling fits one scalar \(T\) on held-out data to minimize the NLL of \(\operatorname{softmax}(z/T)\). Dividing every logit by the same positive number never changes the argmax, so accuracy is identical while confidence is softened. (08)

14In knowledge distillation, why use a temperature, and why multiply the soft loss by \(T^2\)?

At \(T = 1\) the teacher's probabilities are nearly one-hot, so the information about which wrong classes resemble the right one sits in tiny probabilities that carry no gradient. Softening with \(T > 1\) exposes it. The soft term's gradient, and its high-temperature limit, are:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\,\mathrm{KL}}{\partial z} &= \frac{q^{T} - p^{T}}{T} \\ &\approx \frac{z - v}{K T^2} \end{aligned} \]

So it shrinks like \(1/T^2\); multiplying by \(T^2\) keeps its weight relative to the hard-label term fixed as \(T\) changes. At very high \(T\), distillation becomes logit matching with squared error. (06)

15Hinge vs logistic loss. Why doesn't an SVM give probabilities?

Both are convex surrogates for the 0–1 loss as functions of the margin \(m = yz\). Hinge, \(\max(0, 1 - m)\), is exactly zero beyond the margin, so only support vectors shape the solution, giving sparse, margin-maximizing behaviour. Logistic, \(\log(1 + e^{-m})\), never reaches zero, keeps nudging every point and is a likelihood. Hinge's population minimizer is \(\operatorname{sign}(2p - 1)\), the best decision rather than the probability, so SVM scores need Platt scaling to become probabilities. (09)

16Why can't you train directly on accuracy, F1 or NDCG? What do you do instead?

They're piecewise constant in the weights: small weight changes almost never flip a prediction or a sort order, so the gradient is zero almost everywhere and undefined at the jumps. Instead: train a smooth surrogate (cross-entropy for accuracy, pairwise logistic for AUC, LambdaRank for NDCG, soft F1), tune the threshold on validation data to maximize the metric, or use REINFORCE with the metric as a reward and a baseline to cut variance, as in sequence-level training and RLHF. (13)

17Triplet loss: what is the margin for, and why do hard negatives matter? Can they hurt?

\(L = \max\big(0,\ d(a, p) - d(a, n) + m\big)\). The margin demands that the negative be farther than the positive by \(m\), not just barely farther, which sets the scale of the separation. Most random triplets already satisfy it and give zero gradient, so you mine hard or semi-hard negatives within the batch. The very hardest negatives early in training can collapse the embedding to a point and are often label noise, which is why FaceNet used semi-hard ones. (10)

18Why is InfoNCE's temperature important, and why does batch size matter?

InfoNCE is softmax cross-entropy over similarities divided by \(\tau\), and its gradient pushes each negative in proportion to its softmax weight. Small \(\tau\) concentrates the push on the hardest negatives: more uniform embeddings, but less stable and more sensitive to false negatives. Large \(\tau\) treats all negatives alike. Since cosine similarities lie in \([-1, 1]\), without a small \(\tau\) the softmax can't get confident at all. Batch size sets the number of negatives: more and harder negatives per step, and the mutual-information bound \(\log N - L\) is capped at \(\log N\). Hence large batches, MoCo's queue, and gathering embeddings across GPUs. (10)

19Mean vs sum reduction: what changes when you switch? And how should a padded sequence loss be averaged?

Sum makes the gradient, and so SGD's effective learning rate, grow with batch size; mean keeps it independent of \(B\). Adam is mostly insensitive to the loss scale, apart from \(\epsilon\) and L2 terms. With gradient accumulation, divide by the number of micro-batches. For sequences, mask padding with ignore_index and divide by the number of real tokens, never by all positions. Per-token averaging weights long sequences more; per-sequence averaging weights each sequence equally. With accumulation, divide by the total tokens across all micro-batches. (12)

20How do you weight the losses in a multi-task model?

Task losses differ in units, scale and gradient size, so fixed equal weights let one task dominate. Simple baselines normalize each loss by its initial value or a running mean. Uncertainty weighting learns \(s_k = \log\sigma_k^2\) per task and minimizes \(\sum_k \big(\tfrac12 e^{-s_k}L_k + \tfrac12 s_k\big)\), giving each task a weight of about \(1/L_k\). GradNorm balances the tasks' gradient norms on the shared layers; PCGrad projects away conflicting gradient components. (12)

21How does training a neural network relate to maximum likelihood? What is the network assuming about the data?

Minimizing the usual losses is maximum-likelihood estimation: the network outputs a distribution's parameters, and the loss is that distribution's negative log-likelihood.

Assuming i.i.d. examples, the dataset's likelihood is a product over examples, and its negative log is a sum, which mini-batch SGD can estimate:

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat\theta &= \arg\max_\theta \prod_i p_\theta(y_i \mid x_i) \\ &= \arg\min_\theta \sum_i -\log p_\theta(y_i \mid x_i) \end{aligned} \]

The head fixes the distribution, and the distribution fixes the loss:

  • linear output → Gaussian with fixed variance → MSE (predicts the mean);
  • sigmoid → Bernoulli → BCE;
  • softmax → categorical → cross-entropy;
  • softmax at every position → autoregressive language model → token-level cross-entropy.

The assumptions:

  • Independent examples.
  • Outputs independent given the input: per-pixel MSE blurs uncertain images for this reason.
  • For MSE, constant noise.

Weight decay adds a Gaussian prior, which turns MLE into MAP.

Follow-ups:

  • Is every deep-learning loss MLE? No. VAEs and diffusion models maximize a bound on the likelihood, GANs use none, and triplet, hinge and contrastive losses are margin objectives.
  • Why are big networks overconfident? The NLL keeps rewarding larger logits on training data.
  • What does the CLT have to do with it? It motivates Gaussian noise, and it makes mini-batch gradients roughly Gaussian with variance shrinking as \(1/B\).

(01)

22Where do cross-entropy and the other losses come from? How do you pick a loss for a new target?

Each loss is the negative log-likelihood of an assumed distribution for \(y \mid x\); pick the distribution that matches how the target is generated, and the loss follows.

  • Binary label: a Bernoulli with \(p = \sigma(z)\) gives BCE, \(-y\log p - (1-y)\log(1-p)\). That is logistic regression.
  • One of \(K\) classes: a categorical with \(p = \text{softmax}(z)\) gives cross-entropy, \(-\log p_{y}\).
  • Real value, symmetric noise: Gaussian gives MSE (predicts the mean); Laplace gives MAE (predicts the median), more robust to outliers.
  • Counts: Poisson with a log link, loss \(\mu - y\log\mu\); predictions stay positive and the variance grows with the mean.
  • Positive, skewed amounts (spend, claim size): Gamma or Tweedie with a log link.

Why this matters: the distribution decides what the optimum predicts (mean, median, probability), how hard outliers pull, and whether predictions respect the target's range. A mismatched loss still trains, but it estimates the wrong quantity, for example a mean when the business needs a quantile. Regularization fits the same frame: a Gaussian prior on the weights adds L2, a Laplace prior adds L1. (01)
