Data preparation and feature engineering

What to do to a tabular dataset before a model sees it, and why each step exists: the first look, cleaning, honest splits, missing values, outliers, encoding categories, scaling, collinearity, building and selecting features, class imbalance, and telling overfitting from underfitting.

About 45 minutes · mechanisms, trade-offs, pseudocode and 25 questions

In short

  1. One rule runs through everything. Anything that learns a number from the data (a median, a category's average target, a scaler's mean, a list of selected features) is fitted on the training rows only, inside each cross-validation fold. Fixes that learn nothing, such as correcting a type or a typo, can run before the split.
  2. Before filling a blank, ask why it's blank: by pure chance (MCAR), because of other things you can see (MAR), or because of the missing value itself (MNAR). Blanks often carry signal, so keep it with a missing-indicator column or an explicit “Missing” category. Filling with the mode or median and leaving no trace is safe only when the blanks are random and few.
  3. Encoding follows the model and the number of levels. One-hot for a few levels; out-of-fold, smoothed target encoding or count encoding for many; native categorical support in boosting libraries; embeddings in neural nets. Target encoding leaks because each row's own label sits inside its category's average.
  4. Outliers: first decide whether a value is an error or a genuine extreme. Errors are fixed, or turned into missing values. Genuine extremes are capped or transformed, or handled by a robust loss or a tree. In fraud, the extremes are the signal.
  5. Scaling matters for anything built on distances, penalties or gradient descent; trees ignore monotone transforms. Collinearity makes coefficients unstable but usually leaves predictions fine, and it splits a tree's feature importance between correlated features.
  6. Split the way the model will meet new data: by time for forecasting and credit, by group when new customers arrive, stratified when a class is rare. For imbalance, move the threshold first, reweight second, resample last.

01The first look

The first look is a checklist, not free browsing. Each check answers one question: what could make a model wrong, or make it look better than it is? Write the answers down; most of them decide a later step.

Where to look. Hygiene checks (types, duplicates, missing shares) are fine on all the data, since they learn nothing the model uses. Exploration that shapes modelling choices, such as which features look predictive or where to cap a tail, uses the training portion only; choosing features because they fit the test rows is a quiet leak.

Adversarial validation is a fast check that training and test data look alike. Label training rows 0 and test (or recent) rows 1 and train a classifier to tell them apart. An AUC near 0.5 means they look alike; a high AUC means they differ, and its top features show where: drift, a changed definition, or an ID that encodes time.

02Cleaning

Cleaning makes each value mean what it claims to mean. Most cleaning steps are row-by-row rules that learn nothing from the data, which is why they can safely run before the split.

Duplicates

  • What: exact copies (a replayed batch, a join that fanned one row out into several) and near-copies (the same customer re-applying, a retried payment).
  • Why it matters: copies get extra weight, and a copy in training with its twin in validation means the model is graded on rows it memorized.
  • What to do: first ask whether the repeat is real; two identical purchases can both be genuine. Remove artefacts; keep genuine repeats but split by entity. Check the row count after every join, since a many-to-many join multiplies rows silently.

Inconsistent categories

  • What: one real level written several ways: case, stray spaces, typos, synonyms (“Self-employed”, “Business owner”).
  • Why it matters: one level splits into several rare ones with noisy averages, and a new spelling at serving time becomes an unseen category.
  • What to do: trim, lower-case and normalize Unicode, then map with an explicit table. Fuzzy matching finds candidates, but a person approves the merges: “Mumbai” and “Mumba” are one place, “Austria” and “Australia” are not.

Wrong types, impossible values and units

  • Wrong types: numbers stored as text (“1,200”, “12k”), mixed date formats, booleans as “Y”, “yes” and 1. And the reverse: IDs and postcodes stored as numbers, so a model treats one postcode as “bigger” than another.
  • Sentinels: −1, 0 or 9999 meaning “unknown”. An income of 0 that means “not provided” is a blank pretending to be a value, and a linear model reads it as very low income. Convert sentinels to proper blanks.
  • Impossible values (an age of 230, a negative quantity, an end before a start) are errors, not outliers. Set them to missing rather than deleting the row.
  • Unit mismatches (rupees vs thousands, 35 vs 0.35, grams vs kilograms) show up as clusters a factor of 10, 100 or 1,000 apart. Split the distribution by source or by date to find them.

Label noise

  • What: wrong targets: annotators disagree, a definition changed (default at 30 days past due, later 90), or a label isn't mature yet (a loan disbursed last month can't have defaulted, so it's “good” too early).
  • Why it matters: random noise caps the accuracy you can reach and blurs probabilities; systematic noise, like immature labels, teaches something false.
  • Detect: rows whose out-of-fold probability strongly disagrees with their label (the idea behind confident learning), the highest-loss rows, annotator disagreement; then review a sample by hand.
  • Treat: relabel or drop clear errors, set a maturity window for outcomes, and prefer training that doesn't chase every point (label smoothing, early stopping). Keep the test labels cleanest of all, or every model comparison is noisy.

03Split first: honest splits and leakage

Every later step asks the same question: was this fitted on data the model will legitimately have? So the split comes before any step that learns from the data.

Train, validation, test

  • Training set: fits the model's parameters and every preprocessing statistic.
  • Validation set (or cross-validation folds): chooses hyperparameters, features, thresholds and the preprocessing recipe.
  • Test set: touched once, at the end. Every look followed by a change turns it into a validation set, and the estimate becomes optimistic.

The rule for preprocessing: a step that learns a number (a median, a mean and standard deviation, a category's average target, a vocabulary, a list of selected features) is fitted on training rows and applied unchanged to validation, test and production rows. Steps that learn nothing, like parsing a date, can run anywhere.

Choosing the split

The split must imitate how the model will meet new data. Ask: at prediction time, what's new?

SituationSplitWhy
Independent rows, classificationStratified k-foldEach fold keeps the class ratio. With 2% positives, a random fold can get very few, and its score is noise.
Many rows per customer, store or device; new ones at deploymentGroup k-foldAll rows of one group stay together, so the model can't score well by recognizing the customer.
Data ordered in time; predicting forward (credit, demand, fraud)Time-based: expanding or rolling window, plus a final out-of-time testTrain on the past, test on the future, as in production. Random splits leak trends and future regimes backwards.
Labels that cover a horizon (default within 12 months)Time-based with a gapThe last training rows' outcomes are only observed during the validation period; the gap drops them.

Leakage, in four kinds

Leakage is any information available in training or validation that won't be available when the model makes a real prediction. It's the most common reason for “great offline, poor in production”.

  1. Target leakage: a feature recorded after the label, or caused by it: “number of collections calls” when predicting default, “chargeback filed” when predicting fraud. The test, per feature: is this value known, with this exact content, at the moment of prediction?
  2. Train–test contamination: the same row, a near-copy or the same customer on both sides of the split. Oversampling or SMOTE before the split does this too.
  3. Preprocessing fitted on all the data: a scaler, imputer, encoder, PCA or feature selection fitted before splitting. Scaling leaks a little; target encoding and feature selection leak a lot, because they use the labels.
  4. Temporal leakage: random splits of time-ordered data, a customer's lifetime average that includes later rows, or today's value of a slowly changing table joined instead of its value at prediction time.

How it shows up: a score too good for the problem; one feature dominating importance, or predicting the target almost perfectly alone; a random split scoring far above a time-based or grouped one.

Pipelines are the fix

A Pipeline chains preprocessing and the model into one object, and a ColumnTransformer applies different preprocessing to different columns. Hand the whole pipeline to cross-validation and every step is refitted on each training fold, so the validation fold never touches any statistic. The same fitted object then runs in production, which removes train–serve skew (two codebases computing a feature slightly differently). A full example is in section 13.

Trap

“Scaling has no labels, so scaling everything before cross-validation is fine.” The effect is small for scaling, but the same order of steps with target encoding, feature selection or SMOTE inflates the score a lot. Put every fitted step inside the pipeline.

04Missing values

Filling a blank is the last step, not the first. Three questions come before any method:

  1. Why is it missing? The mechanism decides which methods are unbiased.
  2. Does missingness itself predict the target? If it does, it's a feature, and filling it in without a trace throws that feature away.
  3. Will it be missing in production in the same way? The model must meet blanks at serving time exactly as it met them in training.

Why values go missing: MCAR, MAR, MNAR

MechanismExampleWhat it implies
MCAR, missing completely at randomA sensor drops readings at random; a batch of forms was lost in a migration.The complete rows are a random sample, so dropping rows adds no bias; it only loses data. A mean fill keeps the mean right but still shrinks the spread and the correlations.
MAR, missing at random given what's observedIncome is blank more often for self-employed applicants, and employment type is recorded. Among the self-employed, a high earner and a low earner are equally likely to leave it blank.The reason for the blank is a column you have. Dropping rows under-represents the self-employed, so it's biased. Imputing income from employment type and the other columns removes that bias.
MNAR, missing not at randomHigh earners leave income blank because it's high (privacy), or low earners because it's low. The value itself decides whether it gets reported.The reason is the hidden value, so no column you have can fully correct for it: two applicants who match on every observed column still differ, because the blank itself hints at the income. Imputing from other columns stays biased. The information sits in the fact of the blank, so keep it as a feature.

MAR vs MNAR: does the blank say something about its own value?

One question separates them. Take two applicants who match on every column you have. If you learn that one of them left income blank, does your guess of that person's income change?

  • No: MAR. The reason for the blank is already in your data (employment type, say). Once you know it, the blank adds nothing about the income. (MCAR passes too: it's the special case where the blank doesn't depend on the observed columns either.)
  • Yes: MNAR. The blank carries information about the very value it hides. High earners hiding their salary is the classic case: a blank income means “probably high”, even among people who look identical on paper.

In symbols, with \(M\) the “is blank” flag, \(x_{\text{obs}}\) the columns you can see and \(x_{\text{mis}}\) the hidden value:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{MCAR:}\quad P(M \mid x_{\text{obs}}, x_{\text{mis}}) &= P(M) \\ \text{MAR:}\quad P(M \mid x_{\text{obs}}, x_{\text{mis}}) &= P(M \mid x_{\text{obs}}) \\ \text{MNAR:}\quad P(M \mid x_{\text{obs}}, x_{\text{mis}}) &\ \text{still depends on}\ x_{\text{mis}} \end{aligned} \]

In words: under MAR, once you know the columns you can see, the hidden value no longer changes the chance of a blank. Under MNAR it still does.

Worked example. Eight applicants, true monthly income in ₹k. The true average is 60.

SalariedSelf-employedAverage of all 8
Truth30, 40, 50, 60 (mean 45)30, 60, 90, 120 (mean 75)60
MAR: two self-employed blanks, picked at random (60 and 90)all seen30 and 120 seen (mean 75)Drop the blank rows: 55, too low, because the self-employed are under-represented. Fill each blank with its group's mean (75): 60, correct

Under MAR, the column that explains the blanks (employment type) removes the bias completely. Under MNAR it removes none of it here (and in general it can't remove all of it): inside each group, the people who hid their income are the richer ones, and no observed column can see that. What does survive is the blank itself. An income_missing flag is 1 for exactly the two top earners, and a model can learn from it.

The same blanks can be MAR or MNAR, depending on what you record. Say doctors are the ones who skip the income field, and among doctors, skipping has nothing to do with how much each one earns. If occupation is a column, the blanks are explained by it: MAR. Leave occupation out, and the same blanks now depend on something you can't see that's tied to income: MNAR for your dataset. So MNAR really means “depends on something unobserved that's related to the value”, and the hidden value itself is the textbook case.

Trap

“MAR means the values are missing at random.” The name misleads: MAR blanks are not random overall (the self-employed have more of them). They're random only once you account for the observed columns. And the share that's missing says nothing about the mechanism.

A fourth case, structural blanks: “months since last loan” for someone who has never had a loan. Nothing is unknown here; the value is undefined. Imputing a typical value would invent a loan. Use a domain rule instead: a “none” category, or a sentinel plus an indicator.

Why the names matter in practice. For a prediction model, the key question is whether blanks at serving time are produced the same way as in training. If they are, the blank can be used as a feature whatever the mechanism, even MNAR. For inference (estimating a coefficient, an effect, a population average) the mechanism decides whether the estimate is biased, and MNAR needs assumptions the data can't check.

Diagnosing the mechanism

  1. Compare rows with and without the value. Look at the other columns' distributions and at the target rate in each group. If the groups differ, the data isn't MCAR.
  2. Predict the blank. Train a classifier on the other columns to predict “is this value missing?”, scored out-of-fold. An AUC near 0.5 is consistent with MCAR. A clearly higher AUC means it isn't MCAR: the blanks depend on observed columns (MAR, or MNAR if the value itself matters too), and the classifier's top features say which. An AUC near 0.5 can't rule out MNAR. Little's MCAR test is the formal version of this check, for means.
  3. Check whether the blank predicts the target. If rows with a blank default at twice the rate of the rest, the blank is a predictor, however it arose.
  4. MAR vs MNAR can't be settled from the data. The difference is about values you never observe. Use domain knowledge (why would someone skip this field?), a follow-up sample where the true values are obtained, or an outside source that has them for some rows.

Missingness as a signal: the indicator column

A missing indicator is a 0/1 column that records whether the original value was blank, added next to the filled-in column. The model can then give blanks their own effect, and it knows which values are real and which were filled in.

In a linear model it frees the blank rows' prediction. Fill blanks with any constant \(c\), giving the filled column \(\tilde x\) (\(x\) where observed, \(c\) where blank), and add the indicator \(I\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat y &= \beta_0 + \beta\, \tilde x + \theta I \\ \text{observed rows:}\quad \hat y &= \beta_0 + \beta x \\ \text{blank rows:}\quad \hat y &= \beta_0 + \beta c + \theta \end{aligned} \]

In words:

  • Without the indicator, a blank row is treated as a real point at \(x = c\), so the one fitted line has to pass near it too. If blank rows have unusual targets, they pull the line. Real rows (1, 1), (2, 2), (3, 3) have slope 1; add two blank rows with \(y = 8\), filled at \(c = 0\), and the fitted slope becomes −1.8.
  • With the indicator, a blank row's prediction \(\beta_0 + \beta c + \theta\) is one free number, and \(\theta\) sets it to the blank rows' own level (8 here). The line is then fitted to the real rows alone, so \(\beta\) stays exactly 1.
  • So the choice of \(c\) doesn't matter: \(\theta\) absorbs it (\(\theta = 8 - c\) here). The exception is regularization: a far-off \(c\) such as −999 needs a huge \(\theta\), which the penalty shrinks, so fill with the median.

Add one when:

  • the target rate differs between blank and non-blank rows, or MNAR is plausible;
  • you fill with a simple value for a linear model or a neural net;
  • the share missing isn't trivial (an indicator that's almost always 0 carries little).

Skip it, or think twice, when:

  • a boosting library handles blanks natively: it already learns where blanks go at each split;
  • the collection process will change. If the income field becomes mandatory, a model that learned “blank means risky” has a feature that no longer fires;
  • many columns go blank together (one source failed). Use one indicator per source or pattern, not one per column.

Trade-off: the model now depends on how the data was collected, which is often a strong signal and always a fragile one. Monitor missing rates in production: a pipeline bug that blanks a field changes scores without any error.

Numeric options

Mean or median. Replace each blank with the training mean or median. It's fast, simple and stable. The median is the usual choice for skewed columns such as income, where a few huge values pull the mean. What it distorts, with a share \(r\) missing completely at random and filled with the mean (\(\tilde x\) is the filled column, \(z\) any other column):

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(\tilde x) &\approx (1 - r)\, \operatorname{Var}(x) \\ \operatorname{Corr}(\tilde x, z) &\approx \sqrt{1 - r}\; \operatorname{Corr}(x, z) \end{aligned} \]

In words: every filled value sits exactly at the centre, so it adds nothing to the spread and nothing to any co-movement with other columns. The variance shrinks, every correlation weakens, and the histogram grows a spike at the mean.

Under MAR it's also biased: filling a self-employed applicant's income with the overall median ignores what employment type says. In a model with several correlated features, the other features absorb the missing part of \(x\), which biases their coefficients. With an indicator, much of the damage to prediction goes away; the damage to inference doesn't. Prediction vs inference below shows why, with numbers.

A constant, plus an indicator. Fill with a fixed value. For trees, an out-of-range value such as −999 puts every blank at one end, so one split can separate them: a crude version of native handling. For linear models, an out-of-range constant without an indicator is harmful: it acts as an extreme point with high leverage and tilts the slope. With an indicator the constant doesn't matter, but only without regularization and before scaling: a −999 fill inflates a later scaler's \(\sigma\) and squeezes the real values together. For linear models and neural nets, fill with the median and let the indicator carry the signal.

kNN imputation. For a row with a blank, find the \(k\) most similar training rows, measured on the columns both have, and average their values (a majority vote for categories).

  • Why: it fills from rows that look alike, so it respects local structure and relationships without assuming a model.
  • Costs: features must be scaled first, or the largest-unit column decides who's “similar”. Every imputation searches the training set, so it's slow on big data and the training rows must be kept for serving. Distances get less meaningful as the number of columns grows.
  • When: small-to-medium data with a few informative numeric columns. Not for big data, many columns, or mostly categorical data.

Model-based (regression) imputation. Train a model to predict the column from the others, on rows where it's observed, and predict the blanks.

  • Why: under MAR it uses exactly the information that explains the blanks, so it removes the bias that mean filling leaves.
  • The catch: predictions sit on the regression surface with no noise, so imputed values are too regular and correlations come out too strong. Adding a random draw of the model's residual (stochastic regression imputation) fixes the spread.
  • When: under MAR, when the column matters to a linear model or to inference.
  • Never use the target as an input in a prediction pipeline: the target isn't known when a new row arrives. (In multiple imputation for inference, the reverse holds: the outcome should be in the imputation model, or the imputed values weaken its relationships.)

Iterative imputation (MICE). Model-based imputation for many columns at once, when several have blanks:

  1. Fill every blank with a simple value (the median).
  2. For each column with blanks in turn, fit a model predicting it from all the other columns, on the rows where it's observed, and replace its blanks with the predictions.
  3. Cycle through the columns several times, until the imputed values stop changing much.

MICE (multiple imputation by chained equations) is the version that adds random draws at step 2 and repeats the whole process to produce several completed datasets (see multiple imputation). sklearn's IterativeImputer returns one completed dataset per run (run it several times with sample_posterior=True for multiple imputation); with random forests as the per-column model it's close to missForest. Trade-offs: it uses the joint structure of all columns and handles blanks in many columns at once, but it's slow, it assumes MAR, it can overfit, convergence isn't guaranteed, and a single deterministic run understates uncertainty.

Time series: forward fill and interpolation. Forward fill carries the last observed value forward; it uses only the past, so it's safe for live prediction. Add “time since last real observation”, because a value carried forward for a month isn't as trustworthy as yesterday's. Linear interpolation between neighbours uses the next value, which doesn't exist yet at prediction time: fine for cleaning a historical chart, a leak in a forecasting feature.

Domain rules. Often the best imputer is knowing the data. A blank “number of previous defaults” for a bureau record that exists means 0; a blank “spouse income” for a single applicant means “not applicable”; a blank discount means no discount. Encode these explicitly, before any statistical method.

Categorical options

An explicit “Missing” category: usually the best default. Treat the blank as its own level.

  • Why: it keeps the signal if there is one, assumes nothing about the mechanism, and works with every encoder (one-hot gives it a column, target encoding gives it its own rate, a tree can split it off).
  • Costs: one more level. If blanks are very rare it's a rare category with a noisy estimate (group it or smooth it). If training had no blanks but production does, “Missing” is an unseen category, so plan for it.

Mode (most frequent value): acceptable only in a narrow case.

  • When it's fine: the blanks are MCAR, so the blank rows are a random sample and the most common level is the best single guess; the share is small (a few percent), so the distortion is negligible; and the blank doesn't predict the target. Quick baselines and linear models where you'd rather not add a level fit this case.
  • What goes wrong otherwise: filled rows join the mode category, so it becomes a mixture of two populations. Its target rate, its encoding and its coefficient all drift towards the blanks' behaviour, so the biggest category's own signal gets corrupted. If the blanks were informative (MAR or MNAR), that information is erased. And if no level dominates, the mode is a poor guess: it's right only as often as its share.

A classifier-based imputer. Under MAR, predict the missing category from the other columns (employment type from income, age and region), trained on rows where it's observed and fitted on training data only. It's better than the mode when the other columns really predict the category. Costs: a model to maintain, and its hard guesses look more certain than they are; passing the predicted probabilities instead of one label keeps the uncertainty. Add an indicator if the blank itself predicts the target.

kNN. The majority category among the most similar training rows. The same trade-offs as for numbers: needs scaling, slow on big data.

Models that handle blanks themselves

XGBoost: a learned default direction. At each candidate split, the rows with a blank in that feature are tried on both sides. The split gain is computed twice, with the blanks' gradient sums \(G_{\text{miss}}, H_{\text{miss}}\) added to the left child, then to the right:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Gain} &= \tfrac12\Big[\frac{G_L^2}{H_L + \lambda} + \frac{G_R^2}{H_R + \lambda} \\ &\qquad - \frac{(G_L + G_R)^2}{H_L + H_R + \lambda}\Big] - \gamma \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{blanks left:}\quad G_L &\leftarrow G_L + G_{\text{miss}} \\ H_L &\leftarrow H_L + H_{\text{miss}} \\ \text{blanks right:}\quad G_R &\leftarrow G_R + G_{\text{miss}} \\ H_R &\leftarrow H_R + H_{\text{miss}} \end{aligned} \]

In words: \(G\) and \(H\) are the sums of the loss's first and second derivatives over the rows in a child, \(\lambda\) is the L2 penalty on leaf values, and \(\gamma\) the minimum gain a split must clear. The direction with the higher gain becomes that node's default, so blanks are routed wherever they look most like the data. Only the non-missing values are sorted and scanned, which also makes this fast on sparse data. Each node learns its own direction, so a blank can mean “risky” at one node and “safe” at another.

  • LightGBM does the same by default (and can be told to treat zeros as missing).
  • CatBoost treats a numeric blank as smaller than every value by default (“Min”), so a split can always put the blanks on one side. Categorical columns need blanks turned into an explicit string such as “Missing”, which becomes its own level.
  • sklearn: HistGradientBoosting learns the direction like XGBoost; plain decision trees (version 1.3 on) and random forests (1.4 on) accept NaN too.
  • Not handled natively: linear and logistic regression, SVMs, kNN, most neural nets. They need imputation.

Should you impute before boosting? Usually not: the learned direction is at least as good as a single fill value, and it keeps the signal. The trap: if a feature had no blanks in training, there was nothing to learn from, so a blank at serving goes to an arbitrary or majority side (sklearn sends it to the child with more rows). A pipeline bug that blanks a field can silently make risky rows look safe. Monitor missing rates.

Dropping rows or columns

  • Dropping rows is acceptable when the blanks are MCAR, few, and you have data to spare. It's biased under MAR and MNAR, since the rows you keep are no longer representative. That bias is about averages and the overall population (a mean income, a default rate). For a regression's coefficients there's an exception worth knowing: if whether a row is blank depends only on the model's features (even on the missing value itself) and not on the target, the complete rows still give unbiased coefficients, just noisier ones. Dropping rows does bias them when the blanks depend on the target. And it doesn't solve the production problem: the deployed model will still meet blanks, so you need a strategy for them anyway.
  • Dropping a column is reasonable when it's mostly blank and neither its values nor its blankness predict the target. Check first: a column that's 95% blank whose presence predicts default should become an indicator, not be deleted.
  • Rows with a missing target can't be used for supervised training. But check why they're missing: in credit, only approved applicants have outcomes, so the training data is a selected sample (the reject-inference problem).

Fit imputers on the training fold only

Imputers learn numbers (medians, the kNN pool, per-column models), so they sit in the pipeline: refitted on each training fold, and frozen from the training fit in production. Fitting one on all rows leaks a little for a median, more for an iterative imputer, which learns relationships between columns, and seriously for anything that uses the target.

Multiple imputation, for inference

A single imputation treats filled values as if they were observed. Coefficients may be fine, but their standard errors are too small, because the uncertainty about what the blanks really were has been ignored. When you need inference (confidence intervals, p-values, effect sizes), use multiple imputation:

  1. Create \(M\) completed datasets, each with its own random draws for the blanks (MICE with noise). \(M\) of 5 to 20 is common; use more when the share missing is large.
  2. Fit the same model on each.
  3. Pool the results with Rubin's rules:
\[ \begin{aligned} \bar Q &= \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} \hat Q_m \\ \bar W &= \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} W_m \\ B &= \frac{1}{M - 1} \sum_{m=1}^{M} \big(\hat Q_m - \bar Q\big)^2 \\ T &= \bar W + \Big(1 + \frac{1}{M}\Big) B \end{aligned} \]

In words: \(\hat Q_m\) is the estimate from dataset \(m\) and \(W_m\) its squared standard error. The pooled estimate \(\bar Q\) is their average. The total variance \(T\) adds the usual sampling variance \(\bar W\) to the disagreement between imputations \(B\), which is the uncertainty single imputation throws away. For pure prediction, multiple imputation rarely pays off; averaging predictions over the \(M\) datasets is at most a mild ensemble.

Prediction vs inference: why a fill can be fine for one and wrong for the other

The two goals ask different things of the filled data:

  • Prediction only cares whether \(\hat y\) is accurate on new rows. A coefficient can be “wrong” as long as the predictions are good.
  • Inference cares about the coefficients themselves (“how much does default risk rise per ₹10k of income?”) and their uncertainty: standard errors, confidence intervals, p-values.

A simulation. Two features \(x\) and \(z\) on the same scale (mean 0, standard deviation 1) with correlation 0.6, and \(y = 1 \cdot x + 1 \cdot z + \text{noise}\), so both true coefficients are 1. Then 40% of \(x\) is blanked, the gaps are handled in each way below, and \(y\) is regressed on \(x\) and \(z\): 1,000 rows, repeated 300 times. “CI covers” is how often the 95% confidence interval for \(x\)'s coefficient contains the true 1; it should be 95%. “Test RMSE” is the prediction error on 20,000 new rows whose blanks are made the same way; lower is better.

What the numbers say, step by step:

  1. A median fill biases the coefficients, and the correlated feature absorbs the missing effect. Take Case 1. In a blank row, \(x\) is a constant, so the only thing left to explain \(y\) is \(z\). Because \(z\) moves with \(x\) (on average \(x = 0.6\,z\)), the best slope on \(z\) in those rows is \(1 + 0.6 = 1.6\): its own 1, plus 0.6 standing in for \(x\). In the complete rows it's 1. One shared coefficient compromises at 1.31, and \(x\)'s coefficient shrinks to 0.81 to compensate. Both are wrong, and the confidence interval almost never covers the truth.
  2. The indicator fixes the level of the blank rows, not their slopes. It gives blank rows their own intercept \(\theta\) and nothing else. Under MNAR, the blank rows' \(x\) is high on average, and that level shift is most of the extra prediction error, so the indicator takes the test RMSE from 1.20 to 1.10 (a model that also gives blank rows their own slope on \(z\) reaches 1.085). That's “much of the damage to prediction goes away”. But the slope compromise on \(z\) is still there, so the coefficients stay biased (0.88 and 1.20, covering only 54%): “the damage to inference doesn't”. With \(x\) as the only feature (the setup in the indicator section), an intercept is all the blank rows need. The correlated \(z\) is what makes them need their own slope too.
  3. Under MCAR the indicator changes nothing, because blank rows aren't different on average, so there's no level to fix. Prediction was barely hurt anyway: 1.15, against 1.12 for the best a model can do with 40% of \(x\) blank (one that gives blank rows their own slope on \(z\)).
  4. Even an unbiased single fill understates the uncertainty. One random draw gets the coefficients right, but the regression treats the guessed values as real data. It reports a standard error of 0.040 (its estimate of how much the coefficient would move from sample to sample), but across the 300 repeats the coefficient actually moves by 0.053. The interval is too narrow, so it covers the truth only 87% of the time. Multiple imputation adds the disagreement between draws (\(B\) in Rubin's rules) and gets back to 94%.
  5. Multiple imputation assumes MAR. Under MNAR it's biased too (1.07, 75% coverage). It learns how \(x\) relates to \(z\) and \(y\) from the complete rows and fills the blanks with that. But among rows with the same \(z\) and \(y\), the blank ones have higher \(x\) (that's what MNAR means here), and nothing observed shows it. That's why MNAR calls for a sensitivity analysis, not just a better imputer: redo the estimate with the imputed values shifted up by a few plausible amounts, and check that the conclusion survives.

Say it in an interview: “For a prediction model, a median fill plus a missing indicator is usually fine: the indicator lets blank rows have their own level, and I judge it on validation error. If I need to interpret coefficients or report effects, a simple fill biases the coefficients of correlated features, and any single fill understates the uncertainty, so I'd use multiple imputation and pool with Rubin's rules. If the blanks might be MNAR, I'd add a sensitivity analysis.”

05Outliers

Two things share the name. An error is a wrong value (an age of 230, an amount in the wrong unit); a genuine extreme is right but rare (a very large legitimate payment). They need opposite treatment, so first tell them apart: check domain limits, the source, and whether the rest of the row agrees.

Detecting them

z-score. Flag values more than about 3 standard deviations from the mean:

\[ z_i = \frac{x_i - \bar x}{s} \]

In words: how many standard deviations a value is from the average. It assumes a roughly normal shape, and it suffers from masking: the outliers inflate \(s\) and drag \(\bar x\) towards themselves, which shrinks their own z-scores. In a sample of \(n\) points no z-score can exceed \((n-1)/\sqrt n\), so with ten or fewer points a z above 3 is impossible, however wild the value.

Robust z-score, with the MAD. Replace the mean with the median \(\tilde x\) and the standard deviation with the median absolute deviation:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{MAD} &= \operatorname{median}_i \big\lvert x_i - \tilde x \big\rvert \\ z_i^{\text{robust}} &= \frac{0.6745\,(x_i - \tilde x)}{\text{MAD}} \end{aligned} \]

In words: the median and the MAD barely move when a few values are extreme, so the outliers can't hide themselves. The 0.6745 makes the score comparable to an ordinary z for normal data (the standard deviation is about 1.4826 MAD). A common flag is \(\lvert z^{\text{robust}} \rvert > 3.5\).

IQR fences (Tukey).

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{IQR} &= Q_3 - Q_1 \\ \text{lower fence} &= Q_1 - 1.5\,\text{IQR} \\ \text{upper fence} &= Q_3 + 1.5\,\text{IQR} \end{aligned} \]

In words: the middle half of the data sets the scale, and anything more than one and a half middle-halves beyond it is flagged (3 IQR for “far out”). Quartiles are robust and assume no distribution, but on a right-skewed column such as income the fences flag a large part of the perfectly real long tail. Take the log first, then fence.

Multivariate methods, for points that are normal on every column but odd in combination (a large amount at 3 a.m. from a new device):

  • Isolation forest: random trees with random splits. An outlier sits in a sparse region, so it's isolated in few splits; the score is the average path length. Fast and scalable; its expected-outlier-share setting fixes the threshold, and it struggles when anomalies form dense clusters of their own.
  • Local outlier factor (LOF): compares a point's density with its neighbours', so it finds points that are odd for their region. Slow on big data and sensitive to the number of neighbours.
  • Residual-based: rows with large standardized residuals from a model. In linear regression also check leverage (an unusual \(x\)) and Cook's distance (how much the fit moves without the row).
  • Domain rules: limits no statistic needs to find, such as non-negative quantities and dates not in the future.

What outliers do to statistics and models

  • Mean, standard deviation, scaling: one extreme value shifts the mean and inflates the standard deviation more, so standardization squeezes every normal value into a narrow band. Min-max is worse: one extreme sets the range.
  • Linear models with squared loss: a row's loss grows with its squared residual, so its pull on the fit grows with the residual, without limit. One far point can tilt the line, and a high-leverage point can rotate it. Distance methods (k-means, kNN) get pulled too.
  • Trees, extreme inputs: robust. Splits use only the order of values, so an extreme \(x\) just lands at one end.
  • Trees, extreme targets: not robust. With squared error, a leaf is a mean that one extreme target pulls, and in boosting the largest residuals dominate the gradients. Use a Huber or absolute-error loss.

Treating them

  • Errors: fix or remove. Correct from the source if you can; otherwise set the value to missing and let the missing-value strategy handle it, rather than dropping the row.
  • Cap (winsorize). Clip to percentiles learned on training data, such as the 1st and 99th. The row stays with limited influence, but you lose how extreme it was; add a “was capped” flag if many rows hit the cap.
  • Transform. A log, Box-Cox or Yeo-Johnson transform pulls in a long tail without deleting anything (section 07).
  • Use a robust loss or model. Huber loss is squared for small residuals and linear for large ones, so a far point pulls with constant force instead of growing force:
\[ L_\delta(r) = \begin{cases} \tfrac12 r^2 & \lvert r \rvert \le \delta \\ \delta \big(\lvert r \rvert - \tfrac12 \delta\big) & \lvert r \rvert > \delta \end{cases} \]

In words: \(r\) is the residual and \(\delta\) the point where the loss switches from squared to linear. Absolute error (which predicts the median) and quantile losses are more robust still.

  • Keep them when they are the signal. In fraud, anomaly detection or large insurance claims, the extremes are what the model exists to find; removing them removes the positives. And production will contain extremes anyway, so the model must behave sensibly on them.

Trap

Removing outliers from the test set. It makes the score look better while the model meets the same extremes in production. Decide the outlier rule on training data, then apply it identically everywhere, including at serving time.

06Encoding categorical features

Most models need numbers. An encoding decides what a model can learn from a category: whether it sees an order, how many parameters the category costs, and whether the labels were used to build it. The right choice depends on the model and on the cardinality (the number of levels).

One-hot encoding

One 0/1 column per level. No order is invented, and each level gets its own coefficient.

  • The dummy-variable trap. With an intercept, the \(K\) one-hot columns always sum to 1, which is exactly the intercept column. The columns are perfectly collinear, \(X^\top X\) can't be inverted, and an unregularized linear model has no unique solution. Drop one level (the reference); each remaining coefficient then means “difference from the reference level”.
  • With regularization, keep all \(K\). The penalty picks a unique solution and treats every level symmetrically, whereas dropping one makes the result depend on which level you dropped. Trees and neural nets don't need a level dropped either.
  • High cardinality hurts. Thousands of sparse columns; rare levels get noisy coefficients; memory grows. A tree can only peel off one level per split, so a high-cardinality feature's signal is spread thin across many weak columns and needs many splits to use.
  • Best for: a handful to a few dozen levels, in linear models and neural nets.

Ordinal (label) encoding

One integer per level.

  • Truly ordered levels (education, small/medium/large, credit grades A to E): in a linear model, one slope then covers all levels, which assumes the steps between neighbours are equal. One-hot is the flexible alternative, at the cost of more parameters.
  • Unordered levels in a linear model: wrong. The model treats code 7 as seven times code 1, and the result depends on whatever order (often alphabetical) produced the codes.
  • Unordered levels in a tree: workable. A tree can cut the integer line several times, so it can still separate levels. But each cut splits a contiguous range of codes, so grouping risky levels that happen to sit far apart in the code order costs many splits. Ordering the codes by target rate fixes that, which is where target encoding comes in.
  • (sklearn's LabelEncoder is meant for the target; use OrdinalEncoder for features.)

Target (mean) encoding

Replace each level with the average target of the training rows in that level: the default rate of a postcode, the average basket value of a merchant category. For a multi-class target, use one column per class (the class's share in that level).

Why it works. It turns any number of levels into one numeric column ordered by risk. For a yes/no target, sorting the levels by their rate and trying each cut in that order is guaranteed to find the best split of the levels into two groups (Breiman's result), so one split in a tree does what one-hot needs dozens for. LightGBM's native categorical splits use the same idea, sorting levels by their gradient statistics. For a linear model, it turns a nominal column into one monotone feature.

Smoothing. A level seen 3 times has an average that is mostly noise. Shrink it towards the overall mean \(\bar y\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{enc}(c) &= \frac{n_c\, \bar y_c + m\, \bar y}{n_c + m} \\ &= \lambda_c\, \bar y_c + (1 - \lambda_c)\, \bar y \\ \lambda_c &= \frac{n_c}{n_c + m} \end{aligned} \]

In words: \(n_c\) is the level's row count, \(\bar y_c\) its average target, and \(m\) the prior weight: the overall mean counts as \(m\) extra rows. A big level keeps essentially its own average; a tiny one is pulled to the overall mean; a level never seen gets exactly \(\bar y\). Tune \(m\) by cross-validation, or set it from the data: the noisier the target within a level, the larger \(m\) should be (the empirical-Bayes idea). sklearn's TargetEncoder by default sets \(m\) per level to the level's target variance divided by the overall target variance.

Why it leaks. Computed on the same rows the model trains on, each row's own label is inside its level's average. For a level with one row, the encoding is the label. For a level with 10 rows, a positive row raises its own encoding by a tenth.

The model learns “high encoding means positive”, which holds in training because of the row's own label and fails on new rows. Even a category with no real relationship to the target shows a strong training signal, and the effect is largest for small levels. Smoothing reduces it but doesn't remove it.

The fix: out-of-fold encoding. Split the training rows into \(K\) folds. Encode each fold's rows using averages computed from the other \(K - 1\) folds, so no row's label touches its own encoding. Train the model on these out-of-fold encodings. For validation, test and production rows, use averages from all the training rows.

  • Leave-one-out encoding is a trap. Excluding only the row itself sounds safe:
    \[ \text{enc}_{-i} = \frac{S_c - y_i}{n_c - 1} \]
    Here \(S_c\) is the level's sum of targets. Within one level \(S_c\) and \(n_c\) are the same for every row, so the encoding is a decreasing function of \(y_i\): every positive gets a slightly lower value than every negative in its level. A deep enough tree can read the label level by level. Out-of-fold encoding doesn't have this problem: rows of a level in the same fold share one value, computed without any of their labels. (A faint fold-level trace remains; smoothing and enough rows per fold make it negligible.)
  • It must sit inside cross-validation. If you compute the out-of-fold encodings on all the data and then run cross-validation, the validation fold's labels have already shaped the training rows' encodings. The encoder goes in the pipeline, so it's refitted on each training fold. sklearn's TargetEncoder does the out-of-fold part itself: fit_transform cross-fits, while transform on new rows uses the full training averages. Calling fit and then transform on the training rows yourself skips the cross-fitting and leaks.
  • Time-ordered data: encode each row from rows strictly earlier in time (an expanding average), the way the value would have been computed live.
  • CatBoost's ordered target statistics do the same job differently. Shuffle the training rows into a random order and encode each row from the rows before it, with a prior counted as \(a\) extra rows:
    \[ \text{enc}_i = \frac{S_{\text{before}} + a \cdot \text{prior}}{n_{\text{before}} + a} \]
    Here \(S_{\text{before}}\) and \(n_{\text{before}}\) are the sum of targets and the row count of the same level among the rows earlier in the order. A row's label never enters its own encoding, and no rows are wasted. The first rows of each level get noisy encodings, so CatBoost uses several random orders. Its “ordered boosting” applies the same before-only idea to the gradients.

Trade-offs. Target encoding is compact and handles any cardinality. Its costs: the leakage risk above; noise for rare levels; drift, since a level's rate changes over time; one encoding per target (and per class); and one number per level, so any interaction between the level and other features has to be learned elsewhere.

Frequency and count encoding

\[ \text{freq}(c) = \frac{n_c}{n} \]

In words: replace each level with how often it appears in the training data (its count, or its share).

  • Why it works: popularity is often predictive. A merchant seen millions of times is an established business; a device model seen twice is unusual, which matters in fraud.
  • Why it's safe: it never uses the target, so there's no target leakage, and it's one column whatever the cardinality.
  • When: high-cardinality features in tree models, usually next to a target encoding (how common versus how risky); unsupervised and anomaly models, which have no target.
  • Trade-offs: levels with equal counts become identical. Counts depend on data volume and window, so use shares or a fixed trailing window (“transactions at this merchant in the last 30 days”), computed the same way at serving. An unseen level gets 0, which is usually meaningful.

Hashing, binary encoding and embeddings

Hashing. Map each level to one of \(B\) columns with a hash function, \(j = h(c) \bmod B\), and set that column to 1.

  • Why: no vocabulary to store, a fixed number of columns, and new levels are handled automatically. It suits huge or open-ended vocabularies (URLs, user agents, crossed features) in sparse linear models and streaming systems.
  • Cost: collisions. Unrelated levels share a column, so their effects blur together. The chance that a given level shares its bucket with at least one of the other \(K - 1\) levels is
\[ 1 - \Big(1 - \frac{1}{B}\Big)^{K - 1} \approx 1 - e^{-K/B} \]

In words: to keep collisions rare you need many more buckets than levels. Hashing also can't be inverted, so it's the wrong tool when you need to interpret levels, or when the vocabulary is small enough to store.

Binary encoding writes each level's integer code in binary, one column per bit (about \(\log_2 K\) columns). Compact, but each bit groups unrelated levels, so it's mostly a memory trick.

Learned embeddings. In a neural net, each level gets a dense vector trained with the task, so levels that behave alike end up close and rare levels borrow strength from similar ones; the vectors can be reused as tree features. A common size heuristic is about \(\min(50, K/2)\). Map levels below a count threshold to a shared “rare” index, which unseen levels then use. Not for small data or non-neural models, unless the vectors are pre-trained.

Rare and unseen categories

  • Group rare levels. Merge levels with fewer than some number of training rows into “Other”. It cuts noise and columns, and it gives unseen levels a natural home. The threshold is a hyperparameter, and it's learned from training data, so it belongs in the pipeline.
  • Plan for unseen levels at serving time, encoder by encoder: one-hot gives an all-zero row or the “Other” column; target encoding the prior; count encoding 0; embeddings the shared “unknown” index; hashing some bucket. Decide on purpose and test it: a silent mismatch between training and serving codes is a classic production bug.
  • Monitor the share of unseen levels. A jump means the data changed: a new product, a renamed category, a broken upstream mapping.

Transforms for skewed columns

Incomes, amounts, counts and durations are usually right-skewed: most values are small and a few are huge. A transform pulls the tail in.

  • Log (or \(\log(1 + x)\) when zeros occur): turns multiplicative effects into additive ones (“10% more income” becomes a fixed step), reduces the leverage of the tail, and often makes a relationship more linear. \(\log x\) needs \(x > 0\); \(\log(1 + x)\) allows zeros.
  • Box-Cox picks the power that makes the result as close to normal as possible, by maximum likelihood. It requires \(x > 0\):
\[ x^{(\lambda)} = \begin{cases} \dfrac{x^{\lambda} - 1}{\lambda} & \lambda \ne 0 \\ \log x & \lambda = 0 \end{cases} \]

In words: \(\lambda = 1\) only shifts the values, \(\lambda = 0.5\) is a rescaled square root, and \(\lambda = 0\) is the log. The \(-1\) and \(/\lambda\) make the family join smoothly at \(\lambda = 0\).

  • Yeo-Johnson extends the same idea to zero and negative values (profits, balance changes).
  • Quantile transform maps each value to its rank, then to a uniform or normal shape. The most robust (outliers become merely the top ranks), but it bends linear relationships, distorts distances, and clips values beyond the training range.

When transforms matter: for linear models, distance-based methods and neural nets. Trees ignore any monotone transform, since a threshold on \(\log x\) is a threshold on \(x\). Box-Cox's \(\lambda\) and the quantiles are learned, so fit them on training rows.

Transforming the target is different: a model trained on \(\log y\) predicts roughly the median of \(y\), not the mean, so exponentiated predictions underestimate totals unless corrected (Duan's smearing: multiply them by the average of the exponentiated training residuals).

08Collinearity

Collinearity (multicollinearity) means some features are nearly linear combinations of others: floor area and number of rooms; income and an income-derived credit limit. A total next to its parts, or all one-hot dummies next to an intercept, is perfect collinearity.

Why it hurts linear models

When two features move together, the data can't tell which one deserves the credit. The loss surface becomes a long, flat valley along “more weight on one, less on the other”, and the fitted coefficients can slide along it. For ordinary least squares:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_j) &= \frac{\sigma^2}{\sum_i (x_{ij} - \bar x_j)^2} \cdot \text{VIF}_j \\ \text{VIF}_j &= \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2} \end{aligned} \]

In words: \(\sigma^2\) is the noise variance, and the first factor is the variance \(\hat\beta_j\) would have if \(x_j\) were uncorrelated with the rest. \(R_j^2\) is how well the other features predict feature \(j\). Only the part of \(x_j\) the others can't predict helps pin down \(\beta_j\). The variance inflation factor (VIF) says how many times larger the coefficient's variance is than if \(x_j\) were uncorrelated with the rest; its square root is how many times wider the standard error is.

  • Unstable coefficients: large standard errors, big changes when a few rows change, and signs that can flip against domain sense.
  • Misleading tests: each feature can look insignificant alone while the pair is clearly significant together (an F-test on both shows it).
  • Interpretation breaks: “the effect of \(x_1\) holding \(x_2\) fixed” describes something the data never shows.
  • Predictions are usually fine while the correlation holds, because they depend on the combined effect, which the data pins down well. A new row that breaks the pattern asks about the one direction the data never covered, and its prediction swings.

Detecting it

  • Correlation matrix: quick but pairwise only; it misses a column that is the sum of three others.
  • VIF per feature (regress it on all the others): above 5 is worth a look, above 10 is serious.
  • Condition number of the standardized feature matrix (largest over smallest singular value): above about 30 is strong collinearity. Eigenvectors of the correlation matrix with near-zero eigenvalues show which columns are involved.

Remedies

  • Do nothing if you only need predictions and the relationship is stable.
  • Drop one of a redundant pair, or combine them (a sum, ratio or domain index).
  • PCA: uncorrelated components, at the cost of interpretability.
  • Ridge: the penalty curves the flat valley, so the coefficients stop sliding and the twins share the weight. Lasso keeps one twin somewhat arbitrarily (unstable selection); elastic net keeps the group.
  • More varied data is the only fix that makes the separate effects estimable.

Trees and feature importance

Trees predict fine with correlated features, but their importance is split. Whichever twin wins a split takes its credit, so impurity importance divides the credit arbitrarily and each twin looks weaker than the signal is. Permutation importance underestimates both, because with one twin shuffled the model still has the other (and the shuffled rows are unrealistic). Random forests, which sample features per split, spread credit evenly; boosting tends to lean on one twin. Measure correlated features as a group: permute them together, or drop the group and refit.

09Feature engineering

Feature engineering hands a model a quantity it would struggle to build itself. The test of a good feature: is it known at prediction time, does it have a reason to predict the target, and would it be computed the same way in production?

Dates and times

  • Components and durations: hour, day of week, month, weekend, holiday, days to or from an event (payday, a festival, the month end); and durations up to the prediction time, such as account age or days since the last purchase, which carry far more than raw dates.
  • Don't use a raw timestamp as a feature: the model learns the trend in the training period and can't extrapolate it to future dates.
  • Cyclical encoding for features that wrap around:
\[ \begin{aligned} h_{\sin} &= \sin(2\pi h / 24) \\ h_{\cos} &= \cos(2\pi h / 24) \end{aligned} \]

In words: the hour \(h\) becomes a point on a circle, so 23:00 and 00:00 are neighbours instead of 23 apart. Both coordinates are needed, because the sine alone gives the same value to two different hours (3:00 and 9:00 share a sine and differ only in the cosine). This matters for linear models, neural nets and distances; a tree can isolate a late-night window from the raw hour with two splits, so it gains less.

Group aggregates

Statistics of an entity's history: a customer's average transaction amount, a merchant's number of distinct customers, a device's count of accounts. Often the strongest features, especially as deviations: this amount divided by the customer's usual amount.

Their leakage risks:

  • The current row's label: “the merchant's fraud rate” over rows that include this transaction is unprotected target encoding. Compute it out-of-fold or from earlier rows only.
  • Future rows: a customer's average over the whole dataset includes later transactions. Compute every aggregate as of the prediction time (a point-in-time or “as-of” join).
  • Pooling train and test: lets test rows shape training features.
  • Small groups: an average over two rows is noise; smooth it towards the overall average and add the count as a feature.

Ratios, interactions and binning

  • Ratios such as debt-to-income or loan-to-value. A tree approximates a ratio only with a staircase of many splits, and a linear model can't form it from the raw columns (only from logs, as \(\log a - \log b\)). Define the zero-denominator case, with an indicator.
  • Interactions (products, crossed categories): a linear model sees one only if you add it; trees find them by nesting splits, but an explicit one helps shallow trees. Let domain knowledge pick them, since all pairwise products explode the feature count.
  • Binning (equal-width or quantile): gives a linear model a step-shaped, non-linear effect and limits outliers' influence; credit scorecards replace each bin by its weight of evidence (the log of the bin's share of good outcomes over its share of bad ones). Costs: variation within a bin is lost and edges create jumps (splines are the smooth alternative). Fit edges on training rows. Trees don't need binning.

Time series: lags and rolling windows, with no future

Lags (yesterday's value, last week's value), rolling means and standard deviations, expanding averages, differences and exponentially weighted averages. The rule: a feature for time \(t\) may use only what was known at time \(t\). If a source arrives two days late, the shortest usable lag is two days. The classic bug is a rolling window that includes the current row, whose target is exactly what you're predicting:

df = df.sort_values("date")
g = df.groupby("store")["sales"]
df["lag_7"] = g.shift(7)
# shift first, so the window ends yesterday
df["mean_28"] = g.transform(
    lambda s: s.shift(1).rolling(28).mean())

Centred windows (which average the past and the future around a point) are fine for smoothing a chart and always a leak in a feature.

Basic text features

Cheap signals (length, word count, share of capitals or digits, keyword flags); TF-IDF with a vocabulary fitted on training text, into a sparse linear model, as a strong baseline; character n-grams for messy short strings such as merchant names; sentence embeddings from a pretrained model as dense features. Watch for text written after the event, such as resolution notes, which often contains the label.

10Feature selection

Why select at all: with many weak features and few rows, extra features add variance; fewer features are cheaper to compute, monitor and keep correct; and there's less surface for leakage. With gradient boosting and plenty of data the accuracy gain is often small, so start by removing junk (IDs, leaky columns, constants) and keep the rest.

  • Filters score each feature on its own: correlation with the target, mutual information, a chi-squared or ANOVA F test, a variance threshold. Mutual information measures how much knowing \(X\) reduces the uncertainty about \(Y\), with \(H\) the entropy (uncertainty):
    \[ I(X; Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X) \]
    It's 0 only when they're independent, so it catches non-linear relationships that correlation misses. Filters are fast, but they judge one feature at a time: they miss features that work only together, and keep two copies of one signal.
  • Wrappers search subsets with the model itself: recursive feature elimination (RFE) fits, drops the weakest features, refits and repeats. They see combinations, but they're expensive and overfit the selection score unless nested inside cross-validation.
  • Embedded methods select while training:
    • L1 (lasso) sets weak coefficients exactly to 0. With correlated features it keeps one more or less at random, so repeat it on bootstrap samples and keep features chosen consistently (stability selection).
    • Tree impurity importance is free, but it's computed on training data and favours continuous and high-cardinality features, which offer more split points to fit noise with. A random ID column can rank highly.
    • Permutation importance shuffles one feature in validation data and measures the drop in score. It works for any model without that bias, but correlated features hide each other's importance.

The pitfall: select inside cross-validation, not before it. Take thousands of pure-noise features and random labels, keep the few most correlated with the labels on all the rows, then cross-validate a model on them: the accuracy comes out well above chance. The selection step saw the validation labels and picked features that happen to fit them.

Selection is part of the training procedure, so it goes in the pipeline and is redone on each training fold, and the final estimate comes from data the selection never saw.

11Class imbalance at the data level

First ask whether it's a problem. With a proper loss (log loss) and a few hundred positives, a model ranks well despite imbalance; what changes is where the threshold belongs and which metric to read. So: threshold first, then weights, then resampling.

  • Move the threshold. With calibrated probabilities, choose it from the costs of the two mistakes, or from capacity (flag the top \(N\) a review team can handle). Flag when \(p > t^*\):
    \[ t^* = \frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP} + C_{FN}} \]
    In words: with \(C_{FP}\) the cost of a false alarm and \(C_{FN}\) the cost of a miss, the threshold is the false alarm's share of the total cost. When a miss costs far more, the threshold is low.
  • Class weights. Count each positive \(w\) times in the loss. It works with every model and discards nothing. But the model now believes positives are \(w\) times more likely in odds terms, so probabilities are inflated; at threshold 0.5 it decides about the same as the unweighted model at threshold \(1/(1 + w)\).
  • Undersample the majority. Keep a fraction \(q\) of negatives. Training gets much cheaper, at the cost of discarded information. Undo the probability inflation with the prior correction:
    \[ \operatorname{logit} p = \operatorname{logit} p_s + \log q \]
    In words: \(p_s\) is the model's probability on the resampled data; adding \(\log q\) to its log-odds restores the real base rate. For class weights, use \(q = 1/w\).
  • Oversample the minority by duplicating positives: the same effect as weights, but duplicates are easy to memorize, especially for trees.
  • SMOTE creates synthetic positives on the line between a positive and one of its positive nearest neighbours:
    \[ \begin{aligned} x_{\text{new}} &= x_i + u\,(x_{\text{nn}} - x_i) \\ u &\sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1) \end{aligned} \]
    Apply it only to training folds, after the split (imbalanced-learn's pipeline does this); before the split, synthetic near-copies of validation rows leak into training. Its points can be unrealistic (half-way values of integer or categorical columns, points between two separate fraud patterns), it amplifies label noise, it distorts probabilities, and with gradient boosting it rarely beats weights or a good threshold.

The right metrics: not accuracy (flagging nobody scores 98% at 2% positives). Use PR-AUC (its random baseline is the positive rate), precision and recall at the operating threshold, or money saved; ROC-AUC ignores the base rate and can look excellent while precision is poor. Add log loss or a calibration plot when probabilities matter. Never resample the validation or test sets: they must reflect the real base rate.

12Overfitting and underfitting

Expected squared error on a new point splits into three parts:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E\big[(y - \hat f(x))^2\big] &= \text{bias}^2 + \text{variance} + \sigma^2 \\ \text{bias} &= \mathbb E[\hat f(x)] - f(x) \\ \text{variance} &= \operatorname{Var}\big(\hat f(x)\big) \end{aligned} \]

In words: bias is how far the average model (over many training sets) is from the truth, so a model too simple for the pattern has high bias. Variance is how much the model changes from one training set to another, so a model flexible enough to fit noise has high variance. The noise can't be removed by any model.

  • Learning curve (both scores against training-set size): curves that meet low mean underfitting, and more data won't help; a gap that narrows as data grows means overfitting, and more data will.
  • Validation curve (both scores against one complexity setting, such as tree depth): pick the validation peak, or the simplest setting near it.
What you seeDiagnosisFixes
Training and validation both poor, small gapUnderfitting (high bias)Better features (ratios, aggregates, interactions); more capacity; less regularization; train longer; check for label noise
Training good, validation much worseOverfitting (high variance)More data; fewer or cleaner features; group rare levels and smooth encodings; stronger regularization; shallower trees, early stopping; bagging
Validation good, test or production much worseLeakage, a shift, or overfitting the validation set through many tuning roundsAudit features for post-prediction information; time or group split; adversarial validation; a fresh holdout and fewer tuning rounds
Fold scores vary a lotSmall or mixed dataRepeated or grouped cross-validation; simpler model; report the spread, not just the mean

A gap isn't always overfitting. If a feature leaks only in training rows, or the validation period differs from the training period, the gap comes from the data, not the model's flexibility, and regularization won't close it.

def target_encode_oof(cat, y, n_folds=5, m=20):
    enc = empty(len(y))
    for fit_idx, enc_idx in KFold(n_folds, shuffle=True):
        prior = mean(y[fit_idx])
        s, n = sum_and_count_by_level(cat[fit_idx],
                                      y[fit_idx])
        for i in enc_idx:          # own label never used
            c = cat[i]             # unseen here: s = n = 0

14Interview questions

Answer out loud first, then open the answer. Each answer stands on its own: the short answer, the mechanism, the why, then the trade-off or the likely follow-up.

1You get a new tabular dataset. What do you do before modelling?

Pin down what a row and the target are, run a checklist for anything that would make the model wrong or falsely good, clean what's broken, and split before fitting anything that learns from the data.

  • The grain and the target: what one row is (an application, a customer-month); how the target is defined, its base rate, and when it becomes known. That moment is the prediction time, and anything recorded after it is leakage.
  • The checklist: missing share per column, columns that go blank together, blank rates over time, and the target rate when blank; duplicates and repeated entities; cardinality and ID-like columns; distributions, skew, sentinel values (−1, 9999), impossible values; drift by month; columns that look recorded after the prediction time, or that predict the target almost perfectly alone.
  • Cleaning: remove artefact duplicates, merge category spellings, fix types and units, turn sentinels and impossible values into proper blanks, check that labels are mature.
  • Then split the way deployment works (by time, by group, stratified), and put every step that learns a number (imputer, encoder, scaler, selection) in a pipeline fitted on training folds.

Why: each check feeds a later decision: the split, the imputation, the encoding, the transforms, the metric. Follow-up: adversarial validation (a classifier trained to tell training rows from test rows) checks whether the two look alike; a high AUC means drift or a time-encoding column. (01, 02)

2What is data leakage? Give examples.

Leakage is information in training or evaluation that won't be available at prediction time. The model learns a shortcut, offline scores look great, and production disappoints.

  • Target leakage: a feature recorded after the label or caused by it. “Number of collections calls” when predicting default; “chargeback filed” when predicting fraud; resolution notes in a ticket-escalation model.
  • Train–test contamination: duplicates or the same customer on both sides of the split; oversampling or SMOTE before splitting, so copies of one row land in both.
  • Preprocessing fitted on all the data: a scaler, imputer, target encoder or feature-selection step fitted before the split or outside cross-validation. Small for scaling; large for target encoding and selection, which use the labels.
  • Temporal leakage: a random split of time-ordered data; a customer's lifetime average that includes later transactions; joining today's value of a slowly changing table instead of its value at the prediction time.

How to spot it: a score too good for the problem; one feature dominating importance; a single feature that predicts almost perfectly; a random split scoring far above a time-based one. The fixes: for every feature, ask when it becomes known; build features point-in-time; split the way deployment works; and keep every fitted step in a pipeline. (03)

3Why fit the scaler on the training data only?

The scaler's mean and standard deviation are learned parameters. Fitted on test rows, they carry test information into training, and in production you couldn't compute them anyway.

The mechanism: fit \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) on the training rows, then apply \((x - \mu)/\sigma\) with those same numbers to validation, test and live rows. In cross-validation, the scaler sits inside the pipeline so it's refitted on each training fold.

  • Evaluate what you deploy. In production the scaler is frozen from training, and it often sees one row at a time. An evaluation that re-fits on the test set measures a procedure you can't run.
  • Leakage. For scaling it's small. The habit is what matters: the same order of steps with a target encoder or feature selection inflates the score a lot.
  • It hides shift. If test incomes run higher, re-centring the test set on its own mean makes them look normal, and the evaluation misses the drift production will have.

Follow-up: “production drifts, should the scaler refit on live data?” Not on its own: the model's weights were learned for the training scaling. Monitor drift and retrain scaler and model together. (07)

4How do you split time-series data?

Train on the past and validate on the future, with a gap the length of the label horizon, and keep a final out-of-time test period. Never shuffle.

  • Folds: an expanding window (all history up to a cut-off) or a rolling one (the most recent stretch only, when old data is stale), each followed by a gap, then the next period for validation.
  • The gap: if the label is “default within 12 months”, the last training rows' labels overlap the validation period; the gap removes the overlap. It should also cover reporting delays.
  • Features: lags and rolling windows must end before the prediction time. Shift before rolling, or the window includes the row being predicted.

Why a random split fails: future trends and regimes leak backwards; neighbouring time points are near-duplicates, so validation becomes interpolation rather than forecasting; and it doesn't match deployment, where you always predict forward. Trade-offs: early folds have little training data, and a rolling window throws away old data (good under drift, less data otherwise). With repeated entities, also keep each entity's rows together. (03, 09)

5MCAR vs MAR vs MNAR, and why it matters.

They say what makes a value missing: nothing at all (MCAR), other columns you can see (MAR), or the missing value itself (MNAR). The mechanism decides which handling is unbiased.

  • MCAR (missing completely at random): a sensor drops readings at random. The complete rows are a random sample, so dropping rows adds no bias, it only loses data; a mean fill keeps the mean right but shrinks the variance and the correlations.
  • MAR (missing at random, given observed data): income is blank more often for self-employed applicants, and employment type is recorded. Among the self-employed, high and low earners skip it equally. Dropping rows under-represents the self-employed; imputing income from employment type and other columns removes the bias.
  • MNAR (missing not at random): high earners skip the income field because it's high, so the value causes its own blank. Imputing from other columns stays biased, since even look-alike rows differ in the hidden value; the blank itself carries the information, so keep it as a feature (an indicator or a “Missing” level).
  • The test that separates MAR from MNAR: take two applicants who match on every observed column. If learning that one left income blank changes your guess of that person's income, it's MNAR. Whether it's MAR or MNAR also depends on which columns you record: if doctors skip the field, it's MAR when occupation is a column and MNAR when it isn't.

Diagnosing: compare rows with and without the value (other columns' distributions, target rate); train a classifier to predict the blank from the other columns (AUC near 0.5 is consistent with MCAR). MAR vs MNAR can't be settled from the data, since it's about values you never see; it needs domain knowledge or a sample where the true values are obtained.

Follow-up: for a prediction model, what matters most is that blanks arise the same way at serving; then the blank can be a feature even under MNAR. For inference (coefficients, effects), MNAR needs assumptions the data can't check, so you'd run a sensitivity analysis: redo the estimate assuming the missing incomes are, say, 10–20% higher than imputed (since high earners hide), and check that the conclusion survives. (04)

6How do you impute a categorical feature? When is mode imputation acceptable?

Default to an explicit “Missing” category. Use the mode only when values are missing completely at random, the share is small, and the blank doesn't predict the target.

First check why it's missing, and whether the target rate differs between blank and non-blank rows. Then:

  • “Missing” as its own level: keeps any signal, assumes nothing about the mechanism, and works with every encoder (one-hot gives it a column, target encoding its own rate, a tree can split it off).
  • Mode: under MCAR the blank rows are a random sample, so the most common level is the best single guess, and with a few percent filled the distortion is negligible. Fine for baselines, or for a linear model where you'd rather not add a level.
  • A classifier imputer under MAR: predict the category from the other columns (employment type from income, age, region), trained where it's observed, on training rows only. Passing its probabilities keeps the uncertainty. A kNN majority vote is the local version.
  • Native handling: sklearn's HistGradientBoosting treats a categorical blank as its own category; LightGBM sends it, like any level not listed in a split, to the “other” side; CatBoost needs it turned into a string such as “Missing” first.

Why mode fails outside that case: the filled rows join the most common level, which becomes a mixture of two populations, so its target rate, encoding and coefficient drift towards the blanks' behaviour. Any signal in the blank is erased, and if no level dominates, the mode is right only as often as its share.

Trade-off: “Missing” adds a level, which may be rare (smooth or group it) and is unseen at serving if training had no blanks. Whatever you choose is fitted on training folds and applied identically at serving. (04)

7When would you add a missing indicator?

When missingness might carry information (the target rate differs for blank rows, or MNAR is plausible), especially when you fill blanks with a simple value for a linear model or a neural net.

The mechanism: fill with a constant \(c\), giving the filled column \(\tilde x\) (\(x\) where observed, \(c\) where blank), and add a 0/1 column \(I\) that is 1 where the value was blank. In a linear model:

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat y &= \beta_0 + \beta\, \tilde x + \theta I \\ \text{observed rows:}\quad \hat y &= \beta_0 + \beta x \\ \text{blank rows:}\quad \hat y &= \beta_0 + \beta c + \theta \end{aligned} \]

In words: the blank rows get their own intercept through \(\theta\), so the slope is pinned only by real values, and without regularization (and before any scaling) the fill value \(c\) doesn't matter. Without \(I\), blank rows are forced onto the line at \(x = c\) and bend the slope.

Why: under MNAR the blank is the information; the indicator also tells the model which values are real. When to skip it: boosting libraries already learn where blanks go at each split; a tiny missing share gives an almost-constant column; if the collection process will change (the field becomes mandatory), the learned “blank means risky” effect goes stale; and when many columns go blank together, use one indicator per source, not per column.

Trade-off: the model now depends on how data is collected, so monitor missing rates: a pipeline bug that blanks a field shifts scores without an error. (04)

8What does mean or median imputation do to the data?

It keeps the centre but shrinks the spread, weakens every correlation, and puts a spike at the fill value. Under MAR it's biased, and it erases any signal in the blank.

With a share \(r\) missing completely at random and filled with the mean, for the filled column \(\tilde x\) and any other column \(z\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(\tilde x) &\approx (1 - r)\, \operatorname{Var}(x) \\ \operatorname{Corr}(\tilde x, z) &\approx \sqrt{1 - r}\; \operatorname{Corr}(x, z) \end{aligned} \]

In words: every filled value sits exactly at the centre, so it adds nothing to the spread and nothing to any co-movement with other columns.

  • Mean or median: the median for skewed columns such as income, where the mean is pulled by the tail; the mean keeps the column's average unchanged under MCAR.
  • In models: with several correlated features, the others absorb the missing part of \(x\), biasing their coefficients; standard errors come out too small, as if the guesses were data. For a tree, the blanks merge into the median's neighbourhood, so a blank that meant “risky” now looks average.

Fixes: add a missing indicator (most of the damage to prediction goes away), use model-based imputation under MAR, and multiple imputation when you need standard errors. Always fit the median on training rows. (04)

9How do XGBoost and LightGBM handle missing values? Should you impute first?

At every split they try sending the blank rows left and right and keep whichever gives the higher gain, a learned default direction per node. So usually you shouldn't impute first.

The mechanism. \(G\) and \(H\) are the sums of the loss's first and second derivatives over the rows in a child, \(\lambda\) is the L2 penalty on leaf values and \(\gamma\) the cost of a split. Only non-missing values are sorted and scanned, and the blanks' sums are added to one side, then the other:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Gain} &= \tfrac12\Big[\frac{G_L^2}{H_L + \lambda} + \frac{G_R^2}{H_R + \lambda} \\ &\qquad - \frac{(G_L + G_R)^2}{H_L + H_R + \lambda}\Big] - \gamma \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{blanks left:}\quad G_L &\leftarrow G_L + G_{\text{miss}} \\ H_L &\leftarrow H_L + H_{\text{miss}} \\ \text{blanks right:}\quad G_R &\leftarrow G_R + G_{\text{miss}} \\ H_R &\leftarrow H_R + H_{\text{miss}} \end{aligned} \]

Why it beats a fill value: the direction is chosen by the loss, so informative blanks go where they belong, and the choice can differ by node; a median fill would put blanks among typical values everywhere.

  • Others: CatBoost treats numeric blanks as smaller than every value by default, so a split can isolate them; sklearn's HistGradientBoosting learns a direction like XGBoost.
  • The trap: if a feature had no blanks in training, nothing was learned, and a blank at serving goes to an arbitrary or majority side. A pipeline bug that blanks a field can silently make risky rows look safe, so monitor missing rates.

Trade-off: a tree routes blanks but can't reconstruct the value. If blanks are MAR and the feature matters, a good model-based imputation can add a little; and if the same features feed a linear model, you need imputation for that model anyway. (04)

10When is it fine to drop rows or columns with missing values?

Drop rows only when the blanks are MCAR and few and you have data to spare. Drop a column only when it's mostly blank and neither its values nor its blankness predict the target.

  • Rows: under MAR or MNAR the complete rows aren't representative, so averages and rates computed from them are biased, and the model is tuned to the kind of row that's usually complete. (Exception, if pushed: a regression's coefficients stay unbiased when blanks depend only on the features, not on the target.) And the deployed model still meets blanks, so you need a strategy for them anyway. With many columns each a little blank, dropping incomplete rows can throw away a large share of the data.
  • Columns: a column that's 95% blank but whose presence predicts the target should become an indicator, not be deleted. Check the target rate for blank and non-blank rows first.
  • Rows with a missing target can't train a supervised model, but ask why they're missing. In credit, only approved applicants have outcomes, so the training data is a selected sample (reject inference).

Trade-off: dropping is simple and invents nothing, but it wastes data and can bias the sample. Imputation keeps the data but adds guesses. (04)

11When do you need multiple imputation?

When you need inference (standard errors, intervals, p-values) from data with blanks. Single imputation treats guesses as observed values, so those intervals come out too narrow.

The mechanism: create \(M\) completed datasets, each with random draws for the blanks (MICE with noise), fit the same model on each, and pool with Rubin's rules:

\[ \begin{aligned} \bar Q &= \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} \hat Q_m \\ B &= \frac{1}{M - 1} \sum_{m=1}^{M} \big(\hat Q_m - \bar Q\big)^2 \\ T &= \bar W + \Big(1 + \frac{1}{M}\Big) B \end{aligned} \]

In words: \(\hat Q_m\) is the estimate from dataset \(m\), \(\bar W\) the average of their squared standard errors, and \(B\) how much the estimates disagree across imputations. The pooled estimate is the average, and the total variance \(T\) adds the imputation uncertainty \(B\) that single imputation throws away.

Trade-offs: \(M\) times the cost, and it assumes MAR. Include the outcome in the imputation model here (unlike a prediction pipeline, where the target isn't available for new rows). For pure prediction it rarely pays off: averaging predictions over the imputations is at most a mild ensemble. (04)

12How do you detect and treat outliers? When do you keep them?

First decide whether each one is an error or a genuine extreme. Fix errors, often by turning them into missing values; limit the influence of genuine extremes; and keep them whenever they're what the model is meant to find.

Detection:

\[ \begin{aligned} z_i &= \frac{x_i - \bar x}{s} \\ z_i^{\text{robust}} &= \frac{0.6745\,(x_i - \tilde x)}{\text{MAD}} \\ \text{lower fence} &= Q_1 - 1.5\,\text{IQR} \\ \text{upper fence} &= Q_3 + 1.5\,\text{IQR} \end{aligned} \]

In words: the z-score counts standard deviations from the mean, but outliers inflate the standard deviation and hide themselves (masking). The robust version uses the median \(\tilde x\) and the median absolute deviation (MAD), which a few extremes can't move; flag above about 3.5. The fences use the quartiles \(Q_1, Q_3\) and \(\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1\); log skewed data first, or they flag the real long tail. For odd combinations of normal-looking values, an isolation forest; in regression, large residuals at high leverage (an unusual \(x\)). Plus domain limits.

  • Treat: fix errors from the source or set them to missing; cap genuine extremes at training percentiles (winsorize); transform with a log or Box-Cox; or use a robust loss (Huber, absolute error) or a tree.
  • Why it matters: one extreme value moves the mean and inflates the standard deviation, so scaling squeezes everything else; squared loss lets a far point pull harder the further it is, without limit (Huber caps the pull). Trees are robust to extreme inputs, but not to extreme targets with squared loss.
  • Keep them in fraud, anomaly detection or large claims, where the extremes are the positives. Production contains extremes too, so the model must handle them.

Trap: removing outliers from the test set flatters the score. Decide the rule on training data and apply it identically everywhere, including at serving. (05)

13How do you encode a high-cardinality categorical?

Not with one-hot. Use out-of-fold, smoothed target encoding or count encoding (often both), native categorical support in a boosting library, or a learned embedding in a neural net, with rare levels grouped.

  • Why not one-hot: thousands of sparse columns; rare levels get noisy coefficients; a tree can peel off only one level per split, so the feature's signal is spread thin and barely used.
  • Target encoding: each level becomes its average target, shrunk towards the overall mean:
    \[ \text{enc}(c) = \frac{n_c\, \bar y_c + m\, \bar y}{n_c + m} \]
    Here \(n_c\) is the level's row count, \(\bar y_c\) its average target, \(\bar y\) the overall average, and \(m\) how many rows' worth of weight the overall average gets. One column, ordered by risk, so one tree split can separate risky levels from safe ones. Computed out-of-fold to avoid leakage.
  • Count encoding: each level becomes its frequency. No target, so no target leakage, and popularity is often predictive in itself.
  • Native support: CatBoost's ordered target statistics, LightGBM's categorical splits (levels sorted by their gradient statistics, then cut).
  • Embeddings in a neural net: a learned vector per level, so similar levels share strength. Hashing for huge or open-ended vocabularies in linear models.

Trade-offs: target encoding risks leakage and drifts as levels' rates change; counts collide (equal counts look identical); hashing collides; embeddings need data per level. In every case, group levels below a count threshold into “Other” and decide what an unseen level gets (the prior, 0, an “unknown” index). (06)

14Why does target encoding leak, and how do you prevent it?

Each training row's own label is inside its category's average, so the feature partly is the label. Prevent it with out-of-fold encoding plus smoothing, fitted inside each cross-validation training fold.

The mechanism: for a level with one row, the encoding equals that row's label. For a level with \(n_c\) rows, a positive raises its own encoding by about \(1/n_c\). The model learns “high encoding means positive”, which is true in training because of the rows' own labels and false for new rows. Even a category with no real relationship to the target looks predictive in training, most strongly for small levels.

  • Out-of-fold: split the training rows into \(K\) folds and encode each fold from the other folds' averages; train on those. Validation, test and live rows use averages from all training rows.
  • Smoothing: shrink each level towards the overall mean with a prior weight \(m\) (the overall mean counts as \(m\) extra rows), so small levels can't take extreme values.
  • Inside cross-validation: computing the encodings on all the data and then cross-validating lets validation labels shape the training features. The encoder goes in the pipeline. sklearn's TargetEncoder cross-fits in fit_transform, which is what a pipeline calls; fit then transform on the same rows would leak.
  • Time-ordered data: encode from earlier rows only. CatBoost's ordered target statistics do this along a random order.

Follow-up, the leave-one-out trap: excluding just the row itself looks safe but isn't.

\[ \text{enc}_{-i} = \frac{S_c - y_i}{n_c - 1} \]

Within one level the sum \(S_c\) and count \(n_c\) are fixed, so the encoding falls as \(y_i\) rises: every positive gets a slightly lower value than every negative in its level, and a deep enough tree can separate them, level by level. A quick check: compare the encoded feature's AUC on training rows with its AUC on validation rows; a big gap is leakage. (06)

15Frequency encoding: when?

When how common a category is predicts the target, or when you need a one-column, leakage-free encoding for a high-cardinality feature in a tree model.

\[ \text{freq}(c) = \frac{n_c}{n} \]

In words: a level seen in \(n_c\) of the \(n\) training rows is replaced by that share (or by the count itself).

  • Why it works: popularity carries meaning. A merchant seen millions of times is an established business; a device model seen twice is unusual, which matters in fraud; niche product categories behave differently from mass-market ones.
  • Why it's safe: it never touches the target, so there's no target leakage, and it works without labels (anomaly detection).
  • Where it fits: tree models, usually next to a target encoding, since the two carry different information (how common versus how risky).

Trade-offs: levels with equal counts become identical even if they behave differently; counts depend on data volume and window, so use shares or a fixed trailing window (“transactions at this merchant in the last 30 days”) computed the same way at serving; an unseen level gets 0, which is usually meaningful. In a linear model it's weak on its own, since it assumes the effect grows linearly with popularity. (06)

16A category appears at inference that wasn't in training. What happens?

Whatever the encoder does by default, which can be a crash or a silent wrong answer. Decide it on purpose for each encoder, and test it.

  • One-hot: sklearn raises an error by default. With handle_unknown="ignore" the row is all zeros, which the model reads as “none of the known levels”; with rare-level grouping it goes to the “infrequent” column.
  • Ordinal codes: an error, or an arbitrary code that lands wherever the thresholds put it. sklearn's OrdinalEncoder can map unseen levels to NaN, so a tree that handles blanks routes them like blanks.
  • Target encoding: the prior, the overall mean. A sensible “know nothing” value.
  • Count encoding: 0, which honestly says “never seen”.
  • Embeddings: a reserved “unknown” index. Train it by mapping rare levels to it, or its vector stays random.
  • Hashing: lands in some bucket and shares it with whatever else is there.

Why it matters: a mismatch between training and serving codes is a classic silent production bug. The safeguard: group rare levels into “Other” in training, so the model has learned how to treat a rare or unknown level, and monitor the share of unseen levels in production. A jump means a new product, a renamed category or a broken upstream mapping. (06)

17Do trees need scaling or one-hot encoding?

Scaling, never. One-hot, sometimes: a tree needs numbers (unless its library handles categories natively), but for many levels other encodings work better.

  • Scaling: a split is a threshold on one feature, “income below t”. Any monotone rescaling or transform (standardizing, a log) moves the threshold with the data and gives exactly the same partition of the rows.
  • One-hot: fine for a few levels. For many, each split peels off one level and rare levels' columns offer tiny gains, so the signal is spread thin.
  • Ordinal codes work for trees, because several cuts can carve out any levels, but an arbitrary order wastes splits on separating levels that sit far apart.
  • Better for many levels: target or count encoding, or native categorical support (LightGBM, CatBoost, XGBoost, sklearn's HistGradientBoosting). Ordering levels by their target statistic lets one split group them, which for a binary target finds the best two-way grouping.

Follow-up, what trees do care about: extreme targets under squared loss (leaf means and boosting gradients get pulled; use Huber), leakage, and correlated features splitting importance. (06, 07)

18Standardization vs normalization.

Standardization rescales a column to mean 0 and standard deviation 1. Normalization usually means min-max scaling to \([0, 1]\), and in sklearn's Normalizer it means rescaling each row to unit length.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{standardize:}\quad \tilde x &= \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \\ \text{min-max:}\quad \tilde x &= \frac{x - x_{\min}}{x_{\max} - x_{\min}} \\ \text{robust:}\quad \tilde x &= \frac{x - \operatorname{median}}{\text{IQR}} \end{aligned} \]
  • Standardization is the default for penalized linear models, SVMs, kNN, PCA and neural nets: everything built on distances, penalties or gradient descent.
  • Min-max suits naturally bounded features (pixels, percentages) or models that need bounded inputs.
  • Robust scaling (subtract the median, divide by the IQR, \(Q_3 - Q_1\), the spread of the middle half) when outliers would otherwise set the scale.
  • Row normalization for vectors compared by direction, such as TF-IDF text vectors with cosine similarity.

Why scale at all: mixed units make gradient descent zig-zag through a stretched loss surface, let one feature dominate distances, and make a penalty punish features with small numeric ranges more. Trade-offs: min-max is fragile, since one outlier sets the range and new values fall outside \([0, 1]\); standardization isn't robust either; and neither changes the shape of a skewed column, which is a transform's job. Fit on training rows only. (07)

19A feature is heavily right-skewed. What do you do, and does it matter for XGBoost?

For linear models, distance-based methods and neural nets, transform it: a log, Box-Cox or Yeo-Johnson, or a quantile transform. For XGBoost it doesn't matter, because trees only use the order of values.

\[ x^{(\lambda)} = \begin{cases} \dfrac{x^{\lambda} - 1}{\lambda} & \lambda \ne 0 \\ \log x & \lambda = 0 \end{cases} \]

In words: Box-Cox is a family of power transforms with the log at \(\lambda = 0\); \(\lambda\) is chosen by maximum likelihood on training data to make the column as close to normal as possible. It needs positive values; use \(\log(1 + x)\) for counts with zeros and Yeo-Johnson for negative values.

  • Why it helps: a few huge values dominate squared loss and act as high-leverage points; multiplicative effects become additive after a log; relationships often become more linear; scaling becomes meaningful.
  • Quantile transform: the most robust option, but it bends relationships, distorts distances, and clips values beyond the training range.
  • XGBoost: a threshold on \(\log x\) is a threshold on \(x\), so the splits are identical.

Follow-up, a skewed target is different, even for XGBoost: with squared loss, the largest targets dominate the gradients. Options are a log target (which then predicts something close to the median, so totals come out low unless corrected) or a loss suited to the target, such as Huber, Poisson or Tweedie. (07)

20Collinearity: why it's a problem, how to detect it, what to do.

Highly correlated features make a linear model's individual coefficients unstable and hard to interpret, though predictions are usually fine. Detect it with VIF and the condition number; if you need the coefficients, drop, combine, use PCA, or use ridge.

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_j) &= \frac{\sigma^2}{\sum_i (x_{ij} - \bar x_j)^2} \cdot \text{VIF}_j \\ \text{VIF}_j &= \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2} \end{aligned} \]

In words: \(\sigma^2\) is the noise variance and \(R_j^2\) is how well the other features predict feature \(j\). Only the part of \(x_j\) they can't predict helps estimate \(\beta_j\), so as \(R_j^2\) nears 1 the coefficient's variance blows up.

  • Why it hurts: the data can't say which twin deserves the credit, so the coefficients slide along a flat valley: large standard errors, signs that flip, each feature insignificant alone while the pair is clearly significant together. Predictions depend on the combined effect, which is well pinned down, so they're fine while the correlation holds, and swing for rows that break it.
  • Detect: correlation matrix (pairwise only, misses one column being a combination of several); VIF per feature (above 5 worth a look, above 10 serious); condition number of the standardized feature matrix (above about 30).
  • Fix: nothing, if only predictions matter and the pattern is stable; drop one twin; combine them (a sum, a ratio, an index); PCA; ridge, which shares the weight between twins (lasso keeps one arbitrarily, elastic net keeps the group).

Follow-up: trees predict fine, but correlated features split their importance (next question). (08)

21Two correlated features both show low importance in a random forest. Why?

They share the credit. The forest uses one twin or the other at different splits, so impurity importance divides their joint importance between them, and permutation importance barely drops when one is shuffled, because the other covers for it.

  • Impurity importance adds up the gain from every split on a feature. Whichever twin wins a given split takes that split's credit. A random forest samples features at each split, so both twins win some splits, and each ends up with roughly half of what one would get alone.
  • Permutation importance shuffles one feature and measures the drop in score. The model still has the twin, so the score barely moves. Shuffling one twin also creates rows that never occur, like a large area in one unit and a small area in the other.
  • Boosting tends to lean on one twin and ignore the other, so the split is less even but equally arbitrary.

Why it matters: you might drop both and lose the signal, or conclude the underlying concept doesn't matter. Fix: group correlated features (cluster them by correlation), then permute each group together, or drop the group and refit; for SHAP, sum the group's values. (08)

22You add “the merchant's fraud rate” as a feature. What can go wrong?

It's target encoding in disguise, so it can leak in three ways: through the current row's own label, through future rows, and through test rows. It's also noisy for small merchants.

  • The row's own label: if the rate includes the transaction being scored, a fraud raises its own feature. Compute it out-of-fold, or from earlier transactions only.
  • Future rows: a rate over the whole dataset includes frauds that happened after the transaction. Compute it as of the transaction's time.
  • Label delay: fraud labels arrive weeks later (chargebacks). At scoring time, recent transactions have no labels yet, so the feature must use only labels that would have been known then; a rate that counts them anyway looks better offline than it can be live.
  • Pooling: computed over training and test rows together, it lets test labels shape training features.
  • Small merchants: one fraud in three transactions gives a wild rate. Smooth towards the overall rate, and add the transaction count as its own feature.

Why it's worth the care: done right, entity-level risk rates are among the strongest features. Trade-off: they drift (fraudsters move to new merchants), and new merchants have no history, so the prior and the count carry the load at first. (09)

23How do you do feature selection without leaking?

Treat selection as part of training: run it inside each cross-validation training fold, as a pipeline step, never on the full dataset before cross-validation.

The mechanism: take thousands of pure-noise features and random labels, keep the few most correlated with the labels on all rows, then cross-validate: accuracy comes out well above chance. The selection saw the validation labels and kept features that happen to fit them. Inside the pipeline, each fold selects using its own training rows, so the validation score measures the whole procedure honestly.

  • Filters (correlation, mutual information): fast, but one feature at a time, so they miss features that only work together and keep redundant copies.
  • Wrappers (recursive feature elimination, forward selection): use the model, but expensive and easy to overfit without nesting.
  • Embedded: L1 zeros out weak coefficients but picks among correlated features arbitrarily; tree impurity importance is biased towards high-cardinality features; permutation importance on validation data is fairer but hides correlated features.

Trade-off: different folds may select different features; that's expected, since you're evaluating the procedure. The final model reruns selection on all training data, and the final number comes from an untouched test set, or from nested cross-validation (an inner loop selects, an outer loop scores). (10)

24SMOTE: before or after the split, and does it help?

After the split, on training folds only, inside cross-validation. And on tabular data with gradient boosting it rarely beats class weights or a well-chosen threshold.

\[ \begin{aligned} x_{\text{new}} &= x_i + u\,(x_{\text{nn}} - x_i) \\ u &\sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1) \end{aligned} \]

In words: a synthetic positive is placed at a random point on the line between a real positive \(x_i\) and one of its nearest positive neighbours.

  • Before the split: synthetic points built from validation rows end up in training, near-copies of what you validate on, so the score is inflated.
  • Pitfalls: interpolated points can be unrealistic (half-way values of integer or categorical columns, points between two separate fraud patterns that land among genuine rows); it amplifies label noise; it depends on scaling, since it uses distances; and it distorts probabilities with no simple correction.
  • The better order: keep the loss proper and move the threshold to \(t^* = C_{FP} / (C_{FP} + C_{FN})\), where \(C_{FP}\) and \(C_{FN}\) are the costs of a false alarm and a miss; then class weights; then undersampling negatives, undoing the probability inflation with \(\operatorname{logit} p = \operatorname{logit} p_s + \log q\), where \(p_s\) is the model's probability on the resampled data, \(q\) the share of negatives kept, and \(\operatorname{logit} p = \log\big(p/(1 - p)\big)\).

Follow-up: judge any of these with PR-AUC and precision and recall at the operating threshold, not accuracy, and never resample the validation or test sets: they must keep the real base rate. (11)

25Overfitting vs underfitting: how do you tell, and how do you fix each?

Compare training and validation performance. Both poor means underfitting (high bias); training good and validation poor means overfitting (high variance). Learning and validation curves confirm which.

\[ \mathbb E\big[(y - \hat f)^2\big] = \text{bias}^2 + \text{variance} + \sigma^2 \]

In words: \(\hat f\) is the fitted model and \(f\) the true function. Bias is how far the average fitted model, over many training sets, is from \(f\) (too simple); variance is how much \(\hat f\) changes between training sets (too flexible); \(\sigma^2\) is noise no model can remove.

  • Learning curve (scores against training-set size): curves that meet low means underfitting, and more data won't help; a gap that narrows as data grows means overfitting, and more data will.
  • Validation curve (scores against one complexity setting): pick the validation peak, or the simplest setting near it.
  • Fix underfitting: better features (ratios, aggregates, interactions), more capacity, less regularization, longer training; check label noise.
  • Fix overfitting: more data, fewer or cleaner features, grouped rare levels and smoothed encodings, stronger regularization, shallower trees, early stopping, bagging.

Follow-up: a big gap isn't always overfitting. If a feature leaks only in training rows, or the validation period differs from the training period, the gap comes from the data; check with a time-based split and adversarial validation before adding regularization. (12)

15Cheat sheet

The rule and the split

Anything that learns a number is fitted on training folds, inside the pipeline. Split as deployment works: by time (with a gap) for forecasting and credit, by group for repeated entities, stratified for rare classes. Look at the test set once.

Missing values

MechanismBlank depends onHandling
MCARNothingDrop: no bias, less data. Mean fill: shrinks spread
MARObserved columnsImpute from them (model-based, MICE)
MNARThe value itselfKeep the blank as a feature; sensitivity analysis for inference

Categorical: a “Missing” level by default; the mode only if MCAR, few, and not predictive. Numeric: median plus indicator; model-based under MAR; native handling in boosting. Indicator \(I\) with its own intercept \(\theta\), mean-fill damage (share \(r\) missing), and Rubin's total variance over \(M\) imputations:

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat y &= \beta_0 + \beta \tilde x + \theta I \\ \operatorname{Var}(\tilde x) &\approx (1 - r)\operatorname{Var}(x) \\ \operatorname{Corr}(\tilde x, z) &\approx \sqrt{1 - r}\, \operatorname{Corr}(x, z) \\ T &= \bar W + (1 + 1/M)\, B \end{aligned} \]

Outliers

z-score (flag above 3), robust z (flag above 3.5), IQR fences:

\[ \begin{aligned} z &= (x - \bar x)/s \\ z^{\text{robust}} &= 0.6745\,(x - \tilde x)/\text{MAD} \\ \text{low fence} &= Q_1 - 1.5\,\text{IQR} \\ \text{high fence} &= Q_3 + 1.5\,\text{IQR} \end{aligned} \]

Error: fix it or set it missing. Genuine: cap, log, robust loss, or keep it (fraud).

Encoding

Smoothed target encoding (out-of-fold), the leave-one-out version that leaks, frequency, and the hashing collision chance:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{enc}(c) &= \frac{n_c \bar y_c + m \bar y}{n_c + m} \\ \text{enc}_{-i} &= \frac{S_c - y_i}{n_c - 1} \\ \text{freq}(c) &= n_c / n \\ P_{\text{collide}} &\approx 1 - e^{-K/B} \end{aligned} \]

Few levels: one-hot (drop one if unregularized with an intercept). Many: target plus count encoding, native categorical, or embeddings. Unseen: the prior, 0, or an “unknown” index.

Scaling and transforms

Standardize, min-max, robust, Box-Cox (log at \(\lambda = 0\)), cyclical hour:

\[ \begin{aligned} \tilde x &= (x - \mu)/\sigma \\ \tilde x &= (x - x_{\min})/(x_{\max} - x_{\min}) \\ \tilde x &= (x - \operatorname{median})/\text{IQR} \\ x^{(\lambda)} &= (x^{\lambda} - 1)/\lambda \\ h_{\sin} &= \sin(2\pi h/24) \\ h_{\cos} &= \cos(2\pi h/24) \end{aligned} \]

Scale for distances, penalties, gradient descent and PCA. Trees: never.

Collinearity

VIF above 5 is worth a look, above 10 serious; condition number above about 30.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{VIF}_j &= \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2} \\ \operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_j) &= \frac{\sigma^2 \cdot \text{VIF}_j}{\sum_i (x_{ij} - \bar x_j)^2} \end{aligned} \]

It hurts coefficients, not predictions. In trees, twins split the importance.

Imbalance and fit

Cost threshold, prior correction after resampling, SMOTE, bias–variance:

\[ \begin{aligned} t^* &= \frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP} + C_{FN}} \\ \operatorname{logit} p &= \operatorname{logit} p_s + \log q \\ x_{\text{new}} &= x_i + u\,(x_{\text{nn}} - x_i) \\ \mathbb E\big[(y - \hat f)^2\big] &= \text{bias}^2 + \text{var} + \sigma^2 \end{aligned} \]

Threshold first, weights second, resampling last, and only on training folds. Metrics: PR-AUC, precision and recall at the threshold, cost.
