Unsupervised learning: clustering, PCA and SVD

What to do with data that has no labels: k-means worked by hand, Gaussian mixtures and EM, hierarchical clustering and DBSCAN, how to judge a clustering, PCA derived two ways, the SVD and low-rank approximation, t-SNE and UMAP for pictures, and anomaly detection.

About 60 minutes, plus 28 questions · six points for clustering, four users for PCA · every number checked by code

In short

  1. Unsupervised learning finds structure in features with no target: segments, compressed features, pictures and anomalies. There's no ground truth to score against, so decide what the result is for, then judge it by stability and by whether it's useful downstream, not by one internal metric.
  2. k-means minimizes the within-cluster sum of squares by alternating two steps: assign each point to its nearest centre, then move each centre to the mean of its points. Neither step can raise the objective, so it converges, but only to a local minimum. Seed with k-means++ and keep the best of several runs. It assumes round clusters of similar size in a space where straight-line distance means something, so scale first.
  3. A Gaussian mixture is soft k-means fitted by EM. The E-step gives each point a probability for each component (Bayes' rule), the M-step refits each component by weighted maximum likelihood, and the likelihood never goes down. Hierarchical clustering builds a tree of merges whose shape is set by the linkage. DBSCAN grows clusters from dense core points, finds any shape and labels noise, but one radius can't suit clusters of different density.
  4. Internal metrics reward compact, round clusters, so they can prefer a wrong answer. On two interleaved moons, silhouette, Davies–Bouldin and Calinski–Harabasz all prefer k-means's split (ARI 0.27) to the true moons. ARI and NMI need labels; ARI is corrected for chance and NMI isn't.
  5. PCA finds the directions of most variance, which are also the directions of least reconstruction error: the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, with the eigenvalues as the variance along each. It's computed as the SVD of the centred data, \(X_c = U\Sigma V^\top\), with eigenvalues \(\sigma^2/(n-1)\). Standardize first, and remember that the label may live in a low-variance direction.
  6. Keeping the top \(k\) singular values gives the best rank-\(k\) approximation (Eckart–Young), with squared error equal to the sum of the dropped \(\sigma^2\). That one fact is behind compression, denoising, LSA, matrix-factorization recommenders and low-rank adapters. t-SNE and UMAP are for pictures only: cluster sizes and gaps in their plots mean little.

01What unsupervised learning is for

In supervised learning every row has a target \(y\), and the model learns to predict it. In unsupervised learning there are only the features \(X\). The job is to find structure in them: groups of similar rows, a few directions that capture most of the variation, or regions where the data is dense or thin.

Five jobs it does in practice

  • Segmentation. Group customers by behaviour (recency, frequency and spend; the mix of products) so each group gets its own offer, message or product. The groups describe users; whether an offer changes their behaviour is a separate, causal question for an experiment.
  • Compression and dimensionality reduction. Replace many correlated features by a few. On sklearn's 8×8 digit images, 21 of the 64 principal components keep 90% of the variance, because neighbouring pixels move together. Fewer features means faster models, less noise and less trouble with distances in high dimensions.
  • Visualization. A 2-D map of embeddings or customers (PCA, t-SNE, UMAP) to spot groups, mislabelled points, duplicates and batch effects.
  • Anomaly detection. Fraud, faulty sensors, broken pipelines. Labels for these arrive late, are few, and only cover what someone already caught, so an unsupervised score is often the first line.
  • Features for supervised models. A cluster ID or the distance to each centre, PCA scores, document vectors from a truncated SVD, an anomaly score. Also labelling help: cluster first, then label a few rows from each cluster.

Why evaluation is hard

  • No ground truth. There's no held-out \(y\) to compute an error on. A clustering isn't right or wrong in itself; it is useful or not for a purpose.
  • Several answers can all be valid. The same customers can be grouped by spend level or by product mix. Which one is “correct” depends on the decision it will feed.
  • Every algorithm returns an answer. k-means returns \(k\) clusters even on uniform random noise. A finished run is not evidence of structure.
  • Internal metrics carry the algorithm's own assumptions. The silhouette rewards compact round blobs, which is what k-means makes, so scoring k-means by silhouette is partly circular (section 06).

What to do instead: fix the use before the method; check stability (refit on bootstrap samples or different seeds and compare the labelings with ARI, the adjusted Rand index: 1 means the same grouping, about 0 means no better than chance; section 06); check usefulness (does the segment feature improve a model; can the business name and act on each segment); sanity-check against domain knowledge; and use whatever few labels exist.

Trap

“The clustering found five segments.” It found five because k was set to five. Before presenting segments, show that the structure is there: the clusters survive refits, they differ on features you didn't cluster on, and a reference with no structure (the gap statistic, section 02) scores clearly worse.

02k-means

The objective

Choose \(k\) centres, and give each point to one of them, so that the total squared distance from each point to its own centre is as small as possible:

\[ \begin{aligned} J &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big\lVert x_i - \mu_{c(i)} \big\rVert^2 \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^{k} \sum_{i \in S_j} \lVert x_i - \mu_j \rVert^2 \end{aligned} \]

In words: \(c(i)\) is the cluster point \(i\) belongs to, \(\mu_j\) is the centre of cluster \(j\) and \(S_j\) its set of points. \(J\) adds up every point's squared distance to its centre. Its names: the within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS), or the inertia (sklearn's name).

The same thing from the other side. The total sum of squares around the overall mean is fixed by the data, and it splits exactly into a within-cluster part and a between-cluster part, \(T = W + B\). So making clusters tight (small \(W\)) is the same as pushing their centres apart (large \(B\)). Here \(B = \sum_j n_j \lVert \mu_j - \bar x \rVert^2\): each centre's squared distance from the overall mean, weighted by its cluster's size. For the six points split into {A, B, C} and {D, E, F}, the overall mean is (3.33, 3), both centres are 6.78 from it (squared), so \(B = 3 \times 6.78 + 3 \times 6.78 = 40.67\), and \(43.33 = 2.67 + 40.67\). Calinski–Harabasz (section 06) is built from exactly these two numbers.

Lloyd's algorithm, by hand

Minimizing \(J\) over every possible split is hard, so k-means alternates two easy steps until nothing changes. This is Lloyd's algorithm, which is what people mean by “k-means”:

  1. Assign: each point joins its nearest centre.
  2. Update: each centre moves to the mean of its points.

Take the six points with \(k = 2\), and start badly, with the centres on A and B, which belong together.

Why it converges, and why only to a local minimum

  • The assign step can't raise \(J\). A point only switches to a centre that is closer, so its own term shrinks or stays.
  • The update step can't raise \(J\). For a fixed group, the single point that minimizes the total squared distance to the group is its mean. The reason is an exact split: for any candidate centre \(z\),
\[ \sum_{i \in S} \lVert x_i - z \rVert^2 = \sum_{i \in S} \lVert x_i - \bar x_S \rVert^2 + \lvert S \rvert \, \lVert \bar x_S - z \rVert^2 \]

In words: the cost of any centre is the cost of the mean plus a penalty that grows with how far \(z\) is from the mean. For {A, B, C}, the mean (1.33, 1.33) costs 1.33; the centre (1, 1) costs \(1.33 + 3 \times 0.22 = 2.00\). It's the same fact that makes squared error predict the average.

  • So it must stop. \(J\) is never negative and never goes up, and there are only finitely many ways to split \(n\) points into \(k\) groups. Each change strictly lowers \(J\), so no split repeats, and the loop ends. In the language of optimization this is coordinate descent: minimize over the assignments with the centres fixed, then over the centres with the assignments fixed.
  • But only at a local minimum: a split where neither step helps, which need not be the best one. Finding the best split is NP-hard in general; Lloyd's algorithm is a fast heuristic.

A local minimum, by hand. Four points at the corners of a wide rectangle: (0, 0), (0, 1), (4, 0), (4, 1). The best split is left pair against right pair, \(J = 4 \times 0.25 = 1\). Start the centres at (0, 0) and (0, 1):

  1. (4, 0) is nearer (0, 0) (squared distance 16 against 17), and (4, 1) is nearer (0, 1). The groups are bottom pair against top pair.
  2. The centres move to (2, 0) and (2, 1), and \(J = 4 \times 4 = 16\).
  3. Every point is already nearest its own centre ((0, 0) is 4 from (2, 0) and 5 from (2, 1)), so nothing moves. Stuck at 16, sixteen times the best.

k-means++ seeding

The fix is a better start, spread across the data:

  1. Pick the first centre uniformly at random from the data.
  2. Pick each next centre at random, with probability proportional to \(D(x)^2\), the squared distance from \(x\) to the nearest centre chosen so far.
  3. Then run Lloyd's algorithm as usual.

On the rectangle. Say the first pick is (0, 0). The squared distances to the four points are 0, 1, 16 and 17, so the second centre is (0, 1) with probability \(1/34\), (4, 0) with \(16/34\) and (4, 1) with \(17/34\). That's a 97% chance of starting on the right-hand side, which leads straight to the best split. By symmetry the same holds whatever the first pick, so k-means++ finds the optimum 97% of the time here; two distinct points picked uniformly do it only \(2/3\) of the time.

  • The guarantee: Arthur and Vassilvitskii (2007) proved that the k-means++ seeding alone has expected \(J\) at most \(8(\ln k + 2)\) times the optimum: about 34 times for \(k = 10\). That's a worst-case bound; in practice the result after Lloyd's steps is usually close to the best of many random starts.
  • Why squared distance and not “the farthest point”: farthest-first picks outliers every time. Sampling by \(D^2\) still favours far regions, but a lone outlier is only one point, while a far cluster offers many points to be picked.
  • Cost: \(k\) passes over the data, \(O(nkd)\), the same as one Lloyd iteration. It's sklearn's default; run several seeds and keep the lowest \(J\) (set n_init explicitly: with k-means++ seeding, recent sklearn runs only one by default).

  • Elbow: plot \(J\) against \(k\) and look for where an extra cluster stops paying. Here \(1186 \to 501\), then only \(501 \to 439\). On real data the elbow is often a gentle curve with no clear bend.
  • Silhouette (computed by hand in section 06): how much closer each point is to its own cluster than to the next one, averaged. It peaks at 4.
  • Gap statistic (Tibshirani, Walther and Hastie, 2001): even data with no clusters at all has \(J\) falling with \(k\), so compare against that. Draw \(B\) reference datasets uniformly over the data's bounding box, cluster each, and measure how much lower the real data's \(\log J\) is:
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Gap}(k) &= \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} \log J_k^{(b)} - \log J_k \\ \hat k &= \text{smallest } k \text{ with } \text{Gap}(k) \ge \text{Gap}(k+1) - s_{k+1} \end{aligned} \]

In words: the gap is how far below “no structure” the real clustering's log-inertia sits, and \(s_{k+1}\) is the reference runs' standard deviation times \(\sqrt{1 + 1/B}\). Pick the first \(k\) whose gap is within noise of the next one's. Here, with \(B = 20\): \(0.862 \ge 0.789 - 0.024\), so \(\hat k = 4\). The log makes the comparison about ratios, since \(J\) shrinks multiplicatively as clusters split.

  • In practice: shortlist \(k\) with these, then decide on stability (do the clusters survive refits on resampled data, measured by ARI between the labelings?), actionability (can the team name and use each segment?), and downstream value. With a mixture model, BIC is another option (section 03).

Assumptions, and how k-means fails

The border between two centres is the straight line halfway between them (every point goes to the nearer centre), so every cluster is a convex cell, and a cluster's reach depends only on where its centre is, not on how spread out its points are. That makes k-means quietly assume clusters are round, of similar size and spread, in a space where straight-line distance is meaningful. Each failure below was run on synthetic data:

Trap

Reading “k-means found the segments” off a run on raw features. With income in rupees next to age in years, k-means clusters on income alone, whatever the business meaning. Every distance-based method (k-means, hierarchical, DBSCAN, kNN), and PCA, needs features on comparable scales.

Trap

k-means is not kNN. k-means is unsupervised clustering: \(k\) is the number of clusters, and it's fitted by Lloyd's iterations. kNN is a supervised predictor: \(k\) is the number of neighbours whose labels vote, and there's no training step beyond storing the data.

Variants: mini-batch, k-medoids, k-prototypes

  • Mini-batch k-means. Each step draws a random batch (say 1,024 points), assigns them, and nudges each centre towards its batch points with a step size of 1 / (points that centre has seen so far), so the centre is a running average. A step costs \(O(bkd)\) for batch size \(b\), whatever \(n\) is, and the data can stream from disk. The price is a slightly worse \(J\): on 200,000 points in 10 clusters, mini-batch's inertia came within 0.03% of full k-means's.
  • k-medoids (PAM, “partitioning around medoids”). Each centre must be an actual data point (a medoid), and the cost is the sum of plain distances under any distance: Manhattan, edit distance for strings, or Gower distance for mixed types (the average of a per-feature distance in [0, 1]: the absolute difference divided by the column's range for numeric features, 0 or 1 for a category match or mismatch). It's robust: for the values 1, 2, 3, 4 and 100 the mean is 22, but the medoid is 3 (total distance 101, against 102 for 2 or 4). The classic swap search costs \(O(k(n-k)^2)\) per iteration, fine for thousands of rows; sampling versions (CLARA) go further.
  • k-modes and k-prototypes for categorical and mixed data. k-modes uses the number of mismatching categories as the distance and the most common value per feature as the centre. k-prototypes adds the two: squared Euclidean distance on the numeric columns plus \(\gamma\) times the categorical mismatches, where \(\gamma\) sets how much a mismatch weighs against a unit of numeric distance.
  • For curved clusters: kernel k-means and spectral clustering cluster in a transformed space where the shapes become round; worth knowing by name.
  • Text and utterance embeddings: L2-normalize first. For unit vectors \(\lVert a - b \rVert^2 = 2 - 2\cos(a, b)\), so Euclidean k-means then groups by cosine similarity (spherical k-means also re-normalizes the centres; see similarity). Reduce the dimension before density methods: PCA to tens of dimensions, or UMAP to roughly 5–10 before HDBSCAN (a common rule of thumb, not a law).

Cost. Each Lloyd iteration computes \(n \times k\) distances in \(d\) dimensions, \(O(nkd)\), so \(i\) iterations cost \(O(nkdi)\), with memory \(O((n + k)d)\). It usually converges in tens of iterations. Elkan's variant skips distance computations using the triangle inequality.

Say it in an interview: “k-means minimizes the within-cluster sum of squares. Lloyd's algorithm alternates assigning each point to its nearest centre and moving each centre to its points' mean; both steps can only lower the objective, so it converges, but to a local minimum. I seed with k-means++, which samples new centres with probability proportional to squared distance, run several seeds and keep the best. I pick k with the elbow, silhouette or gap statistic plus stability and business sense, and I scale features first, since it assumes round, similar-sized clusters under Euclidean distance.”

03Gaussian mixtures and EM

Soft assignments

k-means gives every point one cluster. A Gaussian mixture model (GMM) gives every point a probability for each cluster: “this user is 82% cluster 1 and 18% cluster 2” (the user at 2 in the example below, after the first E-step). It treats the data as generated in two steps: pick component \(j\) with probability \(\pi_j\) (its share of the data), then draw the point from that component's bell curve, a normal distribution with its own centre \(\mu_j\) and its own spread and tilt \(\Sigma_j\) (a covariance matrix, built by hand in section 07). The density of a point is then

\[ p(x) = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \pi_j \, \mathcal N(x \mid \mu_j, \Sigma_j) \]

In words: a weighted sum of \(k\) bell curves, with weights \(\pi_j\) that add up to 1.

Given the parameters, the probability that point \(i\) came from component \(j\) is Bayes' rule (the component's share is the prior, its bell curve is the likelihood of the point):

\[ r_{ij} = \frac{\pi_j \, \mathcal N(x_i \mid \mu_j, \Sigma_j)}{\sum_{l=1}^{k} \pi_l \, \mathcal N(x_i \mid \mu_l, \Sigma_l)} \]

In words: how much component \(j\) explains point \(i\), as a share of how much all the components together explain it. These are the responsibilities; each point's add up to 1.

EM as maximum likelihood with hidden labels

The goal is ordinary maximum likelihood: choose \(\theta = (\pi, \mu, \Sigma)\) to maximize

\[ \ell(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log \sum_{j=1}^{k} \pi_j \, \mathcal N(x_i \mid \mu_j, \Sigma_j) \]

In words: the log of the probability the mixture gives to the data. The sum sits inside the log, so the log can't split it into one simple term per component, and setting the derivatives to zero gives equations where each parameter depends on the responsibilities, which depend on the parameters. There's no closed form.

The trick. If we knew which component produced each point (a hidden, or latent, label \(z_i\)), the problem would be easy: fit each Gaussian to its own points. EM alternates between guessing the hidden labels softly and solving that easy problem:

  • E-step (expectation): with the current parameters, compute each point's posterior over its hidden label: the responsibilities.
  • M-step (maximization): maximize the expected complete-data log-likelihood, which is the easy problem with each point split across components by its responsibilities. Its solution is the weighted share, mean and covariance above.

Why the likelihood never goes down. For any weights \(q_{ij} \ge 0\) that sum to 1 over \(j\), the log of an average is at least the average of the logs (Jensen's inequality, because log is concave):

\[ \begin{aligned} \log p(x_i) &= \log \sum_j q_{ij}\, \frac{\pi_j \mathcal N(x_i \mid \mu_j, \Sigma_j)}{q_{ij}} \\ &\ge \sum_j q_{ij} \log \frac{\pi_j \mathcal N(x_i \mid \mu_j, \Sigma_j)}{q_{ij}} \end{aligned} \]

In words: the right-hand side, summed over points, is a lower bound on \(\ell\) for any choice of \(q\), called the evidence lower bound (ELBO). It touches \(\ell\) exactly when \(q_{ij} = r_{ij}\), because then every ratio inside the log equals the same number, \(p(x_i)\).

  1. The E-step sets \(q = r\), so the bound equals \(\ell(\theta_{\text{old}})\).
  2. The M-step picks \(\theta_{\text{new}}\) to maximize the bound with \(q\) held fixed, so the bound at \(\theta_{\text{new}}\) is at least its value at \(\theta_{\text{old}}\). This is the same M-step as above: the bound splits into \(\sum q \log(\pi_j \mathcal N_j)\), the expected complete-data log-likelihood, minus \(\sum q \log q\), which doesn't involve \(\theta\). So maximizing the bound over \(\theta\) is exactly the weighted MLE.
  3. The bound is never above \(\ell\), so \(\ell(\theta_{\text{new}}) \ge \text{bound}(\theta_{\text{new}}) \ge \text{bound}(\theta_{\text{old}}) = \ell(\theta_{\text{old}})\).

That's the same climb you watched in the table: \(-26.49, -15.29, -15.26, \ldots\). Like k-means, EM reaches a local maximum (strictly, a stationary point of the likelihood) that depends on the start, so run several starts (sklearn starts from a k-means solution by default). The same algorithm fits hidden Markov models (Baum–Welch), handles missing data, and fits topic models; only the hidden variable changes.

k-means is the hard-assignment limit

Give every component the same share and the same round covariance \(\sigma^2 I\). Then \(r_{ij} \propto e^{-\lVert x_i - \mu_j \rVert^2 / 2\sigma^2}\), and as \(\sigma\) shrinks the nearest centre's term swamps the rest. For \(x = 3\) between centres 2 and 7:

Choosing the number of components with BIC

The likelihood always rises with more components, so it needs a charge for complexity. The Bayesian information criterion:

\[ \text{BIC} = p \ln n - 2 \ln \hat L \]

In words: misfit (\(-2\) times the maximized log-likelihood) plus a charge of \(\ln n\) per parameter, with \(p\) parameters and \(n\) points; lower is better. AIC charges a flat 2 per parameter, so it tends to pick more components.

Trap

A component can collapse onto one point. As its \(\sigma \to 0\), the density at that point goes to infinity, and so does the likelihood. On the seven users, a 3-component fit puts one component on the lone point 4.5 with \(\sigma\) at the numerical floor (0.001): \(\ln \hat L\) jumps from −15.0 to −8.3, and BIC prefers it (32.2 against 39.8 for two components; one component scores 36.2, so with seven points BIC would honestly prefer one component to two, and the degenerate three beats both). That's a degenerate fit, not a discovery. Guards: a variance floor (sklearn's reg_covar), a prior (a Bayesian GMM), enough points per component, and suspicion of any component holding one or two points.

What a GMM gives that k-means doesn't: soft memberships; ellipses of different sizes and tilts (the 500-versus-50 case in section 02, ARI 0.99 against k-means's 0.20); a likelihood for BIC; and a density, so a point where every component is thin can be flagged as an anomaly. The costs: more parameters, slower fits, collapse, and the same local optima.

Say it in an interview: “A GMM models the data as a weighted sum of Gaussians and is fitted by EM, maximum likelihood with each point's component as a hidden variable. The E-step computes responsibilities by Bayes' rule; the M-step refits each component's share, mean and covariance with those weights. By Jensen's inequality each round can't lower the likelihood, but it finds a local optimum. k-means is the limit with equal, spherical, vanishing covariances. I choose the covariance type by data size and pick the number of components with BIC, watching for components collapsing onto single points.”

04Hierarchical clustering

Agglomerative (bottom-up) clustering starts with every point as its own cluster and repeatedly merges the two closest clusters until one is left. It records the height of each merge, so the result is a whole tree of clusterings, the dendrogram, rather than one split. Cutting the tree at some height gives a flat clustering with as many clusters as branches the cut crosses. (Divisive clustering goes top-down, for example by repeatedly splitting with 2-means; it's less common.)

Linkage: what “closest” means for groups

Distance between two points is given. Distance between two groups is a choice, the linkage, and it decides the shapes you get:

LinkageDistance between groups \(a\) and \(b\)Favours
Singlethe nearest pair, one point from eachlong chains and any shape; one noise point can bridge two clusters (“chaining”)
Completethe farthest paircompact clusters of similar diameter; sensitive to outliers, which inflate the maximum
Averagethe mean over all pairsin between the two
Wardhow much the merge raises the within-cluster sum of squaresround clusters of similar size, like k-means; Euclidean distance only

Ward's merge cost has a closed form in terms of the two groups' sizes and centres:

\[ \Delta(a, b) = \frac{n_a n_b}{n_a + n_b} \, \lVert \mu_a - \mu_b \rVert^2 \]

In words: merging costs more when the centres are far apart and when both groups are big. It is the greedy, one-merge-at-a-time version of the k-means objective.

  • Single linkage cuts at the largest gaps. It's equivalent to building the minimum spanning tree of the points and deleting its longest edges. That's why it follows chains and finds the two moons (ARI 1.0), and why one stray point in a gap glues two clusters together.

  • On the two moons: single 1.0, average 0.33, complete 0.29, Ward 0.24. Only the chain-follower gets curved shapes.

Reading and cutting a dendrogram

  • Height is the linkage distance at the merge. Points that join low are similar; the order of leaves along the bottom carries little meaning (each merge can be flipped).
  • Cut at a height, or for a number of clusters. A long vertical stretch with no merges means clusters that stay apart over a wide range of thresholds, a natural place to cut.
  • Greedy and final: a merge is never undone, so an early mistake propagates up the tree.
  • Strengths: no \(k\) needed in advance; any distance works (Gower for mixed types, correlation distance for time series); one run gives every level of granularity; deterministic.

Cost

It needs every pairwise distance: \(O(n^2)\) memory (except single linkage, which SLINK or a minimum spanning tree does in \(O(n)\)). For 100,000 points that's about \(5 \times 10^9\) distances, 40 GB in 64-bit floats. A naive implementation is \(O(n^3)\) time; good ones are \(O(n^2)\) (SLINK for single linkage, the nearest-neighbour chain for complete, average and Ward). So it suits up to tens of thousands of points. For more, cluster a sample, or first compress the data into a few thousand k-means micro-clusters and build the tree on their centres.

Say it in an interview: “Agglomerative clustering starts from singletons and repeatedly merges the closest pair of clusters, giving a dendrogram I can cut at any level. The linkage decides the shapes: single follows chains but suffers from chaining, complete gives compact clusters but is outlier-sensitive, average is in between, and Ward minimizes the increase in within-cluster variance, like a greedy k-means. It works with any distance, but needs all pairwise distances, \(O(n^2)\) memory, so it's for small to medium data.”

05DBSCAN

DBSCAN (density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise; Ester et al., 1996) says a cluster is a dense region, separated from other clusters by sparse space. It has two settings: a radius \(\varepsilon\) (eps) and a count minPts (sklearn's min_samples, which counts the point itself).

  • A core point has at least minPts points within \(\varepsilon\).
  • A border point isn't core, but lies within \(\varepsilon\) of a core point.
  • Everything else is noise (label −1).
  • A cluster is a set of core points linked by chains of core points, each within \(\varepsilon\) of the next, plus the border points they reach.

By hand

The six points plus two more: H (3, 1), just right of C, and G (9, 1), far from everything. Take \(\varepsilon = 1.5\) and minPts = 3.

  • Border points can be ambiguous. A border point within \(\varepsilon\) of core points from two clusters goes to whichever cluster reaches it first, so it depends on the order of the data. Core points and noise don't.
  • New points: there's no built-in predict. Give a new point the cluster of a core point within \(\varepsilon\), or call it noise.

Choosing eps and minPts

  • The k-distance plot. For every point, find the distance to its (minPts − 1)-th nearest other point, sort these distances, and plot them. Points inside clusters have small values; noise has large ones. Set \(\varepsilon\) at the knee, just before the curve shoots up. Here, with minPts = 3 (the second-nearest other point), the sorted values are 1, 1, 1, 1.41, 1.41, 1.41, 2, 5: the jump from 2 to 5 is G, so the knee says \(\varepsilon \approx 1.5\) to 2.
  • What eps does here: 0.9 makes every point noise (no point has a neighbour that close); anything from 1 up to just under \(\sqrt{13} \approx 3.61\) gives the same two clusters with G as noise (though below 1.41, B, D and F drop to border points, and from 2, H becomes core); from 3.61, the distance from H to D, the two groups merge into one; at 5, G joins them too.
  • minPts: larger values smooth the result and call more points noise. Common rules of thumb: at least the number of features plus one, often twice the number of features, and more for noisy data. Scale the features first, since \(\varepsilon\) is one radius in every direction.

Strengths and failures

  • Strengths: no \(k\); any shape (two moons: ARI 1.0 against k-means's 0.27); outliers labelled as noise rather than forced into a cluster; with a spatial index, about \(O(n \log n)\) in low dimensions (\(O(n^2)\) in the worst case).
  • Failure: clusters of different density. One radius can't fit both. Two tight clusters 1.5 apart (standard deviation 0.2) and a loose one (standard deviation 1.2): at \(\varepsilon = 0.2\) the tight pair is separate, but 123 of the 150 loose points are called noise and the remaining 27 split into four small fragments; from \(\varepsilon = 0.4\) up, the tight pair merges into one cluster.
  • Failure: many dimensions. Distances concentrate, so a single radius stops separating dense from sparse. Reduce dimensions first.
  • HDBSCAN in a line: run DBSCAN at every \(\varepsilon\) at once, build the resulting hierarchy of clusters, and keep the clusters that persist over the widest range of densities. It needs only a minimum cluster size and handles varying density: on the example above it finds all three clusters (ARI 0.98) with 5 noise points. OPTICS is an older relative with the same goal.

Say it in an interview: “DBSCAN defines clusters as dense regions: core points have at least minPts neighbours within eps, clusters are core points linked through each other's neighbourhoods plus the border points they reach, and the rest is noise. It needs no k, finds arbitrary shapes and flags outliers. I pick eps from the knee of the k-distance plot after scaling. It struggles when clusters have different densities, where HDBSCAN is the usual fix, and in high dimensions.”

06Judging a clustering

Two kinds of metric. Internal metrics use only the data and the labels the algorithm produced: are clusters tight and far apart? External metrics compare the labels with known classes (or with another clustering): do they agree?

Silhouette

For each point, ask: how far is it from its own cluster, compared with the nearest other cluster?

\[ \begin{aligned} a(i) &= \text{mean distance from } i \text{ to the other points of its own cluster} \\ b(i) &= \text{smallest mean distance from } i \text{ to the points of another cluster} \\ s(i) &= \frac{b(i) - a(i)}{\max\big(a(i), b(i)\big)} \end{aligned} \]

In words: \(s\) runs from −1 to 1. Near 1, the point sits deep inside its cluster; near 0, it's on a border; below 0, it's closer to another cluster than to its own, probably misassigned. A point alone in its cluster gets 0 by convention. The silhouette score is the mean over all points.

By hand, for A in the final split {A, B, C}, {D, E, F} (plain distances, not squared):

  • \(a\): A to B is 1 and A to C is 1, so \(a = 1\).
  • \(b\): A to D is \(\sqrt{25} = 5\), to E \(\sqrt{32} = 5.66\), to F \(\sqrt{41} = 6.40\), so \(b = 5.69\).
  • \(s = (5.69 - 1)/5.69 = 0.82\).

All six: 0.82, 0.76, 0.76, 0.74, 0.81, 0.80, mean 0.78. It needs all pairwise distances, \(O(n^2)\), so on large data compute it on a random sample.

Davies–Bouldin and Calinski–Harabasz

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{DB} &= \frac{1}{k} \sum_{j=1}^{k} \max_{l \ne j} \frac{S_j + S_l}{M_{jl}} \\ \text{CH} &= \frac{B / (k - 1)}{W / (n - k)} \end{aligned} \]

In words: for Davies–Bouldin, \(S_j\) is the average distance from cluster \(j\)'s points to its centre and \(M_{jl}\) the distance between two centres; each cluster is scored against its most confusable neighbour, spread over separation, and lower is better. Calinski–Harabasz is the between-cluster sum of squares over the within-cluster sum of squares, each divided by its degrees of freedom, like an ANOVA F statistic; higher is better.

On the six points: each cluster's average distance to its centre is 0.654 and the centres are 5.21 apart, so DB = \((0.654 + 0.654)/5.21 = 0.25\). The total sum of squares 43.33 splits into \(W = 2.67\) and \(B = 40.67\), so CH = \((40.67/1)/(2.67/4) = 61.0\). Both are cheap: one pass over the points once you have the centres.

These ask whether two labelings group the points the same way, ignoring what the clusters are called (swapping the labels 0 and 1 changes nothing). Compare the k-means split after iteration 1, {A, C} and {B, D, E, F}, with the final one, {A, B, C} and {D, E, F}.

The problem: chance scores well. With many clusters, most pairs are apart in any labeling, so even random labels agree on them. 1,000 points with 10 random labels against 10 true groups score a Rand index of 0.82.

ARI corrects for chance. It compares the together-in-both count with what random labelings with the same cluster sizes would give:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{expected} &= \frac{7 \times 6}{15} = 2.8 \\ \text{max} &= \frac{7 + 6}{2} = 6.5 \\ \text{ARI} &= \frac{4 - 2.8}{6.5 - 2.8} = 0.32 \end{aligned} \]

In words: how far above chance the agreement is, as a share of the most it could be above chance. 1 is a perfect match, about 0 is random (the 1,000 random points: 0.004), and it can go negative.

Where the two numbers come from. If the final labels were shuffled (keeping the cluster sizes), each of the 7 pairs that are together in iteration 1 would also be together in the final split with probability 6/15, so \(7 \times 6/15 = 2.8\) such pairs are expected by chance. The together-in-both count can't exceed either labeling's own count (7 or 6); ARI uses their average as the ceiling, a convention that keeps the formula symmetric.

NMI (normalized mutual information): how much knowing one labeling tells you about the other, scaled to 0–1. Mutual information \(I\) is divided by the average of the two labelings' entropies \(H\). Entropy is \(H = -\sum p \ln p\) over the cluster shares (the same entropy as in decision trees): \(H(\text{final}) = \ln 2 = 0.693\) and \(H(\text{iteration 1}) = -(\tfrac{2}{6}\ln\tfrac{2}{6} + \tfrac{4}{6}\ln\tfrac{4}{6}) = 0.637\). Mutual information sums \(p_{\text{cell}} \ln\big(p_{\text{cell}}/(p_{\text{row}}\, p_{\text{col}})\big)\) over the table's non-empty cells: \(\tfrac{2}{6}\ln 2 + \tfrac{1}{6}\ln 0.5 + \tfrac{3}{6}\ln 1.5 = 0.231 - 0.116 + 0.203 = 0.318\). So NMI \(= 0.318 / 0.665 = 0.48\). NMI is not corrected for chance and rises with the number of clusters: 200 random clusters against 10 true groups on 1,000 points score NMI 0.24, while ARI and the adjusted version, AMI, stay near 0. Use ARI or AMI when comparing clusterings with different \(k\).

Silhouette, Davies–Bouldin and Calinski–Harabasz all measure compact, well-separated blobs in Euclidean space, the same thing k-means optimizes. On two interleaved moons:

All three internal metrics prefer the wrong answer, because each moon is long and curved: its inner tip sits closer to the other moon's centre than to its own (22% of each moon's points do), so the true labels look “badly separated”. More ways they mislead:

  • They depend on the feature space. Change the scaling or the features and the numbers change; they can't compare clusterings built on different representations.
  • Noise labels need care. DBSCAN's −1 points aren't a cluster; include them and the score drops, drop them and it's flattering.
  • A score on a 2-D t-SNE or UMAP map says nothing about the original space (section 10).
  • Good geometry isn't usefulness. Tight clusters of “new users” and “old users” can be perfectly separated and useless for the decision at hand.

So match the metric to the cluster shape you expect, prefer stability across refits (ARI between bootstrap runs), validate against a small labelled sample when you can, and judge by the downstream decision.

Say it in an interview: “Without labels I use internal metrics: silhouette compares each point's mean distance to its own cluster with the nearest other cluster, Davies–Bouldin compares spread with separation, Calinski–Harabasz is between over within variance. They all reward compact convex clusters, so on curved shapes they prefer k-means's wrong answer. With labels I use ARI, which is corrected for chance, rather than the raw Rand index or NMI, which drifts up with more clusters. Above all I check stability across refits and whether the clusters help the decision.”

07PCA

Principal component analysis turns the axes of the data to line up with the directions it spreads along most, then keeps the first few. The four users: monthly spend \(x_1\) and bill payments \(x_2\), in ₹ thousand: (8, 4), (9, 3), (11, 7), (12, 6). Their mean is (10, 5); subtracting it (centring) gives (−2, −1), (−1, −2), (1, 2), (2, 1). Everything below works on the centred rows.

Why centre: variance is spread around the mean, so directions must start from the mean. Without centring, the top direction points from the origin towards the data's mean instead of along its spread: on the raw four users it comes out as (0.89, 0.46), at 27°, essentially the direction of the mean (10, 5), not the 45° along which they vary. (The ratings example in section 09 isn't centred, and its first direction is just the overall rating level.)

The goal, two ways, and why they're the same

Pick a direction, a unit vector \(u\). Each centred point \(x\) splits into the part along \(u\), of length \(u^\top x\), and the part off it. They're at right angles, so Pythagoras applies to every point:

\[ \begin{aligned} \lVert x \rVert^2 &= (u^\top x)^2 + \big\lVert x - (u^\top x)\,u \big\rVert^2 \\ \underbrace{\sum_i \lVert x_i \rVert^2}_{\text{fixed}} &= \underbrace{\sum_i (u^\top x_i)^2}_{\text{spread along } u} + \underbrace{\sum_i \big\lVert x_i - (u^\top x_i)\,u \big\rVert^2}_{\text{reconstruction error}} \end{aligned} \]

In words: the total squared distance from the mean doesn't depend on \(u\). Whatever spread a direction captures, the rest is the error left if you keep only that direction. So the direction of maximum variance is the direction of minimum reconstruction error: two descriptions of one choice. Scanning angles on the four users (total 20):

Scanning angles works in 2-D. The general answer comes from the covariance matrix.

The covariance matrix

\[ \begin{aligned} C &= \frac{1}{n-1} X_c^\top X_c \\ &= \begin{pmatrix} \operatorname{var}(x_1) & \operatorname{cov}(x_1, x_2) \\ \operatorname{cov}(x_1, x_2) & \operatorname{var}(x_2) \end{pmatrix} \end{aligned} \]

In words: \(X_c\) is the centred data, one row per user. The diagonal holds each feature's variance; the off-diagonal says how the two move together. For the four users: \(\operatorname{var}(x_1) = (4 + 1 + 1 + 4)/3 = 3.33\), \(\operatorname{var}(x_2) = 3.33\), and \(\operatorname{cov}(x_1, x_2) = (2 + 2 + 2 + 2)/3 = 2.67\):

\[ C = \begin{pmatrix} 3.33 & 2.67 \\ 2.67 & 3.33 \end{pmatrix} \]

The variance of the data projected on any unit direction \(u\) is \(u^\top C u\). For \(u = (1, 0)\) that's just \(\operatorname{var}(x_1) = 3.33\), the first row of the table above.

Eigenvectors and eigenvalues, by hand

Which \(u\) maximizes \(u^\top C u\) with \(\lVert u \rVert = 1\)? First without calculus, once the answer is known. Any unit \(u\) is a mix of the two perpendicular eigenvectors found below, \(u = a v_1 + b v_2\) with \(a^2 + b^2 = 1\). Then \(u^\top C u = 6a^2 + 0.67b^2\), a weighted average of the eigenvalues, which is largest with all the weight on \(v_1\). At 30° (15° off \(v_1\)): \(6 \times 0.933 + 0.67 \times 0.067 = 5.64\), the table's value.

The general derivation adds a Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda\) for the length constraint, maximizing \(u^\top C u - \lambda(u^\top u - 1)\). Setting its gradient to zero gives \(2Cu - 2\lambda u = 0\), so

\[ C u = \lambda u \]

In words: the best direction is one that \(C\) only stretches, never turns: an eigenvector, stretched by its eigenvalue \(\lambda\). Its variance is \(u^\top C u = \lambda u^\top u = \lambda\), so the top direction is the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue. The next component maximizes the same thing at right angles to the first, which gives the second eigenvector, and so on.

Step 1: the eigenvalues. \(Cu = \lambda u\) has a non-zero solution only when \(C - \lambda I\) squashes some direction to zero, that is when its determinant is 0:

\[ \begin{aligned} \det(C - \lambda I) &= (3.33 - \lambda)^2 - 2.67^2 = 0 \\ 3.33 - \lambda &= \pm 2.67 \\ \lambda_1 &= 6 \\ \lambda_2 &= 0.67 \end{aligned} \]

In words: for any 2×2 symmetric matrix this is the quadratic \(\lambda^2 - (\text{trace})\lambda + \det = 0\); here the trace is 6.67 and the determinant 4, and the roots are 6 and 0.67. Check: they add to the trace and multiply to the determinant.

Step 2: the eigenvectors. For \(\lambda_1 = 6\): \((C - 6I)u = 0\) reads \(-2.67u_1 + 2.67u_2 = 0\), so \(u_1 = u_2\), and at unit length \(v_1 = (0.707, 0.707)\). Check: \(C(1, 1) = (3.33 + 2.67, 2.67 + 3.33) = (6, 6) = 6 \times (1, 1)\). For \(\lambda_2 = 0.67\): \(v_2 = (0.707, -0.707)\), and \(C(1, -1) = (0.67, -0.67)\).

  • Meaning: PC1 is “spend and bills up together”, PC2 is “one up, the other down”.
  • At right angles, always: \(C\) is symmetric, and a symmetric matrix's eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are perpendicular. So the scores are uncorrelated: their covariance is \(v_1^\top C v_2 = \lambda_2\, v_1^\top v_2 = 0\).
  • Loadings: the entries of \(v_j\), how much each original feature contributes to component \(j\) (here 0.707 each for PC1).
  • Scores (each user's coordinates on the new axes): \(z_1 = X_c v_1 = (-2.12, -2.12, 2.12, 2.12)\), variance 6; \(z_2 = X_c v_2 = (-0.71, 0.71, -0.71, 0.71)\), variance 0.67; their correlation is 0.
  • Signs are arbitrary: \(v\) and \(-v\) are the same axis, so a refit can flip a component's sign.
\[ \begin{aligned} Z &= X_c V_k \\ \hat X &= \bar x + Z V_k^\top \end{aligned} \]

In words: \(V_k\) holds the top \(k\) eigenvectors as columns. Multiply the centred data by it to get the scores (project); multiply the scores by its transpose and add the mean back to reconstruct.

Explained variance, and how many components to keep

The eigenvalues add up to the total variance, the trace of \(C\): \(6 + 0.67 = 3.33 + 3.33\). Turning the axes moves variance between directions without changing the total. The explained variance ratio of component \(j\) is \(\lambda_j / \sum_l \lambda_l\): here 6/6.67 = 90% for PC1 and 10% for PC2.

What you lose is what you drop. Keep PC1 only: the first user is rebuilt as \((10, 5) + (-2.12) \times (0.707, 0.707) = (8.5, 3.5)\), an error of \((-0.5, 0.5)\), squared length 0.5. Over the four users the error is 2.0, which is \((n - 1) \times \lambda_2 = 3 \times 0.67\): the variance of the dropped component.

  • Cumulative explained variance: keep enough for 90–95%. On the 64-pixel digit images that's 21 components for 90% and 29 for 95%.
  • Scree plot: eigenvalues in order; look for the elbow, as with k-means.
  • Best, when PCA feeds a model: treat \(k\) as a hyperparameter and pick it by cross-validated downstream performance, with PCA fitted inside the pipeline (pipelines) so it never sees the validation rows.
  • For a picture: 2 or 3, whatever they explain (28.5% on the digits).

Why standardize first

Variance depends on units. Take age in years and income in rupees (income = 1,000 × age + noise, 500 people). Raw, income's variance is about \(4.5 \times 10^8\) and age's about 90, so PC1 is just income and “explains” 99.99998% of the variance; age is ignored. After standardizing each column to mean 0 and standard deviation 1, PC1 is (0.707, 0.707), “older and richer together”, explaining 72%. With two standardized features of correlation \(r\), PC1 always explains \((1 + \lvert r \rvert)/2\); here \(r = 0.45\).

  • Standardizing first is the same as running PCA on the correlation matrix instead of the covariance matrix.
  • Don't standardize when features share units and bigger variance really does mean more important: pixel intensities, or spend per category when the big categories should dominate. Standardizing would blow tiny-variance noise columns up to the same weight as the signal.
  • Fit the scaler and the PCA on training rows only (scaling).

Whitening

Divide each score by the standard deviation of its component, \(z_j / \sqrt{\lambda_j}\). Every component then has variance 1 and the covariance of the result is the identity. For the four users the whitened scores are \((\pm 0.87, \pm 0.87)\): PC1 shrank by \(\sqrt 6\) and PC2 grew by \(\sqrt{1.5}\).

  • Uses: decorrelated, equal-scale inputs for methods that want them (ICA, some older image pipelines), and Mahalanobis distance, which is plain Euclidean distance after whitening (section 11).
  • The danger: low-variance directions are often noise, and whitening inflates them to the same size as the signal. Keep only the top components before whitening.
  • ZCA whitening rotates back afterwards (\(V \Lambda^{-1/2} V^\top\)), so the whitened features stay close to the original ones.

What PCA cannot do

  • Curved structure. PCA finds flat subspaces only. A spiral or a curved manifold needs kernel PCA, an autoencoder, or UMAP for a picture.
  • Know what matters for the label. PCA never looks at \(y\), and the label may live in a low-variance direction. Spend this month and spend last month: the level varies a lot between users, the month-to-month change very little, but churn depends on the change. PC1 (99% of the variance) predicts churn at 49% accuracy, a coin toss; PC2 (1%) at 90%. Supervised alternatives: PLS (partial least squares: directions that covary with \(y\)) and LDA (linear discriminant analysis: directions that separate the classes).
  • Resist outliers. It's built on squared distances, so one far point can swing PC1 towards itself. Clip first or use robust PCA.
  • Stay interpretable. Every component mixes every feature, with arbitrary signs, and you still need every raw feature at prediction time. Feature selection keeps names and drops inputs; PCA does neither.

Trap

“Keep 95% of the variance” as a rule before a supervised model. Variance is not relevance: the churn signal above is in the 1% you'd throw away. When PCA feeds a model, choose the number of components by validation score, not by variance.

PCA for collinearity

Strongly correlated features show up as tiny eigenvalues: a near-zero eigenvalue means some combination of columns barely varies, so one column is almost a linear function of the others, and that eigenvector names the columns involved (detection and VIF are in collinearity). Principal component regression regresses on the top components instead of the raw columns: the predictors are uncorrelated, so the coefficients are stable, at the price of interpretability. Ridge is the soft version of the same idea: it shrinks the fit most along the small-eigenvalue directions instead of dropping them (ridge).

Say it in an interview: “PCA centres the data and finds orthogonal directions of maximum variance, which by Pythagoras are also the directions of minimum reconstruction error. They're the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, and each eigenvalue is the variance along its direction, so the explained variance ratio is eigenvalue over trace, and the reconstruction error is the variance of what I drop. I standardize unless the units are comparable, choose the number of components by validation when it feeds a model, and remember that it's linear, unsupervised and outlier-sensitive.”

08The SVD

In plain words: rotate, stretch, rotate

Every matrix \(A\) (\(m \times n\), any shape, any rank) can be written as

\[ A = U \Sigma V^\top \]

In words: \(V\)'s columns \(v_j\) are perpendicular unit directions in the input space; \(\Sigma\) is diagonal, holding the singular values \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge 0\); \(U\)'s columns \(u_j\) are perpendicular unit directions in the output space. Read right to left, multiplying by \(A\) means: turn the space so the \(v_j\) line up with the axes (\(V^\top\)), stretch axis \(j\) by \(\sigma_j\) (\(\Sigma\)), and turn the result so the axes line up with the \(u_j\) (\(U\)). One column at a time:

\[ A v_j = \sigma_j u_j \]

In words: input direction \(v_j\) comes out as output direction \(u_j\), scaled by \(\sigma_j\). The number of non-zero \(\sigma_j\) is the rank of \(A\). (“Rotate” may include a reflection.) This is the singular value decomposition.

A small SVD, by hand

Take \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}\).

  1. Form \(A^\top A\). Since \(A^\top A = V \Sigma U^\top U \Sigma V^\top = V \Sigma^2 V^\top\) (because \(U^\top U = I\)), its eigenvectors are the \(v_j\) and its eigenvalues are the \(\sigma_j^2\). Likewise \(AA^\top = U\Sigma^2 U^\top\): \(U\) holds the eigenvectors of \(AA^\top\), with the same non-zero eigenvalues. If you do go through one of these products, pick the smaller: with more features than rows, \(AA^\top\) is the small one. And centred data with \(n\) rows has at most \(n - 1\) non-zero singular values, so at most \(n - 1\) useful components. \(A^\top A = \begin{pmatrix} 25 & 20 \\ 20 & 25 \end{pmatrix}\), the same pattern as the covariance matrix in section 07, so the eigenvalues are \(25 \pm 20\) = 45 and 5, with eigenvectors along (1, 1) and (−1, 1).
  2. Singular values: \(\sigma_1 = \sqrt{45} = 6.71\), \(\sigma_2 = \sqrt 5 = 2.24\). \(v_1 = (1, 1)/\sqrt 2\), \(v_2 = (-1, 1)/\sqrt 2\).
  3. Left vectors from \(u_j = A v_j / \sigma_j\): \(A v_1 = (3, 9)/\sqrt 2\), and dividing by \(\sqrt{45}\) gives \(u_1 = (1, 3)/\sqrt{10}\). \(A v_2 = (-3, 1)/\sqrt 2\), and dividing by \(\sqrt 5\) gives \(u_2 = (-3, 1)/\sqrt{10}\).
  4. Check: \(U\Sigma V^\top\) multiplies back to \(A\). \(V^\top\) turns by −45° and \(U\) by +71.6°. And \(\lvert \det A \rvert = 15 = 6.71 \times 2.24\): the area of the ellipse over the area of the circle.

Fine for 2×2 with a pen. Numerical libraries compute the SVD directly, without forming \(A^\top A\); the last subsection of this section says why.

PCA is the SVD of the centred data

Take the SVD of the centred data, \(X_c = U \Sigma V^\top\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} C &= \frac{X_c^\top X_c}{n-1} \\ &= V \, \frac{\Sigma^2}{n-1} \, V^\top \\ \lambda_j &= \frac{\sigma_j^2}{n-1} \\ Z &= X_c V \\ &= U \Sigma \end{aligned} \]

In words: the right singular vectors \(V\) are the principal directions, the squared singular values divided by \(n - 1\) are the eigenvalues, and \(U\Sigma\) are the scores. PCA needs nothing else.

On the four users. \(X_c^\top X_c = \begin{pmatrix} 10 & 8 \\ 8 & 10 \end{pmatrix}\), eigenvalues 18 and 2, so \(\sigma = \sqrt{18} = 4.24\) and \(\sqrt 2 = 1.41\). Divided by \(n - 1 = 3\): \(18/3 = 6\) and \(2/3 = 0.67\), the eigenvalues from section 07. The left vectors:

  • \(u_1 = X_c v_1 / \sigma_1 = (-3, -3, 3, 3)/\sqrt 2 / \sqrt{18} = (-0.5, -0.5, 0.5, 0.5)\).
  • \(u_2 = X_c v_2 / \sigma_2 = (-0.5, 0.5, -0.5, 0.5)\).
  • Scores \(U \Sigma\): the first column is \(4.24 \times (-0.5, -0.5, 0.5, 0.5) = (-2.12, -2.12, 2.12, 2.12)\), the PC1 scores again.

A matrix as a sum of layers. The SVD also writes \(X_c\) as a sum of rank-one pieces, one per singular value, in order of importance:

\[ X_c = \sigma_1 u_1 v_1^\top + \sigma_2 u_2 v_2^\top \]

In words: each layer is one column pattern times one row pattern, scaled by its singular value. The first layer here has rows (−1.5, −1.5), (−1.5, −1.5), (1.5, 1.5), (1.5, 1.5): exactly the PC1 reconstruction minus the mean. Dropping layers is the next section.

Why compute it with the SVD, not \(X^\top X\)

  • Forming \(X^\top X\) squares the condition number (the ratio of the largest to the smallest singular value, which measures how much rounding errors can grow). Two nearly duplicate columns: the data matrix has condition number 19,157; \(X^\top X\) has 367 million, which is \(19{,}157^2\). Float64 keeps about 16 significant digits, so squaring the condition number loses twice as many of them.
  • An example where it loses everything (Läuchli's matrix): \(L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 10^{-8} & 0 \\ 0 & 10^{-8} \end{pmatrix}\), whose true singular values are 1.414 and \(10^{-8}\). The SVD of \(L\) returns both. But \(L^\top L\) has \(1 + 10^{-16}\) on its diagonal, which rounds to exactly 1 in float64, so the stored matrix is \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\), whose eigenvalues are 2 and 0. The small direction is gone before any eigenvalue routine runs.
  • Only the top few are needed, and truncated or randomized SVD computes just those, working directly on huge or sparse matrices (no dense \(X^\top X\), no centring that would fill in the zeros). That's how LSA runs on a term-document matrix.
  • Cost: a full SVD of an \(m \times n\) matrix (\(m \ge n\)) is \(O(mn^2)\); a randomized top-\(k\) SVD is about \(O(mnk)\).

For tall, narrow, well-conditioned data the covariance route is faster and usually accurate enough, and libraries sometimes take it (sklearn's PCA picks a solver by shape). The precision argument bites when some directions have tiny variance next to others: exactly the collinear case.

Say it in an interview: “The SVD writes any matrix as \(U\Sigma V^\top\): rotate, stretch by the singular values, rotate. PCA is the SVD of the centred data matrix: \(V\) holds the principal directions, the eigenvalues of the covariance are \(\sigma^2/(n-1)\), and \(U\Sigma\) are the scores. I compute PCA by SVD rather than eigendecomposing \(X^\top X\), because forming \(X^\top X\) squares the condition number and loses the small directions, and truncated SVD handles huge or sparse data.”

09Low-rank approximation

Eckart–Young: keep the top singular values

Write \(A = \sum_j \sigma_j u_j v_j^\top\) and keep the first \(k\) layers: \(A_k = \sum_{j \le k} \sigma_j u_j v_j^\top\). The Eckart–Young theorem says no rank-\(k\) matrix is closer to \(A\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \lVert A - A_k \rVert_F^2 &= \sum_{j \gt k} \sigma_j^2 \\ \lVert A - A_k \rVert_2 &= \sigma_{k+1} \end{aligned} \]

In words: in squared error summed over all entries (the Frobenius norm), the best rank-\(k\) approximation misses exactly the squared singular values you dropped; in the worst-direction sense (the spectral norm) it misses the largest one you dropped. PCA's “the error is \((n - 1)\) times the dropped eigenvalues”, that is the dropped \(\sigma^2\), is this theorem applied to the centred data.

Where low rank is used

  • Compression. A rank-\(k\) approximation of an \(m \times n\) matrix stores \(k(m + n + 1)\) numbers. A 1,000 × 1,000 image at rank 50 needs 100,050 numbers instead of a million: 10%.
  • Denoising. Signal is often low rank and noise spreads over every direction. A 200 × 50 matrix of rank 3 plus unit noise has singular values 125, 102, 90, then 20.7, 20.0, 19.0, … : three stand out, the rest form a noise floor. The root-mean-square error against the clean signal is 1.33 keeping 1 layer, 0.28 keeping 3, 0.58 keeping 10, and 1.00 keeping all 50 (the noise itself). Cut at the gap.
  • LSA. A truncated SVD of a TF-IDF or PPMI term-document matrix gives each word and document a dense vector, so “car” and “automobile” land close together through shared contexts. The details live in classical NLP; it uses truncated SVD without centring to keep the matrix sparse.
  • Recommenders by matrix factorization. Model the ratings as user factors times item factors, \(R \approx P Q^\top\) with rank \(k\), so a user's predicted rating of a film is the dot product of their taste vector and the film's vector, as in the example. But real rating matrices are mostly blank, and plain SVD would treat every blank as a zero rating. So the factors are fitted on the observed entries only, with an L2 penalty, by alternating least squares or SGD (the “Funk SVD” of the Netflix Prize), usually with a bias per user and per item; implicit feedback (views, clicks) uses weighted ALS. The learned vectors also serve as embeddings for retrieving candidates.
  • Low-rank adapters (LoRA). Fine-tuning learns a weight update \(\Delta W = BA\) of rank \(r\) instead of a full matrix: for a 4,096 × 4,096 layer and \(r = 8\), that's 65,536 trainable numbers instead of 16.8 million (0.39%). It belongs in a fine-tuning note; the bet behind it is that the update a new task needs is close to low rank.

Say it in an interview: “Truncating the SVD to the top \(k\) singular values gives the best rank-\(k\) approximation in Frobenius and spectral norm; the squared error is the sum of the dropped singular values squared. Recommenders use the same factorization idea, but fit the factors on the observed ratings only, with regularization, by ALS or SGD, because blanks aren't zeros.”

10t-SNE and UMAP

Both draw high-dimensional data (embeddings, gene expression, images) as a 2-D map in which points that are neighbours in the original space stay neighbours. They keep who is near whom, not distances, which is what makes them good for pictures and bad for anything else.

t-SNE, in words

  1. Neighbour probabilities in the original space. For each point \(i\), turn its distances to the others into probabilities with a Gaussian centred on \(i\): near points get most of the probability. The Gaussian's width is set separately for each point, so that the effective number of neighbours equals the perplexity (formally, 2 to the power of the entropy of those probabilities). Dense regions get narrow Gaussians, sparse ones wide. The probabilities are then made symmetric, \(p_{ij}\).
  2. Neighbour probabilities on the map. The same for the 2-D points, \(q_{ij}\), but with a heavy-tailed Student-t curve (one degree of freedom) instead of a Gaussian.
  3. Move the 2-D points by gradient descent to make the two sets of probabilities match, minimizing the KL divergence:
\[ \text{KL}(P \,\Vert\, Q) = \sum_{i \ne j} p_{ij} \log \frac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}} \]

In words: a pair that is close in the data (large \(p\)) but far apart on the map (small \(q\)) costs a lot; a pair that is far in the data but drawn close costs little. So local neighbourhoods are kept faithfully and global layout isn't. The heavy tails let moderately distant points sit far apart on the map, which relieves the “crowding problem”: there isn't room in 2-D for everything that is moderately far in 50-D.

  • Perplexity: typical values 5 to 50, default 30. Small values show fine local structure (and can invent clumps in noise); large ones show more of the layout. It must be well below the number of points.
  • Cost: exact t-SNE is \(O(n^2)\); the Barnes–Hut approximation is \(O(n \log n)\). The objective is non-convex: different seeds give different maps, and initializing from PCA makes runs more stable. Standard t-SNE has no transform for new points.

UMAP, in words

UMAP builds a weighted graph of each point's nearest neighbours (n_neighbors, default 15: small values stress local detail, large ones global layout), then lays out a 2-D graph whose edges match it, by minimizing a cross-entropy that pulls neighbours together and pushes random non-neighbours apart. min_dist sets how tightly points may pack on the map. Compared with t-SNE it's faster and scales to millions of points, often keeps more of the global arrangement (though still not reliably), can transform new points, and can output more than two dimensions.

Visualize with it; cluster and model in the original space or on PCA components. UMAP followed by HDBSCAN is a known pipeline, but validate its clusters in the original space too.

Say it in an interview: “t-SNE converts distances into neighbour probabilities, Gaussian in the data with a per-point width set by perplexity and Student-t on the map, and minimizes the KL divergence, so it preserves local neighbourhoods but not global distances. UMAP does the same job with a k-nearest-neighbour graph and a cross-entropy, and is faster and keeps somewhat more global structure. I use both for visualization only: cluster sizes and gaps on the map mean little, so I check anything interesting in the original space.”

11Anomaly detection

Anomalies are rare points that don't fit the rest: fraud, faulty sensors, data errors, abuse. Labels are usually few, late and biased towards what was already caught, so most methods score how unusual each point is, and the score ranks points. A reviewer, or labels that arrive later, decides what is actually bad, and review capacity sets the threshold.

One feature: z-scores and robust z-scores

Flag values far from the centre in units of spread. The plain z-score uses the mean and standard deviation, which the outliers themselves inflate (masking); the robust version uses the median and the median absolute deviation. Both, with their thresholds, are in outliers. They miss points that are normal on every feature but odd in combination, which is what the rest of this section is for.

Mahalanobis distance

Measure distance from the centre in standard deviations along each principal direction, so a step against the data's correlation counts for more than the same step along it:

\[ d_M(x)^2 = (x - \mu)^\top C^{-1} (x - \mu) \]

In words: split the offset \(x - \mu\) along the principal directions, square each piece, divide by that direction's variance and add up, \(d_M^2 = \sum_j \big(v_j^\top (x - \mu)\big)^2/\lambda_j\). That's ordinary distance after whitening (section 07).

On the four users (mean (10, 5), covariance with eigenvalues 6 along (1, 1) and 0.67 along (1, −1)), take two new users, both \(\sqrt 8 = 2.83\) from the mean:

  • A threshold: for normal data, \(d_M^2\) follows a chi-square distribution with \(d\) degrees of freedom. With \(d = 2\), the 1% cut-off is 9.21, and \(P(\chi^2_2 \gt 12) = 0.0025\).
  • PCA reconstruction error is a close cousin: keep the top components and score each point by its squared distance off that subspace. Keeping PC1 only, (12, 7) scores 0 and (12, 3) scores 8: it's entirely off the usual direction.
  • Caveats: the mean and covariance are estimated from data that contains the anomalies, which pull them; use a robust estimate (minimum covariance determinant; sklearn's EllipticEnvelope). It assumes one elliptical cloud, and it needs many more rows than features (four users are only an illustration).

Isolation forest and LOF

Isolation forest: random trees with random cuts isolate an odd point in few cuts, so a short average path means an anomaly; the usual default for large tabular data. Worked by hand in random forests.

Local outlier factor (LOF) compares each point's density with its neighbours' densities, so it finds points that are odd for their region:

  1. The reachability distance from \(a\) to a neighbour \(b\) is \(\max(\text{k-distance}(b), d(a, b))\): the real distance, but never less than \(b\)'s own distance to its \(k\)-th neighbour. That smooths out tiny distances inside dense clumps.
  2. The local reachability density of \(a\) is 1 over its average reachability distance to its \(k\) neighbours.
  3. \(\text{LOF}(a)\) is the average density of \(a\)'s neighbours divided by \(a\)'s own. About 1: as dense as its neighbourhood. Well above 1: much sparser than its neighbours.

Why local matters. A tight cluster (standard deviation 0.2) and a loose one (1.5), 50 points each, plus two test points: one 0.9 from the tight cluster's centre, one 2.5 from the loose cluster's centre. With \(k = 10\):

LOF costs \(O(n^2)\) without an index and is sensitive to \(k\); in sklearn, scoring new points needs its novelty mode.

Other scores, one line each

  • One-class SVM: learns a boundary around normal data in a kernel space, with \(\nu\) roughly the share allowed outside; sensitive to scaling and the kernel width, and its fit time grows faster than the square of the number of rows, so it suits tens of thousands of rows, not millions.
  • Autoencoder reconstruction error: a network trained to reconstruct normal data rebuilds anomalies badly; a non-linear version of the PCA score above. Needs mostly clean training data.
  • From this note: low GMM density (section 03), distance to the nearest k-means centre (section 02), DBSCAN noise (section 05).

Evaluating with few labels

  • Treat it as ranking, at the review budget. If analysts can check 100 cases a day, measure precision in the top 100 (precision at k): of those flagged, how many were confirmed.
  • Label a stratified sample. A purely random sample holds almost no positives. Label the top-scored cases plus a random sample of the rest, then weight back to estimate precision and recall.
  • Use precision–recall, not ROC, when positives are rare: average precision reflects how clean the top of the list is (PR-AUC).
  • Inject known anomalies (synthetic or replayed past cases) to check recall on specific patterns, remembering that synthetic anomalies are easier than real ones.
  • Watch the score over time: the alert volume and the score distribution drift when the data or the attackers change.
  • Close the loop, and beware selection bias. Confirmed cases become labels for a supervised model (or the score becomes one of its features). But reviewers only see what was flagged, so misses stay invisible; keep a small random audit sample to measure what the detector misses.

Say it in an interview: “With no labels I'd score unusualness and rank: robust z-scores per feature, Mahalanobis distance or PCA reconstruction error for broken correlations, isolation forest as the scalable default, LOF when 'odd' depends on the local density. The threshold comes from review capacity. To evaluate with few labels I'd measure precision at the review budget, label a stratified sample to estimate recall, use average precision rather than ROC-AUC, and keep a random audit sample, because reviewers only ever see what the detector flagged.”

k-means with k-means++

import numpy as np

def kmeanspp_init(X, k, rng):
    n = len(X)
    C = [X[rng.integers(n)]]           # 1st: uniform
    for _ in range(k - 1):
        d2 = ((X[:, None] - np.array(C)[None])**2
              ).sum(-1).min(1)         # D(x)^2
        C.append(X[rng.choice(n, p=d2 / d2.sum())])
    return np.array(C, dtype=float)

def kmeans(X, k, C=None, n_iter=100, seed=0):
    X = np.asarray(X, dtype=float)
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    C = (kmeanspp_init(X, k, rng) if C is None
         else np.array(C, dtype=float))
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        d2 = ((X[:, None] - C[None])**2).sum(-1)
        lab = d2.argmin(1)                 # assign
        newC = C.copy()
        for j in range(k):                 # update
            if (lab == j).any():           # empty: keep
                newC[j] = X[lab == j].mean(0)
        if np.allclose(newC, C):
            break
        C = newC
    J = ((X - C[lab])**2).sum()            # inertia
    return C, lab, J

def npdf(x, m, s):
    return np.exp(-(x - m)**2 / (2 * s**2)) \
           / (s * np.sqrt(2 * np.pi))

def em_gmm_1d(x, pi, mu, sd, n_iter=200, tol=1e-8):
    x = np.asarray(x, dtype=float)
    pi, mu, sd = map(np.array, (pi, mu, sd))
    ll_old = -np.inf
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        # E-step: responsibilities, n x K
        w = pi * npdf(x[:, None], mu, sd)
        r = w / w.sum(1, keepdims=True)
        ll = np.log(w.sum(1)).sum()  # at old params
        # M-step: weighted MLE per component
        Nk = r.sum(0)
        pi = Nk / len(x)
        mu = (r * x[:, None]).sum(0) / Nk
        var = (r * (x[:, None] - mu)**2).sum(0) / Nk
        sd = np.sqrt(var + 1e-6)     # floor: no collapse
        if ll - ll_old < tol:
            break
        ll_old = ll
    return pi, mu, sd, ll

PCA, by eigendecomposition and by SVD

def pca_eig(X, k):
    mu = X.mean(0)
    Xc = X - mu
    C = Xc.T @ Xc / (len(X) - 1)
    vals, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(C)     # ascending
    order = np.argsort(vals)[::-1][:k]
    V, lam = vecs[:, order], vals[order]
    Z = Xc @ V                          # scores
    return Z, V, lam, lam / np.trace(C)

def pca_svd(X, k):
    mu = X.mean(0)
    Xc = X - mu
    U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(Xc, full_matrices=False)
    lam = S**2 / (len(X) - 1)           # eigenvalues
    Z = U[:, :k] * S[:k]                # = Xc @ V_k
    return Z, Vt[:k].T, lam[:k], lam[:k] / lam.sum()

def reconstruct(Z, V, mu):
    return mu + Z @ V.T

from collections import deque

def dbscan(X, eps, min_pts):
    n = len(X)
    D = np.sqrt(((X[:, None] - X[None])**2).sum(-1))
    nbrs = [np.flatnonzero(D[i] <= eps) for i in range(n)]
    core = np.array([len(nb) >= min_pts for nb in nbrs])
    lab = np.full(n, -1)                # -1 = noise
    c = 0
    for i in range(n):
        if not core[i] or lab[i] != -1:
            continue
        lab[i] = c                      # new cluster
        q = deque([i])
        while q:                        # grow by BFS
            p = q.popleft()
            if not core[p]:
                continue                # border: no expand
            for j in nbrs[p]:
                if lab[j] == -1:
                    lab[j] = c
                    q.append(j)
        c += 1
    return lab, core

14Interview questions

Answer out loud first, then open the answer. Each answer stands on its own: the short answer, the mechanism, the why, then the trade-off or the likely follow-up. Questions 3, 11, 16, 19 and 20 are hand drills; do them with a pen. Question 8 is the from-scratch build.

1What is unsupervised learning used for, and why is it hard to evaluate?

Finding structure in features with no target: segments, compressed features, pictures, anomalies, and features for supervised models. It's hard to evaluate because there's no ground truth.

  • Uses: customer segmentation; dimensionality reduction (21 of 64 PCA components keep 90% of the digit images' variance); 2-D maps of embeddings; fraud and fault detection where labels are scarce; cluster IDs, PCA scores or SVD document vectors as model features.
  • Why evaluation is hard: no held-out \(y\); several clusterings can be equally valid for different purposes; every algorithm returns an answer, even on noise; internal metrics encode the algorithm's own assumptions.
  • What to do: fix the use first, then check stability across refits (ARI between bootstrap runs), usefulness downstream, domain sense, and any labels you have.

(01)

2Walk through k-means. What does it minimize, and why does it converge?

It minimizes the within-cluster sum of squared distances to the centres, by alternating assign and update steps, each of which can only lower that objective.

  • Objective: \(J = \sum_i \lVert x_i - \mu_{c(i)} \rVert^2\), the inertia.
  • Assign: each point to its nearest centre, so each point's term can only shrink.
  • Update: each centre to the mean of its points, because for a fixed group \(\sum \lVert x - z \rVert^2 = \sum \lVert x - \bar x \rVert^2 + n\lVert \bar x - z \rVert^2\), smallest at \(z = \bar x\).
  • Convergence: \(J \ge 0\) never increases and there are finitely many partitions, so it stops; it's coordinate descent. But at a local minimum, not necessarily the global one (NP-hard in general).
  • On the six points from a bad start: \(J\) went 80, 21.25, 7.94, 2.67, then nothing moved.

Follow-up: cost is \(O(nkd)\) per iteration. (02)

(02)

4Why does k-means get stuck in local minima, and how does k-means++ help?

Lloyd's steps only make local improvements, so a bad start can reach a split where neither step helps. k-means++ starts with centres spread out by sampling each new one with probability proportional to its squared distance from the nearest chosen centre.

  • Example: corners (0, 0), (0, 1), (4, 0), (4, 1). Starting at (0, 0) and (0, 1) gives top versus bottom, \(J = 16\), and nothing moves; the best split, left versus right, has \(J = 1\).
  • k-means++ on it: after picking (0, 0), the squared distances are 0, 1, 16, 17, so the second centre lands on the right with probability 33/34 = 97%.
  • Guarantee: expected \(J\) at most \(8(\ln k + 2)\) times the optimum, from the seeding alone.
  • Why not farthest-point: it would pick outliers every time; \(D^2\) sampling favours far regions with many points.
  • In practice: several seeds (n_init), keep the lowest \(J\).

(02)

5How do you choose k?

Not by minimizing \(J\), which always falls with \(k\). Shortlist with the elbow, silhouette or gap statistic, then decide on stability and usefulness.

  • Elbow: where extra clusters stop reducing \(J\) much. Often vague on real data.
  • Silhouette: mean \((b - a)/\max(a, b)\); pick the peak.
  • Gap statistic: compare \(\log J_k\) with its average on uniform reference data over the same bounding box; choose the smallest \(k\) with \(\text{Gap}(k) \ge \text{Gap}(k+1) - s_{k+1}\). On 400 points from 4 blobs: elbow, silhouette (0.606) and gap (0.862) all point to 4.
  • BIC if you fit a Gaussian mixture.
  • Then: stability across bootstrap refits (ARI), whether the business can name and act on each segment, and downstream value.

(02)

6When does k-means fail? What happens if you don't scale the features?

Whenever its hidden assumptions break: round, similar-sized clusters, under a Euclidean distance that's meaningful, with no big outliers.

  • Why those assumptions: each point goes to the nearer centre, so borders are straight lines halfway between centres; every cluster is a convex cell whose reach ignores its spread.
  • Unscaled features: distance is dominated by the biggest-unit column. Age groups plus an irrelevant income in rupees: ARI 0.004; standardized, 1.0.
  • Curved shapes: two moons, ARI 0.27 (DBSCAN and single linkage get 1.0).
  • Unequal spreads: a tight 50-point group steals 137 points from a spread-out 500-point group (ARI 0.20; a GMM gets 0.99).
  • Outliers: one whale takes a whole cluster; log first.
  • Categorical data: means of one-hot codes are meaningless; use k-modes, k-prototypes or k-medoids with Gower distance.

(02)

7k-means on 50 million users, or on data with categorical columns: what changes?

For scale, mini-batch k-means; for categorical or mixed data, k-modes, k-prototypes or k-medoids with a suitable distance.

  • Mini-batch: each step assigns a random batch and moves each centre towards its batch points with step 1/(points seen), a running mean. Cost per step is independent of \(n\), and data can stream. On 200,000 points the inertia came within 0.03% of full k-means.
  • Also: compute the silhouette on a sample (it's \(O(n^2)\)); k-means++ seeding is \(k\) passes, so seed on a sample if needed.
  • k-medoids: centres are real data points, any distance (Gower for mixed types), robust to outliers (for 1, 2, 3, 4, 100, the medoid is 3, the mean 22), but the swap search is \(O(k(n-k)^2)\) per iteration.
  • k-prototypes: squared Euclidean on numeric columns plus \(\gamma\) times the number of categorical mismatches; numeric centres are means, categorical ones modes.

8Write k-means from scratch.

Seed with k-means++, then loop assign and update until the centres stop moving; return centres, labels and inertia.

def kmeans(X, k, n_iter=100, seed=0):
    X = np.asarray(X, dtype=float)
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    C = [X[rng.integers(len(X))]]
    for _ in range(k - 1):           # k-means++
        d2 = ((X[:, None] - np.array(C)[None])**2
              ).sum(-1).min(1)
        C.append(X[rng.choice(len(X),
                              p=d2 / d2.sum())])
    C = np.array(C, dtype=float)
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        d2 = ((X[:, None] - C[None])**2).sum(-1)
        lab = d2.argmin(1)           # assign
        newC = np.array([X[lab == j].mean(0)
                         if (lab == j).any() else C[j]
                         for j in range(k)])  # update
        if np.allclose(newC, C):
            break
        C = newC
    return C, lab, ((X - C[lab])**2).sum()
  • Points to mention: broadcasting gives the \(n \times k\) distance matrix; an empty cluster keeps its old centre (sklearn instead moves it to a far point); run several seeds and keep the lowest inertia; scale features first.
  • On the six points it returns centres (1.33, 1.33) and (5.33, 4.67), inertia 2.67.

(12)

9What is a Gaussian mixture model, and how does it differ from k-means?

A model of the data as a weighted sum of Gaussians, each with its own mean, covariance and share, fitted by EM. It gives soft memberships and elliptical clusters of different sizes, where k-means gives hard labels and round, equal clusters.

  • Density: \(p(x) = \sum_j \pi_j \mathcal N(x \mid \mu_j, \Sigma_j)\).
  • Gains: soft responsibilities (a user can be 92% one segment); different shapes and spreads (on the 500-versus-50 example, ARI 0.99 against k-means's 0.20); a likelihood, so BIC can choose \(k\); a density for anomaly scores.
  • Costs: many more parameters (a full covariance per component is \(d(d+1)/2\) numbers), slower, local optima, and components can collapse onto single points.

(03)

10Explain EM. Why does the likelihood never decrease?

EM maximizes a likelihood with hidden variables by alternating a soft guess of the hidden variables (E) and a maximization as if they were known (M). Each round maximizes a lower bound that touches the likelihood at the current parameters, so the likelihood can't fall.

  • The problem: \(\ell(\theta) = \sum_i \log \sum_j \pi_j \mathcal N(x_i \mid \theta_j)\) has a sum inside the log, so no closed form. If each point's component were known it would be easy.
  • E-step: responsibilities \(r_{ij}\), the posterior over each point's component, by Bayes' rule.
  • M-step: weighted MLE: \(\pi_j = N_j/n\), \(\mu_j = \sum_i r_{ij}x_i/N_j\), covariance likewise.
  • Why monotone: by Jensen, \(\log p(x_i) \ge \sum_j q_{ij} \log(\pi_j \mathcal N_j / q_{ij})\) for any \(q\), with equality at \(q = r\). E makes the bound tight, M raises the bound, so \(\ell_{\text{new}} \ge \text{bound}_{\text{new}} \ge \text{bound}_{\text{old}} = \ell_{\text{old}}\).
  • Limits: converges to a local optimum; depends on initialization (k-means is the usual start).

12Show that k-means is a special case of a Gaussian mixture.

Fix all shares equal and all covariances to the same \(\sigma^2 I\), and let \(\sigma \to 0\): the responsibilities harden to 0 or 1 for the nearest centre, and EM becomes Lloyd's algorithm.

  • Responsibilities: \(r_{ij} \propto e^{-\lVert x_i - \mu_j \rVert^2 / 2\sigma^2}\); as \(\sigma\) shrinks the nearest centre's term dominates. For \(x = 3\) between centres 2 and 7: 0.70 at \(\sigma = 3\), 0.87 at 2, 0.9994 at 1.
  • E-step becomes assign to the nearest centre; M-step becomes the mean of the assigned points.
  • Consequence: k-means inherits the assumption of round clusters with equal spread and (roughly) equal weight.

13How do you choose the number of mixture components? What goes wrong with too many?

BIC, \(p \ln n - 2 \ln \hat L\), lowest wins; with too many components, some collapse onto one or two points and the likelihood blows up.

  • Example: 500 points from two Gaussians: BIC 2392.5, 1977.9, 1996.1 for 1, 2, 3 components. The third component gains 0.2 in log-likelihood but costs \(3 \ln 500 = 18.6\).
  • Collapse: a component on a single point with \(\sigma \to 0\) has unbounded density. On seven points, a 3-component fit parks one component on the lone point 4.5: log-likelihood −8.3 against −15.0, and BIC wrongly prefers it.
  • Guards: a variance floor (reg_covar), a Bayesian GMM, simpler covariance types, enough data per component, and distrust of tiny components.
  • AIC penalizes less (2 per parameter) and picks more components.

14Compare single, complete, average and Ward linkage.

They define distance between groups as the nearest pair, the farthest pair, the average pair, or the increase in within-cluster variance, and that choice sets the cluster shapes.

  • Single: follows chains and finds any shape (two moons: ARI 1.0), equivalent to cutting the longest edges of the minimum spanning tree; but one noise point can bridge clusters (chaining).
  • Complete: compact clusters of similar diameter; an outlier inflates the maximum.
  • Average: in between.
  • Ward: merge cost \(\frac{n_a n_b}{n_a + n_b}\lVert \mu_a - \mu_b \rVert^2\), a greedy k-means; round, similar-sized clusters; Euclidean only.
  • Example, points 0, 2, 3, 6, 10, 15: single splits {0, 2, 3, 6, 10} | {15}; complete and Ward split {0, 2, 3} | {6, 10, 15}.
  • Cost: \(O(n^2)\) memory for the distance matrix (40 GB at 100,000 points), so up to tens of thousands of points.

15Explain DBSCAN. How do you choose eps and minPts, and when does it fail?

Clusters are dense regions: core points have at least minPts points within eps, clusters are core points chained through each other's neighbourhoods plus the border points they reach, and everything else is noise.

  • Example: eps 1.5, minPts 3 on the six points plus H (3, 1) and G (9, 1): {A, B, C} core with H as a border point, {D, E, F} core, G noise.
  • eps: the knee of the sorted k-distance plot (distance to the (minPts − 1)-th nearest other point). minPts: at least features + 1, often twice the number of features; larger for noisy data. Scale first.
  • Strengths: no \(k\), any shape, explicit noise.
  • Fails on varying density: a radius small enough to split two tight clusters calls most of a loose cluster noise (123 of 150 points), and a larger one merges the tight pair. HDBSCAN fixes this (ARI 0.98 on that example). Also fails in high dimensions.
  • New points: no predict; use the cluster of a core point within eps.

17Internal versus external cluster metrics: what are they, and why can the silhouette mislead? Why ARI rather than the Rand index or NMI?

Internal metrics (silhouette, Davies–Bouldin, Calinski–Harabasz) score compactness and separation from the data alone; external ones (ARI, NMI) compare with known labels. Internal metrics reward round blobs, and ARI is the one corrected for chance.

  • Misleading: on two moons, k-means's wrong split (ARI 0.27) beats the true moons on all three: silhouette 0.49 against 0.33, DB 0.78 against 1.16, CH 586 against 261.
  • Rand index counts agreeing pairs, and random labels score high when there are many clusters (0.82 for 10 random clusters on 1,000 points).
  • ARI = (index − expected)/(max − expected): about 0 for random, 1 for perfect. Example from the note: 4 together-in-both pairs, expected 2.8, max 6.5, so ARI 0.32.
  • NMI isn't chance-corrected and rises with more clusters (0.24 for 200 random clusters); use ARI or AMI to compare different \(k\).

18Derive PCA. Why are the principal components eigenvectors of the covariance matrix?

Maximize the variance of the data projected on a unit vector \(u\), \(u^\top C u\), subject to \(u^\top u = 1\); the Lagrange condition is \(Cu = \lambda u\), and the variance achieved is \(\lambda\).

  1. Centre the data; the projected scores are \(X_c u\), with variance \(\frac{1}{n-1}\lVert X_c u \rVert^2 = u^\top C u\).
  2. Lagrangian \(u^\top C u - \lambda(u^\top u - 1)\); gradient \(2Cu - 2\lambda u = 0\), so \(u\) is an eigenvector.
  3. Variance \(= u^\top C u = \lambda\), so take the largest eigenvalue.
  4. The next component maximizes the same thing orthogonal to the first: the next eigenvector. \(C\) is symmetric, so they're orthogonal and the scores are uncorrelated.

Follow-up: the same directions minimize reconstruction error (question 21). (07)

21Show that maximizing variance and minimizing reconstruction error give the same direction.

For each centred point, Pythagoras splits its squared length into the part along \(u\) and the part off it; the total is fixed, so maximizing one minimizes the other.

  • \(\lVert x \rVert^2 = (u^\top x)^2 + \lVert x - (u^\top x)u \rVert^2\), because the two parts are perpendicular.
  • Sum over points: \(\sum \lVert x_i \rVert^2\) doesn't depend on \(u\), so maximizing \(\sum (u^\top x_i)^2\) (variance times \(n - 1\)) minimizes \(\sum\) residual².
  • Four users: total 20; at 45°, 18 along and 2 off; at 0°, 10 and 10; at 135°, 2 and 18.
  • For \(k\) components the same argument works with the projection onto a \(k\)-dimensional subspace; the error equals \((n - 1)\) times the sum of the dropped eigenvalues.

22Should you standardize before PCA? How many components do you keep? When does PCA hurt a supervised model?

Standardize unless the units are shared and meaningful; choose the number of components by validation when PCA feeds a model; PCA hurts when the label lives in a low-variance direction.

  • Standardize: otherwise the biggest-unit feature is PC1 (income in rupees: 99.99998% of the variance). Standardizing = PCA on the correlation matrix. Don't for pixels or same-unit spends.
  • How many: 90–95% cumulative variance (21 and 29 of 64 for digits), a scree elbow, or best, cross-validation of the downstream model with PCA inside the pipeline.
  • When it hurts: spend this month and last month: PC1 (the level, 99% of variance) gives 49% churn accuracy; PC2 (the change, 1%) gives 90%. PCA never sees \(y\); PLS or LDA do.
  • Also: components mix every feature (less interpretable), and you still need every raw feature at prediction time.

23What is the SVD, how does it relate to PCA, and why compute PCA with it?

\(A = U\Sigma V^\top\): rotate, stretch along the axes by the singular values, rotate. PCA is the SVD of the centred data: \(V\) holds the directions, \(\sigma^2/(n-1)\) are the eigenvalues, \(U\Sigma\) the scores.

  • Why: \(X_c^\top X_c = V\Sigma^2 V^\top\), so the eigenvectors of the covariance are \(V\).
  • Four users: \(\sigma = 4.24, 1.41\); \(\sigma^2/3 = 6, 0.67\), the eigenvalues.
  • Numerics: forming \(X^\top X\) squares the condition number (19,157 becomes 367 million); in Läuchli's example the small singular value \(10^{-8}\) vanishes entirely, because \(1 + 10^{-16}\) rounds to 1.
  • Scale: truncated or randomized SVD computes only the top \(k\), directly on sparse matrices, with no dense covariance and no centring (LSA).

24What is the best rank-k approximation of a matrix, and where is low rank used?

The truncated SVD: keep the top \(k\) singular values and vectors. By Eckart–Young its squared Frobenius error is the sum of the dropped \(\sigma^2\), and no rank-\(k\) matrix does better.

  • Ratings example (5 users × 4 films): \(\sigma\) = 13.38, 7.12, 1.41, 1.14; rank 2 leaves an error of \(2.0 + 1.31 = 3.31\), 12% relative.
  • Compression: \(k(m + n + 1)\) numbers instead of \(mn\): 10% for a 1,000 × 1,000 image at rank 50.
  • Denoising: keep the singular values above the noise floor (rank 3 of 50: error 0.28 against 1.00 unfiltered).
  • LSA (truncated SVD of TF-IDF); recommenders (user × item factors, fitted on observed ratings only, by ALS or SGD, since blanks aren't zeros); LoRA (a rank-8 update of a 4,096 × 4,096 layer trains 0.39% of the numbers).

(09)

25PCA, t-SNE or UMAP for a 2-D picture of embeddings? What can and can't you read from a t-SNE plot?

PCA for a faithful linear view and global layout; t-SNE or UMAP to see local neighbourhood structure. From t-SNE you can read neighbourhoods, not sizes or distances.

  • t-SNE: Gaussian neighbour probabilities in the data (per-point width from perplexity), Student-t on the map, minimize KL; keeps local structure, not global.
  • UMAP: a k-nearest-neighbour graph and a cross-entropy layout; faster, scales further, a bit more global structure, can transform new points.
  • Can't read: cluster sizes (a cluster with 10 times the spread was drawn the same size), gaps between clusters (a 10:1 ratio of gaps came out about 1:1), density.
  • Practice: several perplexities and seeds; colour by known labels; never cluster or model on the map's coordinates.

26What is Mahalanobis distance, and when is it better than Euclidean?

\(d_M^2 = (x - \mu)^\top C^{-1}(x - \mu)\): distance in standard deviations along each principal direction, the Euclidean distance after whitening. Better whenever features are correlated or on different scales.

  • Example: users at (12, 7) and (12, 3) are both 2.83 from the mean (10, 5). The first moves along the spend-and-bills direction (variance 6): \(d_M = 1.15\). The second moves against it (variance 0.67): \(d_M = 3.46\), unusual.
  • Threshold: for normal data \(d_M^2 \sim \chi^2_d\); with \(d = 2\), the 1% cut-off is 9.21.
  • Caveats: outliers distort the estimated mean and covariance (use a robust estimate such as minimum covariance determinant); assumes one elliptical cloud; needs \(n \gg d\) for a stable \(C^{-1}\).

27How would you detect anomalies with no labels, and evaluate the detector when you get a few?

Score unusualness, rank, and review the top within capacity; evaluate as a ranking with precision at the review budget, a stratified labelled sample and average precision.

  • Scores: robust z per feature; Mahalanobis or PCA reconstruction error for broken correlations; isolation forest as the scalable default; LOF for local density (a point just outside a tight cluster: LOF rank 1, but 52nd by plain neighbour distance); GMM density; autoencoder error for complex data.
  • Prepare: scale features, log heavy tails, choose features that express the behaviour you care about.
  • Evaluate: precision in the top \(k\) analysts can review; label the top-scored cases plus a random sample of the rest and weight back for recall; average precision, not ROC-AUC, for rare positives; injected known patterns for recall checks.
  • Selection bias: reviewers only see flagged cases, so keep a random audit sample; confirmed cases later train a supervised model.

(11)

28“Segment our users.” Walk through how you'd do it end to end.

Agree what the segments are for, build behavioural features, transform and scale them, cluster with a method that fits the shapes, choose \(k\) with metrics plus stability and actionability, then profile and validate.

  • Purpose: who acts on the segments, and how (campaigns, product tiers, risk policy)? That decides the features and the acceptable number of segments.
  • Features: per user over a window: recency, frequency, monetary value, product or category mix, channel; avoid near-duplicates or reduce them with PCA.
  • Transform: log heavy-tailed amounts and counts, clip extremes, standardize. Categorical columns: Gower distance with k-medoids or hierarchical clustering, or k-prototypes; one-hot into k-means only for a few low-cardinality columns, knowing the centres become shares.
  • Cluster: k-means++ with several seeds as the baseline; a GMM for overlapping or elongated segments; k-prototypes for mixed types; DBSCAN or HDBSCAN when outliers or odd shapes matter.
  • Choose and check: elbow, silhouette or gap to shortlist \(k\); stability via ARI across bootstrap refits; segments the team can name and act on.
  • Profile: average the original, untransformed features per segment and name them; explain segments by training a shallow decision tree to predict the cluster label from the original features; track segment sizes and drift over time; assign new users to the nearest centre. When refitting, match new clusters to old ones (for example Hungarian matching on centre distances), since cluster IDs permute between runs.
  • Don't oversell: segments describe users; whether an offer changes a segment's behaviour needs an experiment.

15Cheat sheet

k-means

\[ \begin{aligned} J &= \sum_i \lVert x_i - \mu_{c(i)} \rVert^2 \\ \sum_{i \in S} \lVert x_i - z \rVert^2 &= \sum_{i \in S} \lVert x_i - \bar x_S \rVert^2 + \lvert S \rvert \lVert \bar x_S - z \rVert^2 \\ P(\text{next seed} = x) &\propto D(x)^2 \\ \text{Gap}(k) &= \overline{\log J_k^{\text{ref}}} - \log J_k \end{aligned} \]

Assign to nearest, move to mean; both lower \(J\); local minimum only. Six points: 80 → 21.25 → 7.94 → 2.67. k-means++: expected \(J \le 8(\ln k + 2)\,J^*\). Choose \(k\): elbow, silhouette, gap, stability, actionability. Assumes round, similar-size clusters under Euclidean distance: scale first. Cost \(O(nkdi)\). Mini-batch for scale; k-medoids for any distance or outliers; k-prototypes for mixed types.

Gaussian mixtures and EM

\[ \begin{aligned} p(x) &= \sum_j \pi_j \mathcal N(x \mid \mu_j, \Sigma_j) \\ r_{ij} &= \frac{\pi_j \mathcal N(x_i \mid \mu_j, \Sigma_j)}{\sum_l \pi_l \mathcal N(x_i \mid \mu_l, \Sigma_l)} \\ N_j &= \textstyle\sum_i r_{ij} \\ \pi_j &= N_j/n \\ \mu_j &= \tfrac{1}{N_j}\textstyle\sum_i r_{ij} x_i \\ \Sigma_j &= \tfrac{1}{N_j}\sum_i r_{ij}(x_i - \mu_j)(x_i - \mu_j)^\top \\ \text{BIC} &= p \ln n - 2 \ln \hat L \end{aligned} \]

E: responsibilities by Bayes. M: weighted MLE. Likelihood never falls (Jensen lower bound, tight at \(q = r\)); local optimum. k-means = equal, spherical, \(\sigma \to 0\). Covariance numbers per component: full \(d(d+1)/2\), diag \(d\), spherical 1. Watch for collapse onto single points.

Hierarchical and DBSCAN

\[ \Delta_{\text{Ward}}(a, b) = \frac{n_a n_b}{n_a + n_b}\lVert \mu_a - \mu_b \rVert^2 \]

Single = nearest pair (chains, any shape, chaining); complete = farthest pair (compact); average = mean pair; Ward = least variance increase (k-means-like). \(O(n^2)\) memory. DBSCAN: core ≥ minPts within eps; border near a core; else noise. eps from the k-distance knee. Fails on varying density (use HDBSCAN) and high dimensions.

Cluster metrics

\[ \begin{aligned} s(i) &= \frac{b(i) - a(i)}{\max(a(i), b(i))} \\ \text{DB} &= \frac{1}{k}\sum_j \max_{l \ne j} \frac{S_j + S_l}{M_{jl}} \\ \text{CH} &= \frac{B/(k-1)}{W/(n-k)} \\ \text{ARI} &= \frac{\text{index} - \text{expected}}{\text{max} - \text{expected}} \end{aligned} \]

Silhouette of A: 0.82. Internal metrics favour round blobs (moons: k-means's wrong split wins all three). Rand index is high by chance; ARI ≈ 0 for random; NMI rises with \(k\) (use AMI).

PCA

\[ \begin{aligned} C &= \tfrac{1}{n-1} X_c^\top X_c \\ C v_j &= \lambda_j v_j \\ \text{explained}_j &= \lambda_j / \textstyle\sum_l \lambda_l \\ Z &= X_c V_k \\ \hat X &= \bar x + Z V_k^\top \\ \textstyle\sum_i \lVert x_i - \hat x_i \rVert^2 &= (n - 1)\textstyle\sum_{j \gt k} \lambda_j \end{aligned} \]

Max variance = min reconstruction error (Pythagoras). Four users: \(\lambda = 6, 0.67\); PC1 (0.707, 0.707) explains 90%. 2×2: \(\lambda^2 - \text{tr}\,\lambda + \det = 0\). Standardize unless units are shared. Whitening: \(z_j/\sqrt{\lambda_j}\). Linear, unsupervised (label may be in a low-variance direction), outlier-sensitive.

SVD and low rank

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= U \Sigma V^\top \\ A v_j &= \sigma_j u_j \\ \lambda_j &= \sigma_j^2/(n - 1) \text{ (centred } X_c) \\ \lVert A - A_k \rVert_F^2 &= \textstyle\sum_{j \gt k} \sigma_j^2 \\ \lVert A - A_k \rVert_2 &= \sigma_{k+1} \end{aligned} \]

Rotate, stretch, rotate. PCA = SVD of centred \(X\); scores \(U\Sigma\). Forming \(X^\top X\) squares the condition number. Rank \(k\) stores \(k(m + n + 1)\) numbers. Uses: compression, denoising (cut at the gap), LSA, recommender factors fitted on observed entries, LoRA.

t-SNE, UMAP, anomalies

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{KL}(P \Vert Q) &= \textstyle\sum p_{ij}\log(p_{ij}/q_{ij}) \\ d_M^2 &= (x - \mu)^\top C^{-1}(x - \mu) \sim \chi^2_d \end{aligned} \]

t-SNE and UMAP keep neighbourhoods, not sizes or gaps: pictures only. Anomalies: robust z, Mahalanobis or PCA error, isolation forest, LOF (local density ratio), OC-SVM, autoencoder error. Evaluate as ranking: precision at the review budget, stratified labels, average precision, a random audit sample.
