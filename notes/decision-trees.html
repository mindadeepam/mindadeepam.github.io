Decision trees

What happens at every node of a decision tree: how the split is searched and scored, what a leaf predicts and how far to trust it, how growth is stopped and pruned, where missing values and categories go, and why trees behave as they do.

About 45 minutes, plus the questions · one small credit dataset, worked node by node

In short

  1. A tree asks one-feature yes/no questions and predicts a constant in each leaf: the class shares for classification, the mean for regression. The model is piecewise constant on axis-parallel boxes.
  2. At every node it tries every feature and every threshold. Sort the feature, put one candidate in each gap between distinct values, and sweep once with running counts, scoring each candidate by the size-weighted drop in impurity. That's \(O(d\,n\log n)\) per node, dominated by the sort.
  3. Gini \(1 - \sum_k p_k^2\) and entropy \(-\sum_k p_k \log p_k\) are nearly the same curve, so they almost always pick the same split. Regression uses variance. Misclassification error is a bad criterion: it's straight-sided, so a split that sharpens the class shares without flipping a majority scores zero.
  4. Growth is greedy: the best split now, never revisited. It can't see a pattern like XOR, where no single split helps, and finding the optimal tree is NP-complete.
  5. Left alone, a tree grows until its leaves are pure and memorizes the noise. Stop it early (min_samples_leaf, max_depth) or grow it fully and prune with \(R(T) + \alpha\lvert T\rvert\), choosing \(\alpha\) by cross-validation.
  6. A leaf's probability is its training share \(k/n\): noisy in small leaves, pushed towards 0 and 1 because the tree picked those leaves for looking extreme, and exactly 0 or 1 when pure. Use bigger leaves, smooth, or calibrate.
  7. Trees need no scaling, find interactions, shrug off outliers in the features and learn where to send missing values. They can't extrapolate, they draw diagonals as staircases, they're high-variance, and they favour features with many distinct values, in splits and in impurity importance.

01A tree, and the running example

Every number on this page comes from one made-up dataset of 5,000 loan applicants, so the true rule is known and every result can be checked against it.

  • income: monthly income, ₹15k to ₹150k.
  • emi: the share of that income already going to loan EMIs, 0 to 0.8.
  • late: late payments in the last year (0, 1, 2, …).
  • age: 21 to 60. It has no effect on default: a decoy.

The truth is a set of flat steps. Everyone starts at a 3% chance of default. Being stressed (EMIs over half of income and income under ₹40k) adds 45 points, and 2 or more late payments add 25. So the true chance takes only four values:

8.7% of applicants default. Models train on 3,500 rows and are scored on the other 1,500 by two numbers.

AUC is the chance that a random defaulter scores above a random payer (0.5 is a coin flip, 1 is perfect): it judges the ranking. Log loss averages \(-\log\) of the probability given to what actually happened (lower is better; confident mistakes cost a lot): it judges the probabilities.

Predicting the true chances gets an AUC of 0.817 and a log loss of 0.212, the best any model can do on average, since defaults are random (on one test set a model can edge past it by luck; see section 17). A second target, the credit limit, is \(2 \times \text{income} \times (1 - \text{emi})\) plus noise with spread 10; it's used for regression.

Questions, nodes and leaves

A decision tree predicts by asking one yes/no question at a time, each about a single feature: “is late at most 1?” That question is a split, and the number it compares against is the threshold. Rows that answer yes go left, the rest go right, and each group gets its own next question.

  • Each group along the way is a node; the first, holding everyone, is the root.
  • A node that asks no further question is a leaf. It holds the prediction.
  • The number of questions on the longest root-to-leaf path is the depth.

The whole model is 8 numbers. A new applicant follows the questions to one leaf and gets that leaf's training share of defaults as their probability. As a formula, with leaves \(R_1, \dots, R_J\) (the boxes) and leaf values \(c_j\):

\[ f(x) = \sum_{j=1}^{J} c_j\, \mathbf 1\{x \in R_j\} \]

Exactly one indicator is 1 for any \(x\), so the prediction is flat inside each box and jumps between boxes: the model is piecewise constant. Each question cuts one axis at one value, so every box has sides parallel to the axes. The tree is non-parametric: the number of splits grows with the data, with no fixed set of weights. On the test set it scores an AUC of 0.808 and a log loss of 0.216, close to the best possible (0.817, 0.212).

  • Prediction is cheap: one root-to-leaf path, at most depth comparisons, \(O(\text{depth})\). A depth-3 tree is three nested if statements, easy to port to SQL or a rules engine.
  • CART vs ID3/C4.5: CART (Breiman and others, 1984) is the algorithm sklearn follows: binary splits, Gini or entropy for classification, squared error for regression, cost-complexity pruning. ID3 and C4.5 (Quinlan) use entropy-based gain (C4.5 the gain ratio, section 03) and split a category into one branch per value.
  • Why binary splits: a multiway split cuts the rows into many small pieces at once and inflates the gain of many-valued features. Any multiway split can be written as a sequence of binary ones, so binary trees lose nothing in what they can express, and the data decides how finely to cut.

02Impurity: how mixed a node is

A good question separates defaulters from payers. To score questions, first measure how mixed one group is: the node's impurity. It's 0 when the node is pure (everyone had the same outcome) and largest at an even mix. Write \(p_k\) for the share of class \(k\) in the node.

MeasureFormulaIn plain words
Gini\(1 - \sum_k p_k^2\)The chance that two rows drawn at random from the node (with replacement) have different classes
Entropy\(-\sum_k p_k \log_2 p_k\)How many yes/no questions per row it takes to learn the classes, averaged over many rows (asking cleverly about batches)
Misclassification error\(1 - \max_k p_k\)The share you'd get wrong by predicting the node's majority for everyone

With two classes and \(p\) the share of defaults, Gini is \(1 - p^2 - (1 - p)^2 = 2p(1 - p)\). For a node with 1 default among 4 rows (\(p = 0.25\)):

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Gini} &= 1 - 0.25^2 - 0.75^2 = 0.375 \\ \text{entropy} &= -0.25\log_2 0.25 - 0.75\log_2 0.75 \\ &= 0.5 + 0.311 = 0.811 \\ \text{misclass.} &= 1 - 0.75 = 0.25 \end{aligned} \]
  • At a 50/50 node the three are 0.5, 1 and 0.5, their maxima. With \(K\) equally common classes Gini peaks at \(1 - 1/K\) and entropy at \(\log_2 K\).
  • The whole training set (8.7% defaults) has Gini 0.159 and entropy 0.426 bits: fairly pure, because the answer is usually “repaid”.
  • Gini as two random picks: both picks are class \(k\) with probability \(p_k^2\), so they match with probability \(\sum_k p_k^2\) and differ with probability \(1 - \sum_k p_k^2\).
  • A common slip: “the tree maximizes entropy.” It looks for children with low entropy, so it maximizes the drop in entropy.

Plotted against \(p\), all three are hills: 0 at both ends, highest at 0.5. Gini and entropy are smooth curves of almost the same shape; misclassification error is a tent with straight sides. That shape difference decides which of them can find good splits (the figure in section 03).

03Scoring a split

A split is good if its two children are purer than the parent. Score it by the drop in impurity, with the children's impurities averaged by size: a child holding 90% of the rows counts for 90%. The drop is the split's gain (sklearn's “impurity decrease”); with entropy it's called information gain, which is also the mutual information between the split's answer and the label.

\[ \text{gain} = I(P) - \frac{n_L}{n}\, I(L) - \frac{n_R}{n}\, I(R) \]

\(I\) is the impurity of the parent \(P\) and the left and right children \(L, R\); \(n\) is the parent's rows, and \(n_L, n_R\) the rows sent left and right.

What the code actually computes

Write \(n_{Lk}\) for the rows of class \(k\) on the left (and \(n_{Rk}\) on the right). Since \(n_L\, \text{Gini}_L = n_L - \sum_k n_{Lk}^2 / n_L\), the children's weighted Gini is

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{children} &= \frac{n_L}{n}\text{Gini}_L + \frac{n_R}{n}\text{Gini}_R \\ &= 1 - \frac{1}{n}\Big( \sum_k \frac{n_{Lk}^2}{n_L} + \sum_k \frac{n_{Rk}^2}{n_R} \Big) \end{aligned} \]

So the best split simply maximizes \(\sum_k n_{Lk}^2/n_L + \sum_k n_{Rk}^2/n_R\), which needs only the class counts on each side. For the toy split (5 payers and 1 default left, 1 and 3 right):

\[ \begin{aligned} \tfrac{5^2 + 1^2}{6} + \tfrac{1^2 + 3^2}{4} &= 6.833 \\ 1 - 6.833/10 &= 0.317 \end{aligned} \]

the same 0.317 as above. Counts are what the sweep in section 04 updates in \(O(1)\) per row.

Why weight by size

Take a split that isolates a single payer (on an ID column, say). That one-row child is pure and the other nine barely change. Weighted, the gain is 0.036, almost useless. Unweighted (a plain average of the two children), the gain is 0.233, which would beat the real split scored the same way (0.154). Without the weights, peeling off one pure row would win every time.

Why not misclassification error

The geometry. The parent's share is the weighted average of the children's, \(p = \frac{n_L}{n}p_L + \frac{n_R}{n}p_R\), and the children's weighted impurity is the same weighted average of two points on the impurity curve: a point on the chord between them, directly below the parent's point on the curve (or on it, for the tent).

  • Gini and entropy are strictly concave (they bend down everywhere), so the chord lies strictly below the curve: any split that changes the shares at all has a positive gain. That's also why a gain can never be negative (Jensen's inequality: for a curve that bends down, the weighted average of two heights is at most the height at their weighted average); a printout like −2.8e−17 is rounding.
  • Misclassification error is straight on each side of 0.5, so it's concave but not strictly. When both children fall on the same side, the chord lies on the line and the gain is exactly zero, however much the shares moved.
  • Misclassification error is still fine for pruning and for reporting; it's only blind as a split criterion.

Gini vs entropy: why they agree, and where they don't

Both are the expected “surprise” at a row's class, with a different surprise function:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Gini} &= \sum_k p_k\,(1 - p_k) \\ \text{entropy (nats)} &= \sum_k p_k\,(-\ln p_k) \end{aligned} \]

And \(1 - p\) is the tangent line of \(-\ln p\) at \(p = 1\): Gini is entropy with the log replaced by its first-order approximation. The two curves have nearly the same shape, so they rank candidate splits the same way almost always. On this data, depth-3 trees grown with each share six of their seven splits; the one difference is a noise split (age ≤ 58.20 for entropy, income ≤ 146.44 for Gini).

  • Where they differ: nodes where one class is rare. \(-\ln p\) blows up as \(p \to 0\), so entropy is steeper near the ends. A node with 2% defaults sits at 14% of entropy's peak (0.141 of 1) but only 8% of Gini's (0.039 of 0.5), so entropy gives more credit for cleaning up a nearly pure node, such as a small fraud pocket.
  • Why Gini is the default: it was CART's original choice and skips the logarithm (a small speed gain). In practice depth and leaf size matter far more than this choice.
  • sklearn's criterion="log_loss" is entropy: a leaf's entropy is its log loss when it predicts its own share.
  • A credit team asking “what's the model's Gini?” means the Gini coefficient, \(2\,\text{AUC} - 1\) (0.617 for this tree), a ranking score unrelated to Gini impurity.

Gain ratio (C4.5)

A split of a category into one branch per value (one per customer ID, say) makes every branch pure, so plain information gain loves it. C4.5 divides the gain by the entropy of the branch sizes, the split information, which grows with the number of branches:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{SplitInfo} &= -\sum_{j} \frac{n_j}{n} \log_2 \frac{n_j}{n} \\ \text{GainRatio} &= \frac{\text{information gain}}{\text{SplitInfo}} \end{aligned} \]
  • On the ten applicants: late ≤ 1 splits 6/4, so SplitInfo is 0.971 and the gain ratio is \(0.256 / 0.971 = 0.264\). A ten-way ID split gains the parent's full 0.971 bits, with SplitInfo \(\log_2 10 = 3.32\): ratio 0.292. On ten rows the ID split still wins.
  • On the 3,500 training rows, a 3,500-way ID split has SplitInfo \(\log_2 3500 = 11.77\):
    \[ \begin{aligned} \text{late} \le 1 &:\ 0.038/0.508 = 0.075 \\ \text{ID split} &:\ 0.426/11.77 = 0.036 \end{aligned} \]
    With enough rows the penalty works. C4.5 also only considers attributes whose gain is at least the average, which blocks splits that score well only because SplitInfo is tiny.
  • CART sidesteps the problem with binary splits only, which is why sklearn has no gain ratio.

04Searching for the split at a node

This is what happens at every node, from the root to the last split. The node holds \(n\) training rows and there are \(d\) features.

  1. Summarize the node: its class counts (or, for regression, the sum of its targets), its value (what it would predict as a leaf) and its impurity. If a stop rule fires (depth, too few rows, already pure), it becomes a leaf.
  2. For each feature (all of them, or a random subset if max_features is set), in a random order:
    1. sort the node's rows by that feature;
    2. put one candidate threshold in each gap between consecutive distinct values, at the midpoint: at most \(n - 1\) candidates, fewer when values repeat;
    3. sweep left to right, moving one row at a time from the right side to the left and updating the class counts on each side in \(O(1)\);
    4. at each real gap where both sides keep at least min_samples_leaf rows (the minimum leaf size, section 10), score the split from the counts (section 03) and remember the best.
  3. Keep the best feature and threshold over all features. If no valid split exists, or its weighted gain is below min_impurity_decrease, the node becomes a leaf.
  4. Partition the rows into the two children and repeat the whole procedure inside each (section 07).

Within one node the search is exhaustive: it finds the single best axis-parallel split there. What's greedy is that each node's split is chosen for its own gain alone, without asking what splits it would make possible below, and is never revisited.

  • Why only between distinct values: a threshold can't separate two rows with the same value, so a “gap” inside a run of equal values isn't a candidate.
  • Why the midpoint: any threshold inside a gap sends the same training rows left, so the training gain can't choose among them. The midpoint hedges for unseen values that fall in the gap. (Histogram methods use bin edges instead.)

In NumPy, cumsum does the whole sweep at once:

def best_split(x, y):
    """Best Gini threshold for one feature, 0/1 labels."""
    order = np.argsort(x)
    xs, ys = x[order], y[order]
    n = len(ys)
    n_l = np.arange(1, n)       # rows left of each gap
    k_l = np.cumsum(ys)[:-1]    # defaults left of it
    p_l = k_l / n_l
    p_r = (ys.sum() - k_l) / (n - n_l)
    kids = (n_l * 2 * p_l * (1 - p_l)
            + (n - n_l) * 2 * p_r * (1 - p_r)) / n
    kids[xs[1:] == xs[:-1]] = np.inf  # not a gap
    i = np.argmin(kids)
    p = ys.mean()
    t = (xs[i] + xs[i + 1]) / 2       # the midpoint
    return t, 2 * p * (1 - p) - kids[i]

What it costs

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{per feature} &= O(n_t \log n_t) + O(n_t) \\ \text{per node} &= O(d\, n_t \log n_t) \\ \text{per level} &= O(d\, n \log n) \\ \text{per tree} &= O(D\, d\, n \log n) \end{aligned} \]
  • Per feature, the sort costs \(O(n_t \log n_t)\) and the sweep \(O(n_t)\). \(n_t\) is the node's rows, \(D\) the depth. Each training row sits in exactly one node per level, so a level's nodes add up to \(n\) rows.
  • A balanced tree has \(D \approx \log_2 n\), so about \(O(d\, n \log^2 n)\). A lopsided tree is deeper: the unconstrained tree on this data reaches depth 25.
  • With \(K\) classes, each row move updates \(K\) counts and each candidate is scored in \(O(K)\).
  • Rescoring every threshold from scratch instead of sweeping costs \(O(n_t)\) per candidate, so \(O(n_t^2)\) per feature: 3,500 passes over the rows at the root, for the same answer.
  • Prediction costs \(O(D)\) per row.

Histograms: how large-data libraries skip the sort. LightGBM, XGBoost's hist method and sklearn's HistGradientBoosting cut each feature into at most about 256 quantile bins once, before training. At each node they add up the counts (or gradients) per bin in \(O(n_t\, d)\) and try only the bin edges in \(O(256\, d)\). A sibling's histogram is the parent's minus the other child's, so only the smaller child needs a pass over its rows. The price is resolution: no threshold between two bin edges.

Ties and randomness

sklearn tries the features in a random order at every node, even when it uses all of them. When two features give exactly the same gain, the first one tried wins, so random_state can change a tree. This matters most for duplicated or perfectly correlated features (section 15). On this data, seeds 0 to 4 all give the same depth-3 tree.

Why noise splits win: more thresholds, more lottery tickets

A feature with 3,500 distinct values offers 3,499 candidate cuts; a yes/no feature offers one. Even when both are pure noise, the many-valued one has far more chances at a lucky gain. In 200 simulations with 1,000 rows, a pure-noise target with 10% positives, one continuous noise feature and one binary noise feature, the continuous one won the root 96% of the time.

That's how age ≤ 23.18 got into the tree:

  • The truth in that branch is flat. All 331 applicants there (2+ late payments, income over ₹40.5k) have a true chance of 28%, so every candidate cut is noise: about 330 each for income, EMI share and age, and 3 for late, some 990 in all.
  • A depth limit never refuses a split. With only max_depth=3, every impure node above depth 3 must split, even when all its candidates are noise.
  • So the luckiest cut wins. The best gains there were age 0.0095, EMI 0.0044, income 0.0027 and late 0.0009. The income ≤ 146.44 node is the same story: its truth is a flat 3% and its best gain is 0.0001.

Defences: leaf-size limits and pruning, which refuse or remove splits that gain too little (sections 10 and 11). Conditional-inference trees and CHAID pick the feature by a significance test first, which removes the bias towards many-valued features. The same bias inflates impurity-based importance (section 14).

05Categorical features

A numeric feature has an order, so a split is a cut in that order. A categorical feature (state, employer type, loan purpose) has none: a split sends some subset of the categories left. With \(K\) categories there are \(2^{K-1} - 1\) ways to split them into two non-empty groups: 127 for 8 states, about \(5.6 \times 10^{14}\) for 50. Three ways to search them:

  • Exhaustive: try every subset. Only feasible for small \(K\).
  • One-vs-rest: try the \(K\) splits that peel off one category. This is all a tree can do with one-hot columns.
  • Sort by the mean target: order the categories by their share of class 1 (or their mean target, for regression) and try only the \(K - 1\) cuts in that order, as if it were a numeric feature.

Sorting is exactly optimal for a binary or regression target. For a 0/1 target with Gini or entropy (Breiman and others, 1984), and for squared error (Fisher, 1958), the best of all \(2^{K-1} - 1\) groupings is always one of the \(K - 1\) cuts in sorted order.

The intuition is an exchange argument: if a riskier category sat on the safe side while a safer one of the same size sat on the risky side, swapping them pushes the two sides' shares further apart while the parent's share stays put, and under a concave impurity that can only raise the gain. So the optimal groups are contiguous in the sorted order.

One-vs-rest's best, {B} alone, gains half as much: it needs two levels of splits to build {A, C} vs {D, B}, and each level costs rows. {A, B} | {C, D}, which mixes a safe and a risky category on each side, gains almost nothing (0.00005): both sides land near the overall 17%.

  • More than two classes: there's no single order to sort by, and no such shortcut. Libraries fall back on heuristics: one-vs-rest, or sorting by a gradient statistic, as LightGBM does.
  • The catch: the sort order is learned from the labels. With many rare categories it's mostly noise, and the split overfits. LightGBM guards it with a minimum count per category (min_data_per_group) and smoothing (cat_smooth).

In sklearn's decision tree: encode first

sklearn's DecisionTreeClassifier splits only on numbers (its HistGradientBoosting models do have native categorical splits). A made-up example: 8 states whose default rates alternate low and high in code order (3%, 25%, 4%, 22%, 2%, 28%, 5%, 20%), trained on 2,000 rows and tested on 2,000:

Test AUC; the best possible is 0.718 on average (0.720 edges past it by test-set luck). Arbitrary codes invent an order that a cut can use only once. One-hot needs four levels to peel the states apart. Replacing each state by its default rate turns the sort-by-mean trick into a numeric feature, and one split gets there.

That encoding used the labels: target encoding. With 8 states of 250 rows each it's fine. With thousands of rare categories (merchant IDs, pin codes), a rate from 3 rows is mostly noise, and when it's computed on the rows the tree trains on, each row's own label leaks into its feature: brilliant in training, poor on new data. Compute it out-of-fold (each row's rate comes only from the other folds) and shrink rare categories towards the overall rate \(\bar p\):

\[ \text{rate}_c = \frac{k_c + m\,\bar p}{n_c + m} \]

A category with \(n_c\) rows and \(k_c\) positives is pulled towards \(\bar p\) as if it had \(m\) extra rows at the overall rate. sklearn's TargetEncoder does both; CatBoost uses ordered target statistics. A category never seen in training gets the overall rate with a target encoder, all zeros with one-hot (handle_unknown="ignore"), and an arbitrary position with codes: decide it explicitly and test it.

06Regression trees

For a numeric target, like the credit limit, the same machinery works with a different impurity: the node's variance, the mean squared distance from the node's mean. A leaf predicts the mean of its training targets, because the mean is the single number with the smallest squared error: the total squared error \(\sum_i (y_i - c)^2\) has slope \(-2\sum_i (y_i - c)\), which is zero exactly at \(c = \bar y\). A split's gain is the drop in size-weighted variance, the variance reduction:

\[ \text{gain} = \text{Var}(P) - \tfrac{n_L}{n}\,\text{Var}(L) - \tfrac{n_R}{n}\,\text{Var}(R) \]

The regression sweep: running sums

A node's total squared error needs only the sum of the targets, \(S\), and the sum of their squares:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{SSE} &= \sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2 = \sum_i y_i^2 - \frac{S^2}{n} \\ \text{SSE}_L + \text{SSE}_R &= \sum_i y_i^2 - \Big( \tfrac{S_L^2}{n_L} + \tfrac{S_R^2}{n_R} \Big) \end{aligned} \]

(Expand the square: \(\sum y_i^2 - 2\bar y \sum y_i + n\bar y^2\), and with \(\bar y = S/n\) the last two terms combine to \(-S^2/n\).) The \(\sum y_i^2\) is the same for every split, so the sweep just maximizes \(S_L^2/n_L + S_R^2/n_R\), updating \(S_L\) by one addition per row. On the toy:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{cut 25:}\ & 30^2/1 + 330^2/3 = 37{,}200 \\ \text{cut 45:}\ & 80^2/2 + 280^2/2 = 42{,}400 \ (\text{best}) \\ \text{cut 75:}\ & 190^2/3 + 170^2/1 = 40{,}933 \end{aligned} \]

With \(\sum y_i^2 = 44{,}400\), cut 45's children have SSE \(44{,}400 - 42{,}400 = 2{,}000\), which is \(n = 4\) times the weighted variance of 500 in the table.

07Growing the tree, greedily

A whole tree is section 04 on repeat. Find the best split of all the rows; that makes two groups. Treat each group as a new, smaller dataset and do the same, until a stop rule fires or no split is possible. This is recursive partitioning (the full pseudocode is in section 16):

grow(rows):
    if a stop rule fires or no split is possible:
        return leaf(value of rows)     # shares or mean
    feature, threshold = best split of rows
    left  = grow(rows with feature <= threshold)
    right = grow(the other rows)
    return node(feature, threshold, left, right)

Greedy, and where that fails

Each split is the best one right now, chosen without looking ahead and never revisited. Searching all possible trees is hopeless: building an optimal tree is NP-complete (Hyafil and Rivest, 1976, for the tree that needs the fewest questions on average; finding the smallest tree is NP-hard too), so every standard library grows trees greedily.

Where it fails: XOR. Two features on \([0, 1]\); the label is 1 when exactly one of them is above 0.5. Each feature alone says nothing: split on either at 0.5 and both halves are still 50/50. A depth-2 tree would be perfect (split one feature at 0.5, then the other at 0.5 on each side), but greedy growth can't see that the first split pays off only after the second. On 1,000 rows:

  • the correct first split, \(x_1 \le 0.5\), has a Gini gain of 0.000003;
  • with nothing useful available, the root grabs noise: it peels off the 28 rows whose second feature is below 0.039, where a chance imbalance gives a sliver of gain;
  • training accuracy by depth 1 to 5 is 52%, 53%, 56%, 89% and 100%: three levels are wasted before the real cuts appear;
  • a stop rule of “split only if the gain is at least 0.005” gives up at the root: a single leaf.

Why greedy still found the credit rule. An AND leaves a trace in each feature alone; an XOR leaves none. At the root, income's best gain was 0.009 and EMI's 0.0025: each half of “income under 40 AND EMIs over half” already separates defaulters a little by itself, so greedy picked up income one level down and EMI beneath it.

  • Lookahead would solve the XOR, by scoring pairs of splits together. But every candidate must then be tried with every candidate in both children, roughly \((d\,n)^2\) work per node instead of \(d\,n\), so it's rarely done.
  • Growing deep and pruning back fixes the stop-rule failure: pruning judges whole branches, so it keeps a weak split with strong children (section 11). It won't replace a wasted noise split at the top with the right one, so the tree stays bigger than it needs to be.
  • Averaging many trees is cheaper still. A 200-tree random forest capped at depth 3 scores 96.9% on fresh XOR data, where one depth-3 tree scores 53%: random feature choices and resampling let some trees start with a useful cut.

An applicant with income ₹30k, EMIs at 60% of income, no late payments and age 35 lands in the 213-row leaf. 105 of those 213 training applicants defaulted, so the tree says 49.3%, exactly their share.

Declining the two riskiest leaves turns away 1.2% of applicants; the next leaf jumps that to 6.4%. “Decline exactly the riskiest 5%” is impossible, and score deciles can't be formed.
  • Inside a leaf, everyone is the same. An applicant earning ₹45k and one earning ₹140k, both with no late payments, both get 2.5%.
  • Far outside the data, say ₹5 lakh a month with no late payments, an applicant follows the richest applicants seen into the income > 146.44 leaf: 6.7%. A tree can't say anything new beyond its data (section 13).

09Are the probabilities calibrated?

A probability is calibrated if, among all applicants given 20%, about 20% default. A leaf's probability is its training share, \(\hat p = k/n\), and three things make it rough:

  • Small leaves are noisy. A share from \(n\) rows typically misses the truth by its standard error, \(\sqrt{\hat p(1 - \hat p)/n}\). The 20-row leaf says 90%; one standard error is \(\sqrt{0.9 \times 0.1/20} = 0.067\), so two (roughly a 95% range) give 77% to 100%.
  • Small leaves are pushed towards the extremes. That leaf's truth, 73%, sits outside even that range. It isn't bad luck: the tree chose this leaf because its share looked extreme, out of many candidate cuts, so selected leaves are biased towards 0 and 1, not just noisy.
  • Pure leaves say exactly 0% or 100%, the far end of the same effect, and log loss punishes a confident wrong answer without limit.

Fixing the probabilities

  • Smooth towards the base rate (an m-estimate): pretend each leaf also holds \(m\) extra rows that default at the overall rate \(\bar p\).
    \[ \hat p = \frac{k + m\,\bar p}{n + m} \]
    With \(m = 10\), a pure leaf of 30 says 2.2% instead of 0%, and the min_samples_leaf=30 tree's log loss falls from 0.731 to 0.241 with the same leaves. Choose \(m\) by cross-validation; too large pulls every leaf to 8.7% and loses the spread.
  • Honest leaves: the shares lean to the extremes because the same rows chose the leaves and then filled them. Grow the tree on one half of the training rows and fill the leaf shares from the other half. For a min_samples_leaf=30 tree grown on half the training rows, filling the leaves from the other half and smoothing gives a log loss of 0.227, against 0.725 when it uses its own shares.
  • Calibrate on held-out data with Platt scaling (a logistic fit on the score) or isotonic regression (a monotone step fit). Isotonic can merge neighbouring leaves into one score but can never split one, so the tree still has at most as many scores as leaves.
  • Average many trees, as a random forest does: leaves that disagree average into smoother, less extreme probabilities.

Class weights inflate the probabilities

class_weight="balanced" makes each class count equally in total: the training set has 3,196 payers and 304 defaulters, so each defaulter counts \(r = 3196/304 \approx 10.5\) times as much as a payer. That changes which splits win, and it multiplies every leaf's odds of default by \(r\): a leaf with \(k\) defaulters among \(n\) rows reports \(p_w = rk/(rk + n - k)\). The depth-3 tree's average prediction becomes 32.7% against a real 8.7%, and its log loss 0.466. Solving for \(k/n\) undoes it exactly:

\[ p = \frac{p_w}{p_w + r\,(1 - p_w)} \]

That brings the log loss back to 0.236 (not 0.216, because the splits stay as the weighted tree chose them). The ranking barely moves: multiplying every leaf's odds by the same \(r\) keeps the leaves in order, so only the changed splits move the AUC (0.823 here, against 0.808). Oversampling the rare class \(r\) times does the same and is undone the same way.

Three separate questions: does the model rank well (AUC), do its probabilities mean what they say (calibration, log loss), and where do you cut to approve? A tree can pass the first and fail the second.

11Cost-complexity pruning

Grow a big tree, then cut back the branches that don't earn their keep: post-pruning. A branch is a node plus everything below it; cutting it turns the node back into a leaf. Unlike stopping early, pruning judges a whole branch, not one split, so it can keep a weak split whose children are strong.

CART gives every leaf a price \(\alpha\) and, among all trees obtained by cutting branches off the big one, keeps the one with the lowest

\[ \begin{aligned} R_\alpha(T) &= R(T) + \alpha\,\lvert T\rvert \\ R(T) &= \sum_{\text{leaves } t} \frac{n_t}{N}\, I(t) \end{aligned} \]

\(R(T)\) is the tree's training impurity (each leaf's impurity times its share of the rows) and \(\lvert T\rvert\) its number of leaves. \(\alpha = 0\) keeps the full tree; a large \(\alpha\) leaves only the root. In sklearn it's ccp_alpha.

The weakest link. You don't have to search all subtrees. For each branch \(T_t\) under node \(t\), ask how much training impurity it removes per extra leaf:

\[ g(t) = \frac{R(t) - R(T_t)}{\lvert T_t\rvert - 1} \]

\(R(t)\) is node \(t\)'s impurity if it were a leaf, \(R(T_t)\) the total over the leaves beneath it. Once \(\alpha\) passes \(g(t)\), those leaves aren't worth their price. So cut the branch with the smallest \(g\), recompute \(g\) for the branches above it, and repeat. That gives a short list of nested trees, each the previous one with one more branch cut, each optimal over a range of \(\alpha\). Breiman showed that for every \(\alpha\) there's a unique smallest optimal subtree and that these are nested, so this list contains every tree pruning could ever pick.

12Missing values

At a split on income, a row with a blank income has no side to go to. There are four standard answers: learn a direction, surrogate splits, C4.5's fractional rows, or impute.

Learn a direction for the blanks (sklearn, XGBoost, LightGBM)

sklearn's trees accept NaN (since version 1.3). During the sweep at a node, the rows whose value is missing are set aside, and every candidate threshold is scored twice: once with all the blanks sent left, once with them sent right. The better option wins, and the node stores the direction (tree_.missing_go_to_left). It also scores one extra candidate: all blanks on one side, every present row on the other. So blanks learn their own route at every split. XGBoost's “default direction” and LightGBM work the same way.

If a feature had no blanks at that node in training, a blank at prediction time goes to the child that had more training rows. That's a silent guess, not a learned rule.

Surrogate splits (classic CART)

  1. Choose the primary split, say income ≤ 39.46, using the rows where income is present.
  2. For every other feature, find the split (threshold and direction) that best mimics the primary one: the one that sends the most rows the same way, measured on rows where both features are present.
  3. Keep only surrogates that agree better than the “majority rule” (sending everyone to the primary split's bigger side), ranked by agreement.
  4. For a row missing income, at training or prediction time, use the best surrogate whose feature is present. If all are missing, follow the majority.

Surrogates help only when features are correlated: a loan amount can stand in for income. In this dataset the features were drawn independently, so a surrogate could beat the majority rule only by chance. R's rpart does this; sklearn doesn't.

Fractional rows (C4.5)

C4.5 sends a row with a blank down both branches, weighted by the share of training rows that went each way, and averages the leaves it reaches by those weights.

Impute, or encode the blank

  • Mean or median: simple, but the blank now looks like a typical value, so any signal in being blank is lost.
  • Imputation plus a “was blank” indicator column (SimpleImputer(add_indicator=True)): works with any model, and the tree can split on the indicator.
  • An out-of-range constant such as −999: one split can isolate the blanks, but until it does they travel with the lowest values.
  • Blank as its own category for categorical features.

Fit any imputation on training data only.

When a blank is information

Suppose stressed applicants often skip the income field: half of them leave it blank, against 5% of everyone else. Blank-income applicants then default 23.4% of the time, against 7.4% for the rest. Take an applicant with a blank income, EMIs at 60%, no late payments, age 35:

  • Missing completely at random: the blank tells you nothing.
  • Missing at random (for reasons visible in other columns): other features can stand in.
  • Missing not at random (because of the value itself, as here): the blank carries the most signal, and the model is most fragile when the process creating blanks changes. A pipeline bug that blanks a field would silently move applicants to whichever side blanks learned; a form that makes income mandatory removes the signal overnight. 13How trees behave

Almost everything here follows from one fact: a tree only ever asks “is this one value above or below a cut?”

No scaling: invariant to monotone transforms

Standardizing, taking logs or square roots all keep the values of a feature in the same order, so the same rows fall on each side of every possible cut, and the split search sees exactly the same candidates and gains. The tree comes out the same; only the printed thresholds change units. Refitting with log income gives identical test predictions, and \(e^{\text{threshold}}\) of its income split is 39.455, the same cut.

  • A strictly increasing transform gives the same tree. A decreasing one (multiply by −1) gives the same partitions with left and right swapped.
  • One subtlety: the midpoint of a gap moves under a non-linear transform, so a new value inside that tiny gap could in principle fall the other way.
  • Mixing features is not free. Rotating them (PCA) or replacing income and EMI share by their sum and difference changes the tree, and can turn an axis-parallel rule into a diagonal one.
  • Standardizing anyway is harmless, and convenient when the same pipeline feeds a linear model.

Interactions come free, if each part helps a little

The truth has an AND: high EMIs matter only for low incomes. The tree found it by splitting on income, then on EMI share inside the low-income branch; a split inside a branch applies only there. Logistic regression adds one effect per feature, so it can't express the AND unless handed it: it scores a test AUC of 0.756 alone and 0.822 once given the two true rules as columns, while the tree got 0.808 on its own.

Two limits: a pure XOR defeats greedy growth (section 07), and depth caps the interaction order, since a path of depth \(D\) involves at most \(D\) features.

Outliers: harmless in the features, harmful in the target

An income typed with three extra zeros just moves that applicant further to the right of cuts it was already right of. But a regression tree with squared error chases a typo in the target, because one huge value drags a leaf's mean: one ₹10 crore limit in the training data changes every test prediction of a depth-3 tree, whose first two splits only wall off that row. With absolute error (leaf = median) the typo can't drag a leaf: no split chases it, and at most one leaf's median shifts by one row (here, no prediction changed at all).

Axis-parallel boundaries: diagonals become staircases

Every split cuts one feature at one value, so a tree's boundaries are made of horizontal and vertical pieces. A diagonal boundary has to be built as a staircase, and every step costs leaves. Take a toy with two features where the label is “feature 2 > feature 1”:

High variance: small changes, a different tree

Each split wins by some margin over the runner-up, often a small one, so resampling the rows can change the winner. Every split below depends on the ones above, so one change near the top rewrites the whole branch under it. Refitting the depth-3 tree on 20 bootstrap samples of the training rows (3,500 drawn with replacement):

  • The root is stable: late ≤ 1.5 wins 18 times out of 20 (income ≤ 40.5 the other 2).
  • The small branch is not. Right of the root sit only about 390 applicants, and its question takes 17 different forms in 20 refits: income at 44, at 47.6, at 122.3, age at 25.1, EMI share at 0.1…
  • So are the predictions. One high-risk applicant (true chance 73%) gets anything from 27% to 100%, a standard deviation of 0.23. With full-depth trees it's worse: 100% in 17 refits and 0% in 3, a standard deviation of 0.36.

Averaging many trees fitted on bootstrap samples (bagging, random forests) cancels much of this variance. It's also a warning: before presenting one tree's rules as policy, refit on a few resamples and keep only the rules that survive.

A bias towards many-valued features

A feature with more distinct values offers more candidate thresholds, so it wins more often by luck (section 04): a continuous noise feature beat a binary one 96% of the time. The bias lives in the split search, so it shows up wherever splits are counted, most of all in impurity importance (next section). Leaf-size limits, pruning, and significance-test trees (CHAID, conditional inference) are the defences.

SHAP explains one prediction as a sum: base rate plus one contribution per feature. 15What-ifs

The follow-ups that test whether you know what happens inside the tree.

  • Depth 1, a stump. One split, late ≤ 1.5, and two scores, 5.9% and 30.5%: test AUC 0.653. It can't express the AND (high bias), and it combines no features, so a sum of stumps (boosting with depth 1) is an additive model with no interactions.
  • Unlimited depth. Splits until every leaf is pure: depth 25, 327 leaves, 100% training accuracy, test AUC 0.623, log loss 3.97, every probability 0 or 1 (section 10).
  • min_samples_leaf=1. That's the default, and it means the leaf-size limit is off: a split may peel off a single row. With no depth limit it gives the unlimited tree above. Raising it to 200 gave the best ranking of any single setting here (12 leaves, test AUC 0.824).
  • Duplicating a feature. Predictions don't change, since a split on the copy is the same split. Importance is split between the copies by random_state, which decides which copy is tried first when they tie. Correlated features do the same more softly.
  • Adding a noise feature with many unique values (a random number, an ID). In the depth-3 tree it can still win the nodes where the truth is flat: over 20 random columns it took the income ≤ 146.44 noise split in 10, because the real features' best gain there is only 0.0001; where real signal exists, a real feature wins. In a deep tree it wins splits in small branches, gets an impurity importance of 0.200 (on a par with income) and a permutation importance of about 0, and test performance barely moves. A yes/no noise feature gets 0.014. Many values, many lottery tickets.
  • Scaling a feature (standardizing, rupees to dollars): the same tree, thresholds in new units. Trees need no scaling.
  • A monotone transform (log, square root, rank): the same tree (log income gives identical predictions). A non-monotone one (squaring a centred feature) or mixing features (PCA) changes it.
  • Class imbalance, 1:100 as in fraud. The root's Gini is only \(2 \times 0.01 \times 0.99 \approx 0.02\), so every gain is tiny and a min_impurity_decrease tuned on balanced data blocks every split. A 30-row leaf of ordinary users holds no fraud about three times in four (\(0.99^{30} \approx 0.74\)) and says exactly 0%. predict flags almost nobody. Use leaf sizes big enough to hold some positives, class weights if needed (then correct the probabilities, section 09), and judge by precision-recall, not accuracy.
  • Some labels are wrong. A deep tree memorizes each wrong label as its own tiny leaf and passes the mistake on to every similar new row.
  • The same customers in training and test (repeat borrowers). A memorizing tree scores them well because it has seen them, so the test score is too optimistic. Split by customer, not by row.
  • An outlier in the target. Under squared error it moves leaf means and can capture the top splits; under absolute error it's ignored (section 13).

16The algorithm in pseudocode

The full CART build for classification, with the split search, missing values, categories, prediction and pruning. Regression swaps class counts for the running sum of targets and \(\sum_k n_k^2/n\) for \(S^2/n\).

    node = Node(value=class_shares(y[rows]))
    if (depth == max_depth or len(rows) < min_samples_split
            or is_pure(y[rows])):

    # it has found at least one valid split

                else best_numeric(rows, j))
        if cand and (best is None or cand.gain > best.gain):
            best = cand
    if best is None:
        return node                       # no valid split
    w_gain = best.gain * len(rows) / N
    if w_gain < min_decrease:
        return node                       # no split pays
    left, right = partition(rows, best)
    node.split = best
    node.left = build(left, depth + 1)
    node.right = build(right, depth + 1)
    return node

    L = zeros(K); R = counts(y[present])  # class counts
    M = counts(y[blank])
    best = None
    for i in range(len(present) - 1):     # one sweep
        L[y[present[i]]] += 1             # O(1) per row
        R[y[present[i]]] -= 1
        a, b = X[present[i], j], X[present[i + 1], j]
        if a == b:
            continue                      # not a real gap
        for nan_left in (True, False):    # try both
            l = L + M if nan_left else L
            r = R if nan_left else R + M
            if min(l.sum(), r.sum()) < min_samples_leaf:
                continue
            g = gain(l, r)                # from counts
            if best is None or g > best.gain:

    return best

def predict(node, x):

        if isnan(x[s.feature]):

    while tree.has_splits():

        t = min(tree.internal_nodes(), key=g)
        alphas.append(g(t))

        # node t is now a leaf

Use one tree when:

  • the rules must be read, audited or followed by people: a credit policy (“refer if 2+ late payments, or income under ₹40k with EMIs over half”), a fraud triage rule, a collections segmentation;
  • you want a quick first look or a leakage check: a depth-2 tree with an AUC of 0.99 means look for a leaked feature;
  • you need bins for a scorecard: a shallow tree per feature is a common way to choose the bins for weight-of-evidence logistic regression.

Use something else when:

  • accuracy matters most: a random forest or gradient boosting, which fix a single tree's instability, rough probabilities and staircases;
  • effects are smooth or additive, or boundaries diagonal: a linear model;
  • you must predict beyond the training range: no tree model can, so model a ratio or add a linear part;
  • you'll act on the probabilities: smooth or calibrate first;
  • the inputs are raw images, audio or text: neural networks.

Answer out loud first, then open the answer. Numbers in the answers come from the credit example above, but each answer stands on its own.

Splitting

1Walk through how a tree decides its first split.

It tries every feature and every threshold on all the training rows, scores each candidate by how much it lowers the size-weighted impurity, and keeps the best one.

  1. Measure the root: its impurity, e.g. Gini \(2p(1 - p)\). With 8.7% defaults that's 0.159.
  2. For each feature, sort the rows by it. Candidate thresholds sit at the midpoints between consecutive distinct values: any threshold inside a gap sends the same rows left, so one per gap is enough.
  3. Sweep the sorted rows once, moving them one at a time from the right side to the left and updating the class counts on each side. At each gap, score the split from the counts:
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{gain} &= I(P) - \tfrac{n_L}{n} I(L) - \tfrac{n_R}{n} I(R) \\ \text{children} &= \tfrac{n_L}{n}\text{Gini}_L + \tfrac{n_R}{n}\text{Gini}_R \\ &= 1 - \tfrac{1}{n}\textstyle\sum_k \big(n_{Lk}^2/n_L + n_{Rk}^2/n_R\big) \end{aligned} \]

In words: the gain is the parent's impurity minus the children's, each child weighted by its share of the rows, and the weighted Gini needs only the class counts \(n_{Lk}, n_{Rk}\) on each side.

  1. Take the best threshold per feature, then the best feature. In the credit example, late ≤ 1.5 gains 0.012, beating income (0.009), EMI share (0.0025) and age (0.0005).

Why it's done this way: sorting lets one sweep with running counts score each candidate in \(O(1)\), so a feature costs \(O(n \log n)\) instead of \(O(n^2)\); the size weights stop a tiny pure child from looking excellent. Follow-up: the search is exhaustive for this one split but greedy across levels, so the best first split isn't necessarily the start of the best tree.

2Gini or entropy: what's the difference, and does it matter?

They're nearly the same curve, so they almost always choose the same split. Entropy is steeper near pure nodes, so it gives more credit for cleaning up a nearly pure node; Gini is the default because it's CART's choice and skips the log.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Gini} &= \sum_k p_k (1 - p_k) \\ &= 1 - \sum_k p_k^2 \\ \text{entropy} &= \sum_k p_k (-\log p_k) \end{aligned} \]

In words: both are the expected “surprise” at a row's class. Gini uses \(1 - p\) where entropy uses \(-\ln p\), and \(1 - p\) is the tangent line of \(-\ln p\) at \(p = 1\), so Gini is entropy with the log replaced by its first-order approximation.

  • Where they differ: rare classes. \(-\ln p\) blows up near 0, so entropy rises steeply near the ends. A 2%-positive node sits at 14% of entropy's peak but only 8% of Gini's.
  • In practice: on real data, trees grown with each typically differ in a split or two, often noise splits. Depth and leaf size matter far more.

Trade-off: Gini is a little faster; entropy may suit very imbalanced problems slightly better. Follow-up: “the Gini” in credit scoring usually means the Gini coefficient, \(2\,\text{AUC} - 1\), a ranking metric unrelated to Gini impurity.

4Why weight the children by size? Why not split on misclassification error?

Without the weights, peeling off one pure row would always look great. Misclassification error is a bad criterion because it's straight-sided: a split that sharpens the class shares without flipping a majority scores exactly zero.

  • Size weights: a one-row child is pure (impurity 0). In a plain average it would count for half, so a useless split would beat a real one. Weighted, it counts for 1/\(n\).
  • The geometry: the parent's class share is the size-weighted average of the children's, so the children's weighted impurity is a point on the chord between their two points on the impurity curve.
\[ \begin{aligned} p &= \tfrac{n_L}{n} p_L + \tfrac{n_R}{n} p_R \\ \text{gain} &= I(p) - \tfrac{n_L}{n} I(p_L) - \tfrac{n_R}{n} I(p_R) \\ &\ge 0 \end{aligned} \]

In words: for a strictly concave impurity (Gini, entropy) the chord lies below the curve, so any change in the shares gives a positive gain (Jensen's inequality). Misclassification error, \(1 - \max_k p_k\), is a straight line on each side of 0.5, so when both children keep the same majority the chord lies on it and the gain is 0.

Example: a root split that moves the default share from 8.7% to 5.9% and 30.5% gains 0.012 in Gini and 0.038 in entropy, and exactly 0 in misclassification error, because both sides are still mostly payers. Follow-up: misclassification error is fine for pruning and for reporting accuracy; it's only blind as a split criterion.

5How is the best threshold for a numeric feature found efficiently, and what does building a tree cost?

Sort the node's rows by the feature, then sweep the gaps between distinct values with running class counts, scoring every midpoint in one pass: \(O(n \log n)\) per feature per node, dominated by the sort.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{per node} &= O(d\, n_t \log n_t) \\ \text{per level} &= O(d\, n \log n) \\ \text{whole tree} &= O(D\, d\, n \log n) \end{aligned} \]

In words: \(n_t\) is the node's rows, \(d\) the features, \(D\) the depth. Each row sits in one node per level, so a level's nodes add up to \(n\) rows. A balanced tree has \(D \approx \log_2 n\), about \(O(d\, n \log^2 n)\) in all; prediction is \(O(D)\).

  • Why only distinct values: a threshold can't separate equal values. Why midpoints: every threshold in a gap gives the same training split; the midpoint hedges for unseen values.
  • Why the sweep: moving one row across updates the counts in \(O(1)\) (\(O(K)\) for \(K\) classes). Rescoring each threshold from scratch would cost \(O(n^2)\) per feature.

Trade-off: histogram methods (LightGBM, XGBoost hist) bin each feature into about 256 quantiles once, then each node adds up per-bin counts in \(O(n_t\, d)\) and tries only bin edges; a sibling's histogram is the parent's minus the other child's. Much faster, at the cost of threshold resolution.

6How do trees split categorical features?

A categorical split sends a subset of the categories left. For a binary or regression target, sorting the categories by their mean target and trying the \(K - 1\) cuts in that order is guaranteed to find the best of all \(2^{K-1} - 1\) subsets; otherwise use one-vs-rest or heuristics.

  • The search space: \(K\) categories have \(2^{K-1} - 1\) two-group splits: 127 for 8, about \(5 \times 10^{14}\) for 50. Exhaustive search is out for large \(K\).
  • One-vs-rest: the \(K\) splits that peel off one category, which is all a tree can do with one-hot columns. It needs several levels to build a good grouping, and each level costs rows.
  • Sort by mean target: optimal for a 0/1 target with Gini or entropy (Breiman) and for squared error (Fisher). Intuition: if a riskier category sat on the safe side while a safer one of the same size sat on the risky side, swapping them pushes the two sides apart without moving the parent, which can only raise the gain of a concave impurity. So the best groups are contiguous in sorted order.
  • Example: four categories of 100 rows each, at 5%, 8%, 25% and 30% default. The sorted cut {5%, 8%} vs {25%, 30%} gains 0.0221; the best one-vs-rest split gains 0.0113.

Trade-offs: with more than two classes there's no single order, so no shortcut. The sort order is learned from the labels, so rare categories make it noisy and the split overfits (LightGBM adds minimum counts and smoothing). sklearn's DecisionTreeClassifier needs numbers: one-hot for few categories, or target encoding computed out-of-fold and shrunk towards the overall rate, \((k_c + m\bar p)/(n_c + m)\), to avoid leaking each row's own label.

7What does a regression tree's leaf predict, how are its splits scored, and why is absolute error slower?

A leaf predicts the mean of its training targets, and a split is scored by variance reduction. With absolute error the leaf predicts the median, which can't be updated from running sums, so the sweep is slower.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{SSE} &= \sum_i y_i^2 - \frac{S^2}{n} \\ \text{SSE}_L + \text{SSE}_R &= \sum_i y_i^2 - \Big(\frac{S_L^2}{n_L} + \frac{S_R^2}{n_R}\Big) \end{aligned} \]

In words: \(S\) is the sum of the targets. The total \(\sum y_i^2\) is the same for every split, so the sweep just maximizes \(S_L^2/n_L + S_R^2/n_R\), updating \(S_L\) with one addition per row moved.

  • Why the mean: \(\sum_i (y_i - c)^2\) has slope \(-2\sum_i (y_i - c)\), zero at \(c = \bar y\).
  • Why the median for absolute error: moving the guess up helps every target above it and hurts every target below by the same amount, so the best guess has half on each side. A median can't be maintained with one addition per row, so the sweep has to keep each side's values ordered.
  • Classification is regression in disguise: for a 0/1 label, Gini \(2p(1 - p)\) is exactly twice the variance \(p(1 - p)\), so a squared-error tree on 0/1 labels is the same tree.

Follow-ups: heavy-tailed targets let a few huge values dominate the splits and leaf means, so model the log or use absolute error; counts suit Poisson deviance, which judges a miss relative to the mean.

8Why is greedy tree growth not globally optimal?

Each split is chosen as the best one now, without looking ahead and never revisited. A split that's useless alone but enables great splits below it, as in an XOR, is never chosen.

  • XOR: label 1 when exactly one of two features is above 0.5. Splitting either feature at 0.5 leaves both halves 50/50, so its gain is about 0, although a depth-2 tree using exactly those cuts is perfect. Greedy grabs noise instead (on 1,000 rows it needed depth 5 for what depth 2 can do), and a minimum-gain stop rule never starts at all.
  • Why it usually works: an AND leaves a trace in each feature alone (each half of “low income AND high EMIs” already separates defaulters a little), so greedy can follow it one feature at a time.
  • Why not search properly: finding the optimal tree is NP-complete (Hyafil and Rivest, 1976). One-step lookahead costs roughly \((d\,n)^2\) per node instead of \(d\,n\).

Fixes: grow deep and prune (pruning judges whole branches, so it keeps a weak split with strong children), or average many randomized trees (a random forest of depth-3 trees solves the XOR that one depth-3 tree can't). Optimal-tree solvers exist for small data and shallow trees.

Growing, stopping and pruning

9A tree gets 100% training accuracy and a test log loss near 4. What happened?

It grew without limits until every leaf was pure, so it memorized the training data, noise included, and every prediction is exactly 0 or 1.

  • Mechanism: with sklearn's defaults (max_depth=None, min_samples_leaf=1) a node splits until it's pure or its rows are identical. On 3,500 credit applicants that's depth 25 and 327 leaves, 139 of them a single row.
  • The log loss: a pure leaf predicts 0% or 100%. Every test row that lands in a leaf of the wrong class costs \(-\ln(2.2 \times 10^{-16}) \approx 36\) after clipping. 11% of test rows were such cases: \(0.11 \times 36 \approx 4\).
  • Bias and variance: training AUC climbs with every level, while test AUC peaked at depth 3 (0.808) and fell to 0.62 at full depth. Shallow trees underfit; deep trees fit noise that changes with every sample.

Fix: limit depth or leaf size, prune, or use an ensemble, choosing the settings by cross-validation. Follow-up: a training score says nothing about a tree's quality.

10Which pre-pruning knobs exist, and what's the difference between min_samples_split and min_samples_leaf?

min_samples_split says how many rows a node needs before it may split; its children can still be tiny. min_samples_leaf guarantees every leaf has at least that many rows, which directly limits how noisy a leaf's probability can be.

  • max_depth: readable, but blunt: big and small branches get the same limit.
  • min_samples_split: a 20-row node can still split into 1 and 19. In the credit example it left one-row leaves and a log loss of 2.0.
  • min_samples_leaf: every split must leave enough rows on both sides. The most direct control over leaf noise.
  • max_leaf_nodes: grows best-first, so it goes deep only where the data supports it.
  • min_impurity_decrease: refuses splits whose gain, weighted by the node's share of all rows, is too small.
\[ \tfrac{N_t}{N}\big(I_t - \tfrac{N_{tL}}{N_t} I_L - \tfrac{N_{tR}}{N_t} I_R\big) \ge \text{threshold} \]

Trade-off: every stop rule judges one split at a time, so it can refuse a weak split whose children would be strong (an XOR). Tune depth and leaf size together by cross-validation, with time-based folds when the model will score future data.

11How does cost-complexity pruning work, and how does it differ from stopping early?

Grow a full tree, then for a price \(\alpha\) per leaf keep the subtree that minimizes training impurity plus \(\alpha\) times its leaf count. As \(\alpha\) rises, branches are cut weakest-first, giving a short nested sequence of trees; pick \(\alpha\) by cross-validation.

\[ \begin{aligned} R_\alpha(T) &= R(T) + \alpha\,\lvert T\rvert \\ g(t) &= \frac{R(t) - R(T_t)}{\lvert T_t\rvert - 1} \end{aligned} \]

In words: \(R(T)\) is the leaves' impurity weighted by their share of rows; \(g(t)\) is how much impurity the branch under node \(t\) removes per extra leaf. The branch with the smallest \(g\) is the weakest link: once \(\alpha\) passes \(g(t)\), its leaves aren't worth their price.

  • The loop: cut the weakest branch, recompute \(g\) for the branches above it, repeat. Each cut gives the next tree and the \(\alpha\) where it takes over. Breiman showed these optimal subtrees are nested, so this list contains every tree pruning could pick.
  • Example: a 4-leaf tree on ten rows (leaves with 0 of 3, 1 of 3, 2 of 2 and 1 of 2 positives) has \(\alpha\) values 0.033, 0.05 and 0.163: cut the left branch, then the right, then the root split. On real data, a 327-leaf tree pruned by CV came down to 4 leaves with no noise splits.
  • Why \(\alpha\) by CV: on training data more leaves always lowers \(R(T)\), so the best \(\alpha\) there is always 0.

Pre vs post: stopping early judges each split alone, so it refuses a weak split with strong children; pruning judges whole branches and keeps it. Pruning can only remove splits, never add one growth didn't make, and it costs growing the full tree first.

12Why are decision trees high-variance?

Splits often win by small margins, so a slightly different sample picks a different split, and every split below depends on the ones above, so one change near the top rewrites the whole branch.

  • Hierarchy amplifies noise: a different root threshold changes which rows reach every node below, so their splits change too.
  • Small branches are least stable: fewer rows, closer races. In 20 bootstrap refits of a depth-3 credit tree, the root was the same 18 times, but the question in a 390-row branch took 17 different forms.
  • Leaves are small averages: one applicant's predicted risk ranged from 27% to 100% across the refits (standard deviation 0.23), and 0.36 with full-depth trees, whose pure leaves flip between 0 and 1.

Fixes: bagging and random forests average many trees on resampled data, which cancels much of the variance (decorrelating them with random feature subsets helps more). Bigger leaves and shallower trees reduce it at the cost of bias. Trade-off: an average of 300 trees is no longer a readable rule list; before trusting one tree's rules, refit on resamples and keep only the rules that survive.

Behaviour

13How do trees handle missing values?

Four ways: learn which side blanks go at each split (sklearn ≥ 1.3, XGBoost, LightGBM), use surrogate splits (classic CART), send the row down both branches with fractional weights (C4.5), or impute first, ideally with a “was blank” indicator.

  • Learned direction: during the sweep, rows with a missing value are set aside, and each candidate threshold is scored twice, with the blanks sent left and sent right. The better side is stored per node. If a feature had no blanks at that node in training, a blank at prediction goes to the child with more training rows: a guess, not a learned rule.
  • Surrogate splits: after choosing the primary split, find for each other feature the split that best mimics it (sends the most rows the same way); keep those that beat sending everyone to the bigger side. A row missing the primary feature follows the best available surrogate. They help only when features are correlated.
  • Fractional rows (C4.5): send the row down both branches, weighted by the share of training rows that went each way, and average the leaves it reaches.
  • Imputation: mean or median makes a blank look typical and erases any signal; adding an indicator column restores it.

Why it matters: blanks are often informative (missing not at random). If risky applicants skip the income field, a native-NaN tree learns to send blanks to the risky side (40% for one such applicant), median imputation makes them look safe (7%), and a tree that never saw a blank sends them to the bigger, safer side (2.5%). Trade-off: a model that leans on blanks breaks when the process that creates them changes, so monitor missing rates and treat blanks identically in training and serving.

14Why don't trees need feature scaling?

A split only asks whether a value is above or below a threshold, so only the order of a feature's values matters, and scaling, logs and other monotone transforms keep that order.

  • Mechanism: after a monotone increasing transform, sorting the node's rows gives the same order, so the sweep sees the same candidate partitions with the same gains, and the same split wins at every node. Only the printed thresholds change units (a log-income tree's threshold maps back to the same ₹39.46k).
  • A decreasing transform gives the same partitions with left and right swapped.
  • Contrast: gradient descent, regularization penalties and distances (k-NN, SVMs, PCA) all depend on scale, which is why linear models and neural nets need standardizing.

Caveats: mixing features (PCA rotation, sums and differences) does change the tree. A new value inside the gap between two training values could route differently, since the midpoint moves under a non-linear transform. Standardizing anyway is harmless.

15Why can't trees extrapolate?

Every prediction is a leaf's mean of training targets, so it can't leave the training range, and every input beyond the outermost threshold lands in the same edge leaf: the prediction goes flat.

\[ \begin{aligned} f(x) &= c_j \ \text{ for } x \in R_j \\ \min_i y_i \le c_j &\le \max_i y_i \end{aligned} \]

In words: the leaf value \(c_j\) is an average of training targets, so it's bounded by them, and the edge boxes \(R_j\) stretch to infinity.

  • Example: credit limit grows with income, and training incomes stop at ₹150k. For an applicant with 20% EMIs (truth \(1.6 \times\) income), a depth-6 tree predicts 217.6 at incomes of 150, 200 and 300, where the truth is 240, 320 and 480; a linear model keeps rising.
  • Ensembles too: a random forest averages flat trees and boosting adds them, so both go flat.

Fixes: model a quantity that stays in range (limit per rupee of income) and multiply back, remove the trend first, or add a linear component (LightGBM's linear_tree). Follow-up: in production this shows up as drift: new data piling into the edge leaves.

16Why do trees struggle with diagonal boundaries and smooth additive effects?

Every split is parallel to an axis, so a diagonal boundary must be built as a staircase, and every step costs leaves and data.

  • The staircase: for the boundary “feature 2 > feature 1”, a depth-2 tree (4 leaves) scores 86%, depth 4 (16 leaves) 94%, an unlimited tree (54 leaves) 97.7%, and logistic regression 99.85% with one line. A staircase of \(k\) steps leaves wrong-side triangles of area about \(1/(4k)\), so the error falls only like 1/leaves.
  • Additive effects: giving each of two features 4 steps of effect needs the 4 steps of one repeated inside each of the 4 ranges of the other, 16 leaves, where an additive model needs 8 numbers.

Fixes: hand the tree the combination: with the difference as a feature, a single split is perfect; ratios like debt-to-income are worth building even for trees. Oblique trees split on weighted sums but are slow and hard to read; ensembles average many staircases into a smoother edge. Follow-up: rotating the features (PCA) can turn an axis-parallel rule into a diagonal one and hurt a tree.

17A split like “age ≤ 23.18” appears deep in a small branch. Is it real? Why do such splits appear?

Probably noise. In a small branch where the truth is flat, every candidate cut is noise, a depth limit still forces a split, and the luckiest of hundreds of cuts wins; features with many distinct values hold the most lottery tickets.

  • Mechanism: in a 331-row branch with three continuous features there are about 990 candidate thresholds. The best of 990 random gains looks like a rule: 10 of the 18 youngest applicants happened to default.
  • The bias: a continuous feature offers \(n - 1\) thresholds, a binary one 1. In 200 simulations (1,000 rows, a pure-noise target with 10% positives), a continuous noise feature beat a binary one at the root 96% of the time.
  • How to tell: its leaf is tiny (18 rows), it's among the first branches pruning removes, bootstrap refits rarely repeat it, and on fresh data its leaf's share collapses (55.6% in training, 12.5% on test).

Defences: min_samples_leaf, cost-complexity pruning, and significance-test trees (CHAID, conditional inference) that pick the feature by a test before searching thresholds. Follow-up: the same bias inflates impurity-based feature importance.

Importance and explanations

18How is a tree's feature importance computed, and what are its pitfalls?

sklearn's importance is the mean decrease in impurity (MDI): each split's gain weighted by the share of training rows reaching it, summed per feature and normalized to 1. It's biased towards many-valued features, computed on training data, and split arbitrarily between correlated features; permutation importance on held-out data and SHAP are the usual checks.

\[ \text{importance}_j \propto \sum_{\text{splits } t \text{ on } j} \frac{n_t}{N}\, \text{gain}_t \]
  • It counts impurity removed, not order: a split deep in a 17% branch with a huge gain can outrank the root split.
  • Many-valued bias: in an unconstrained credit tree, pure-noise age (continuous) got 0.205 against 0.096 for late (a real cause with few values); a column of random numbers got 0.200.
  • Training data: splits that only fit noise count fully.
  • Correlated features: an exact copy of a feature takes some or all of its importance depending on random_state.

Permutation importance shuffles one column in held-out data and measures the score drop: the random column scores about 0. But with correlated features, the twin still carries the signal so both look unimportant, and shuffling creates impossible rows. SHAP (TreeSHAP, exact in polynomial time for trees) splits each prediction into additive per-feature contributions averaged over all feature orders; mean \(\lvert\text{SHAP}\rvert\) is a global importance, but it inherits any noise splits the model made, so it's only as clean as the tree. None of them measures causation.

19How would you explain one prediction from a tree?

Walk the row's path: each question moves the node's predicted share, and the steps add up exactly to the prediction minus the base rate. SHAP gives an order-independent version of the same decomposition.

\[ \begin{aligned} f(x) &= \bar p + \sum_{\text{path}} \big(\hat p_{\text{child}} - \hat p_{\text{parent}}\big) \\ 49.3 &= 8.7 - 2.8 + 14.1 + 29.3 \ (\text{points}) \end{aligned} \]

In words: everyone starts at the base rate 8.7%; “at most 1 late payment” lowers it to 5.9%, “income ≤ ₹39k” raises it to 20%, “EMIs over half” to 49.3%. The reasons to give are the steps that raised the risk, largest first; a counterfactual comes free (with EMIs under half, 3.7%).

  • The catch: the path walk credits whichever feature was asked when a jump happened, so it depends on the order of the questions. Here EMI gets +29.3 because income was asked first.
  • SHAP averages each feature's contribution over all orders, using the tree's training counts for features not yet known. For the same applicant it gives income +26.4 and EMI +17.7: without a low income, the EMI question is never reached.

Follow-up: before defending a single tree's decision to a customer, check that the rule survives refits on resampled data.

20What happens if you duplicate a feature, or add a random ID-like column?

A duplicate changes no prediction but splits the importance between the copies arbitrarily. A random many-valued column wins the splits where nothing real is left to find, rarely in a shallow tree and often in a deep one, which inflates its impurity importance while its permutation importance stays near zero.

  • Duplicate: a split on the copy is the same split, so the gains tie exactly. sklearn tries features in a random order per node and the first one wins, so random_state decides which copy gets the credit (0.036 and 0.201 with one seed, 0.237 and 0 with another). Correlated near-copies share it the same way, more softly.
  • Random column, shallow tree: it can't beat a real signal, but it competes for the noise splits. In 20 tries it took the depth-3 tree's income ≤ 146.44 split half the time, with almost no effect on test AUC.
  • Random column, deep tree: in small branches, where the real features are exhausted, its thousands of thresholds win by luck. Its MDI reached 0.200, on a par with income; its permutation importance was −0.003; test AUC barely moved. A random yes/no column got only 0.014.

Why it matters: a true ID column lets a deep tree memorize individuals, which looks good only if the same customers appear in training and test. Drop identifiers, limit leaf size, and judge importance on held-out data.

Probabilities

21Are a tree's predicted probabilities calibrated?

Only roughly, and only for big leaves. A leaf's probability is its training share \(k/n\): noisy in small leaves, pushed towards 0 and 1 because the tree chose those leaves for looking extreme, and exactly 0 or 1 when pure. Deep trees are overconfident.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat p &= \frac{k}{n} \\ \text{SE}(\hat p) &= \sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1 - \hat p)}{n}} \end{aligned} \]

In words: a share from \(n\) rows typically misses by its standard error. A 20-row leaf at 90% has an SE of 6.7 points.

  • Selection bias: of hundreds of candidate cuts, the tree keeps the one whose children look most separated, so small leaves overstate how extreme they are. In the credit tree, an 18-row leaf said 55.6% and delivered 12.5% on test; a 2,436-row leaf said 2.5% and delivered 3.0%.
  • Pure leaves: with min_samples_leaf=30, half the test rows got exactly 0% or 100%: AUC 0.804 but log loss 0.731. Ranking and calibration are different questions.

Fixes: bigger leaves; smoothing towards the base rate, \((k + m\bar p)/(n + m)\) (log loss 0.731 → 0.241 with the same leaves); honest leaves filled from held-out rows; Platt or isotonic calibration on a held-out set; or an ensemble. Check it with a reliability curve, log loss or Brier score on held-out data.

22What does class_weight="balanced" do to a tree?

It makes each minority row count \(r\) times as much in every impurity and every leaf share. That changes which splits win and multiplies every leaf's odds by \(r\), so the probabilities are inflated, while the ranking barely moves.

\[ \begin{aligned} p_w &= \frac{r k}{r k + (n - k)} \\ p &= \frac{p_w}{p_w + r(1 - p_w)} \end{aligned} \]

In words: a leaf with \(k\) positives among \(n\) rows reports \(p_w\) under weights; solving for \(k/n\) gives the second line, which undoes it exactly. Here \(r\) is the ratio of negatives to positives in training.

  • Example: with 8.7% defaults, \(r \approx 10.5\); the balanced tree's average prediction is 32.7% and its log loss 0.466. The correction brings the log loss to 0.236, not to the unweighted tree's 0.216, because the splits stay as the weighted tree chose them.
  • Why the ranking barely moves: multiplying every leaf's odds by the same \(r\) keeps the leaves in order; only changed splits move the AUC.

Trade-off: weights can help a rare class win splits, but if you need probabilities, correct or recalibrate afterwards. Moving the decision threshold is often all that's needed. Oversampling behaves the same way.

23How does severe class imbalance (1:100) affect a tree?

Gains become tiny, leaves of the majority class become pure, predict flags almost nobody, and accuracy becomes meaningless.

  • Tiny gains: the root's Gini is \(2 \times 0.01 \times 0.99 \approx 0.02\), so every gain is small, and a min_impurity_decrease tuned on balanced data blocks every split.
  • Pure leaves: a 30-row leaf of ordinary users holds no positive with probability \(0.99^{30} \approx 0.74\), so most such leaves say exactly 0%.
  • Majority votes: predict uses a 0.5 cut-off, and hardly any leaf has a positive majority.

What to do: use leaf sizes large enough to hold some positives, use predict_proba with a threshold chosen for the business cost, consider class weights (then correct the probabilities), smooth or calibrate the leaves, and judge by precision-recall or PR-AUC, not accuracy. Trade-off: entropy is slightly more sensitive than Gini to cleaning up rare-class pockets, but leaf size matters far more.

Building and choosing

24Write the pseudocode for building a decision tree.

Recursive: at each node, check the stop rules, search every feature's sorted thresholds for the best gain, split, and recurse into both children.

def build(rows, depth):
    node = Leaf(value=class_shares(y[rows]))
    if depth == max_depth or is_pure(rows) \
            or len(rows) < min_samples_split:
        return node
    best = None
    for j in features:
        order = sorted(rows, key=lambda r: X[r, j])
        L, R = zeros(K), counts(y[order])
        for i in range(len(order) - 1):
            L[y[order[i]]] += 1; R[y[order[i]]] -= 1
            if X[order[i], j] == X[order[i + 1], j]:
                continue                 # not a gap
            if min(L.sum(), R.sum()) < min_samples_leaf:
                continue
            g = gini_gain(L, R)          # from counts
            if best is None or g > best.gain:
                a = X[order[i], j]
                b = X[order[i + 1], j]
                best = Split(j, (a + b) / 2, g)
    if best is None:
        return node
    if best.gain * len(rows) / N < min_gain:
        return node
    left = [r for r in rows if X[r, best.j] <= best.t]
    right = [r for r in rows if X[r, best.j] > best.t]
    return Node(best, build(left, depth + 1),
                build(right, depth + 1))

Points to say: sorting lets one sweep with running counts score each candidate in \(O(1)\), so a node costs \(O(d\, n \log n)\); the counts give the Gini gain directly; prediction walks one path, \(O(\text{depth})\). For regression, replace the class counts with running sums of \(y\) and the leaf value with the mean. Missing values add a second pass per threshold (blanks left, then right), and pruning is a separate weakest-link loop over the finished tree.

25When would you use a single tree rather than a random forest or boosting?

When the rules must be read, audited or followed by people, or as a quick baseline and leakage check. When accuracy or probabilities matter most, use an ensemble.

  • Single tree: a credit policy or fraud triage rule people must follow, reason codes from the path, bins for a scorecard, a first look at the data. A depth-2 tree with an AUC of 0.99 is a leak alarm.
  • Its weaknesses: high variance (rules change with the sample), rough probabilities, staircase boundaries, a ceiling on accuracy.
  • Ensembles: forests average away the variance and smooth the probabilities; boosting fixes errors step by step and usually wins on tabular data. Explain them with SHAP, and add monotonic constraints where policy demands a direction.

Trade-off: none of them extrapolates, so for trends add a linear part or model a ratio. If you need one readable tree that behaves like the ensemble, fit a shallow tree to the ensemble's predictions and check how often they disagree.

19Cheat sheet

Impurity

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Gini} &= 1 - \textstyle\sum_k p_k^2 \\ \text{Gini, 2 classes} &= 2p(1 - p) \\ \text{entropy} &= -\textstyle\sum_k p_k \log_2 p_k \\ \text{misclass.} &= 1 - \max_k p_k \\ \text{regression} &= \tfrac{1}{n}\textstyle\sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2 \end{aligned} \]

Scoring a split

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{gain} &= I(P) - \tfrac{n_L}{n} I(L) - \tfrac{n_R}{n} I(R) \\ \text{Gini, kids} &= 1 - \tfrac{1}{n}\textstyle\sum_k \big(n_{Lk}^2/n_L + n_{Rk}^2/n_R\big) \\ \text{SSE, kids} &= \textstyle\sum_i y_i^2 - \big(S_L^2/n_L + S_R^2/n_R\big) \\ \text{SplitInfo} &= -\textstyle\sum_j \tfrac{n_j}{n}\log_2 \tfrac{n_j}{n} \\ \text{GainRatio} &= \text{IG} / \text{SplitInfo} \end{aligned} \]

Search and cost

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{per node} &= O(d\, n_t \log n_t) \\ \text{per tree} &= O(D\, d\, n \log n) \end{aligned} \]
  • Candidates: midpoints of the gaps between consecutive distinct values; sweep with running counts.
  • Categories with a binary or numeric target: sort by mean target, try \(K - 1\) of the \(2^{K-1} - 1\) splits.

Leaves

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat p &= k/n \\ \text{SE} &= \sqrt{\hat p(1 - \hat p)/n} \\ \text{smoothed} &= \frac{k + m\bar p}{n + m} \\ \text{undo weight } r &: \ p = \frac{p_w}{p_w + r(1 - p_w)} \end{aligned} \]

Stopping and pruning

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{min decrease} &\le \tfrac{N_t}{N}\big(I_t - \tfrac{N_{tL}}{N_t} I_L - \tfrac{N_{tR}}{N_t} I_R\big) \\ R_\alpha(T) &= R(T) + \alpha \lvert T\rvert \\ g(t) &= \frac{R(t) - R(T_t)}{\lvert T_t\rvert - 1} \end{aligned} \]

Importance

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{MDI}_j &\propto \textstyle\sum_{t \text{ on } j} \tfrac{n_t}{N}\, \text{gain}_t \\ \text{permutation}_j &= \text{score} - \text{score}_{j \text{ shuffled}} \end{aligned} \]

Behaviour in one line each

  • Monotone transforms change nothing; mixing features does.
  • Flat beyond the training range; diagonals as staircases; smooth additive effects need many leaves.
  • High variance, worst in small branches; averaging fixes it.
  • Many-valued features win more splits by luck, and inflate MDI.
  • Blanks: learned direction, surrogates, or impute plus an indicator.
