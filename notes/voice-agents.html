Voice agents: speech recognition, synthesis and the real-time stack

How a phone or app voice agent hears, decides and speaks in about a second: audio and log-mel features, cascade vs speech-to-speech, how CTC, transducers and Whisper recognise speech, WER and why it misses the phone number, VAD, endpointing and barge-in, how TTS works and is scored, the latency budget stage by stage, and how to build, test and price the whole thing.

About 75 minutes, plus 30 questions · one running call: a caller on an 8 kHz phone line says “cancel order four seven two nine” · every number checked by a script

In short

  1. A voice agent is a real-time loop: take audio in, decide when the caller has finished, understand, reply, speak, and keep listening in case they interrupt. Most production agents are a cascade (speech recognition → LLM → speech synthesis) that streams at every stage. Speech-to-speech models do it all in one network over audio tokens: faster and more natural, but harder to control, debug and use with tools.
  2. Speech recognition (ASR) is an alignment problem: about 100 feature frames a second have to become two or three words a second, and nobody labelled which frame belongs to which word. CTC adds a blank symbol and sums over every alignment. A transducer (RNN-T) adds a small language model and streams naturally. An attention encoder–decoder such as Whisper is very accurate but works on 30-second windows, so it doesn't stream as built.
  3. Word error rate is (substitutions + deletions + insertions) / reference words. Normalise both texts before scoring. WER can exceed 100%, and it weights every word the same, so a 5% WER can still get the phone number wrong. Track entity accuracy alongside it.
  4. Turn-taking is the hardest part of the experience. Every millisecond of the silence threshold is added to every reply, but a short one cuts people off mid-number. Semantic turn detectors shorten the wait when a turn is clearly complete. Barge-in needs echo cancellation, an instant flush of queued audio, and trimming the LLM's history to what the caller actually heard.
  5. Speech synthesis (TTS): text normalisation → phonemes → mel spectrogram → waveform, or one end-to-end model, or a language model over codec tokens. Stream it phrase by phrase. Score it with MOS (whose confidence intervals are wider than they look), re-transcription WER, speaker similarity and time to first audio.
  6. A common goal is roughly 1 second from the caller's last word to the agent's first (a rule of thumb). The end-of-turn wait and the LLM's time to first token dominate. Stream everything, start the LLM early, co-locate services, and fill unavoidable silence. Test with simulated callers and recorded-call regressions. Cost per minute is telephony + ASR + TTS + LLM, and the LLM part grows with call length.

01Audio basics

Before any model, the agent has a stream of numbers. How those numbers were produced (how often the sound was measured, how precisely, and over what band of frequencies) decides what a recogniser can possibly hear. Phone audio is much poorer than what most open models were trained on, and many voice-agent bugs start here.

The running example. A caller rings an order-support line from a mobile phone and says “cancel order four seven two nine”. The audio reaches the agent as an 8 kHz telephone stream. The note follows this call through recognition, scoring, turn-taking, the reply, its latency and its cost.

Sampling rate and bit depth

A microphone turns air pressure into a voltage, and an analogue-to-digital converter measures it \(f_s\) times a second: the sampling rate. A sampled signal can only represent frequencies up to half the sampling rate, the Nyquist frequency \(f_s/2\). Anything above it must be filtered out before sampling, or it folds back as false low frequencies (aliasing).

Vowels carry most of their energy below 4 kHz, which is why phone calls are intelligible at all. But fricatives such as “s”, “f” and “sh” live largely above 4 kHz, so on a phone line “six” and “fix”, or “sun” and “fun”, lose much of what tells them apart. That loss is permanent: resampling 8 kHz audio up to 16 kHz adds samples but cannot create the missing 4–8 kHz band.

Bit depth is how precisely each sample is stored. 16-bit linear PCM has 65,536 levels and about 96 dB of dynamic range (6 dB per bit). Bitrates follow directly:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{bitrate} &= f_s \times \text{bits per sample} \times \text{channels} \\ 16{,}000 \times 16 \times 1 &= 256 \text{ kbit/s} = 32 \text{ kB/s} \\ 8{,}000 \times 8 \times 1 &= 64 \text{ kbit/s} \end{aligned} \]

In words: one minute of 16 kHz, 16-bit mono audio is 1.92 MB. The phone network carries 8 kHz audio at only 8 bits per sample, 64 kbit/s, and gets away with it by spacing the 256 levels unevenly.

μ-law and A-law: 8 bits that sound like more

With 8 evenly spaced bits, a quiet sound (a soft consonant, a distant speaker) would use only a handful of levels and be buried in rounding noise, while loud vowels would waste precision. Hearing is roughly logarithmic, so the phone network (the G.711 standard) compresses each sample logarithmically before rounding it to 8 bits, and expands it again at the other end. This is called companding. The μ-law version, with \(\mu = 255\) and \(x\) scaled to [−1, 1], is:

\[ F(x) = \operatorname{sgn}(x)\,\frac{\ln(1 + \mu \lvert x \rvert)}{\ln(1 + \mu)} \]

In words: small amplitudes are stretched and large ones squeezed, so every loudness level gets about the same relative precision.

A sample at 1% of full scale gets 29 of the 127 positive levels under μ-law and about one level under linear 8-bit coding. Near that amplitude, one μ-law step is about 6% of the value; a linear 8-bit step would be 79% of it. Near half scale the μ-law step is about 4.5%, so the relative error stays roughly constant across loudness, which is what the ear cares about.

  • Who uses which: μ-law in North America and Japan, A-law (\(A = 87.6\), the same idea) in Europe and most other regions. Many cloud telephony APIs hand you 8 kHz μ-law regardless; Twilio's Media Streams, for example, send base64-encoded 8 kHz μ-law over a WebSocket. Others send 8 kHz 16-bit linear PCM, so always check the format; decoding with the wrong one produces noise, not an error.
  • In the real codec the curve is approximated by eight straight segments per sign, which gives 8 bits roughly the useful range of 13–14 linear bits.
  • Packets: phone audio usually travels in 20 ms packets: 160 samples, which at one byte per μ-law sample is 160 bytes.
  • In the pipeline: decode μ-law to linear PCM, resample to the ASR model's rate (with a proper filter), and on the way out downsample the TTS audio to 8 kHz and encode it back to μ-law.

Frames, windows and hops

Speech changes constantly, but over about 20–30 ms the shape of the mouth and throat barely moves, so a short slice of audio has a stable frequency content. Speech features are therefore computed on short overlapping windows that slide along by a hop (or stride). The standard choice is a 25 ms window with a 10 ms hop. At 16 kHz:

\[ \begin{aligned} W &= 0.025 \times 16{,}000 = 400 \text{ samples} \\ H &= 0.010 \times 16{,}000 = 160 \text{ samples} \\ \text{frames} &= 1 + \Big\lfloor \frac{N - W}{H} \Big\rfloor \end{aligned} \]

In words: frames come every hop, so there are 100 a second. For one second of audio (\(N = 16{,}000\)) the formula gives 98 frames; libraries that pad half a window at each end (“centred” frames) give \(1 + N/H = 101\). Whisper's 30-second window has 480,000 samples and, after it drops the last padded frame, 3,000 frames.

Each window is multiplied by a taper (usually a Hann window, which fades the edges to zero so the cut-off ends don't smear energy across frequencies), and a fast Fourier transform turns it into frequencies. With an FFT of size 400, there are \(400/2 + 1 = 201\) frequency bins spaced \(16{,}000/400 = 40\) Hz apart. Squaring the magnitudes gives the power at each frequency, and stacking frames side by side gives the spectrogram: time on one axis, frequency on the other.

Log-mel features, and why mel

201 linearly spaced bins are not how hearing works, and they carry detail ASR doesn't need. Two changes fix that.

  • The mel scale. Perceived pitch is roughly linear in frequency below about 1 kHz and roughly logarithmic above it: 100 Hz to 200 Hz sounds like a big step, 7,000 Hz to 7,100 Hz like nothing. One common formula is \(m = 2595 \log_{10}(1 + f/700)\): 1,000 Hz is 1,000 mel, 4,000 Hz is 2,146 mel, and 8,000 Hz is only 2,840 mel. The spectrogram is pooled through about 80 triangular filters spaced evenly in mel, so they are narrow at low frequencies (about 45–100 Hz wide below 1 kHz) and wide at high ones (500–600 Hz, 13–15 FFT bins, at the top).
  • The log. Loudness is perceived roughly logarithmically, and quiet consonants are thousands of times weaker than vowels; the log compresses that range so both matter. It also turns multiplicative effects such as a microphone's or phone line's frequency response into additive offsets, which per-utterance mean normalisation can subtract.

What this keeps is the spectral envelope, the broad peaks (formants) that mark which vowel or consonant is being said; what it blurs is the fine comb of pitch harmonics, which mostly says who is speaking and with what intonation. The result for a 30-second Whisper window is a matrix of 80 mel bands × 3,000 frames (128 bands in Whisper large-v3): 240,000 numbers instead of 480,000 samples, and far more learnable ones.

  • MFCCs are a further step: a cosine transform of the log-mel bands that decorrelates them, which suited older Gaussian-mixture models. Neural models take log-mel directly.
  • Raw waveform models such as wav2vec 2.0 learn their own front end with convolutions over the samples (one vector per 20 ms), and are then fine-tuned for ASR.

Phone audio into a 16 kHz model

Spread 80 mel filters over 0–8 kHz and 18 or 19 of them sit above 4 kHz, depending on the mel formula. Feed upsampled phone audio to a model trained on 16 kHz recordings and almost a quarter of its input features are empty: the model is seeing a kind of input it rarely saw in training. Add μ-law coding noise, packet loss, cheap handsets and speakerphones, and WER on calls is typically much worse than on clean benchmarks.

  • Train on what you'll serve: fine-tune on real call audio (how, in section 03), or on wideband data passed through the same path (downsample to 8 kHz, μ-law encode and decode, upsample).
  • Or use a telephony model that takes 8 kHz input directly (25 ms windows are 200 samples, 10 ms hops 80 samples).
  • Evaluate on your channel: a model's LibriSpeech WER says little about 8 kHz Hinglish calls from moving vehicles.

Say it in an interview: “Phone audio is 8 kHz, 8-bit μ-law or A-law: 64 kbit/s, nothing above 4 kHz. ASR models usually want 16 kHz log-mel features, 25 ms windows every 10 ms, so 100 frames a second and 80 mel bands. Mel spacing matches how pitch is heard, and the log compresses loudness and turns channel effects into offsets. Upsampling phone audio doesn't bring back the high band, so I'd fine-tune or evaluate on real 8 kHz audio.”

02Two architectures: cascade and speech-to-speech

There are two ways to turn a caller's audio into a spoken reply. The cascade puts text in the middle and uses three specialised models. Speech-to-speech models keep everything as audio tokens inside one network. The choice shapes latency, control, cost and how you debug.

The cascade

Audio arrives over the phone network or WebRTC. A voice activity detector (VAD) and a turn detector decide when the caller has finished (sections 05 and 06). A streaming ASR model has been transcribing all along and emits its final transcript. The LLM, with the conversation history and tools, generates a reply token by token; the orchestrator cuts the reply into phrases and sends each to a streaming TTS model as soon as it is complete, and audio chunks go back to the caller as they're synthesised. Nothing waits for a whole output, which is how the cascade gets close to one second.

  • What text in the middle buys: every turn is logged and inspectable; you can apply guardrails, redact, rewrite numbers for speech, and use any LLM with mature tool calling; each part can be measured on its own (WER, time to first token, time to first audio) and swapped for a better vendor or a language-specific model.
  • What it loses: everything that isn't words. Tone, hesitation, emphasis, anger, laughter and who is speaking are discarded by ASR, and the TTS has to guess the right tone from text alone. Latencies add up across stages, and ASR errors flow straight into the LLM, which sees “cancel the order for seven nine”.

Speech-to-speech models

A speech-to-speech model takes audio in and produces audio out with one network. The trick that makes this a language-modelling problem is the neural audio codec, which turns a waveform into a short sequence of discrete tokens and back.

  1. Encode: a convolutional encoder turns the waveform into one vector per frame (for example every 80 ms).
  2. Quantise in layers (residual vector quantisation, RVQ): replace the vector by the nearest entry of codebook 1; quantise what's left over (the residual) with codebook 2; and so on for \(Q\) codebooks. Each frame becomes \(Q\) integers: the first codebook captures the coarse content, later ones add detail.
  3. Model: a transformer predicts the next audio tokens (and usually text tokens) given everything so far, exactly like a text LLM predicts words. With \(Q\) tokens per frame, the tokens are either flattened into one sequence (\(Q\) times longer), interleaved with a delay pattern, or, as in Moshi, split between two transformers: a large one that takes one step per 80 ms frame and a small “depth” transformer that predicts that frame's 8 codebook tokens one after another.
  4. Decode: the codec decoder turns the predicted tokens back into a waveform.

Token arithmetic. Mimi, the codec behind the Moshi model, encodes 24 kHz audio at 12.5 frames a second with 8 codebooks of 2,048 entries (11 bits each):

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{tokens per second} &= 12.5 \times 8 = 100 \\ \text{bitrate} &= 12.5 \times 8 \times 11 = 1{,}100 \text{ bit/s} \end{aligned} \]

In words: about 1.1 kbit/s, roughly 350 times smaller than raw 24 kHz 16-bit audio (384 kbit/s). EnCodec at 6 kbit/s uses 75 frames a second × 8 codebooks, so 600 tokens a second. Compare text: at about 150 words a minute and roughly 1.3 tokens per word, the same speech is only about 3 text tokens a second. Audio is some 30 times more tokens than its transcript (how many positions the main transformer actually steps through depends on the design above: 12.5 a second in Moshi), which is why speech LLMs need low frame-rate codecs, and why their context fills and their cost per minute rises quickly.

Full duplex. Moshi models two audio streams at once, the user's and its own, plus a stream of text tokens aligned with its own speech (an “inner monologue” that improves what it says). At every 80 ms step it predicts its next tokens while still taking in the user's, so it can speak and listen at the same time, backchannel (“mm-hmm”) and be interrupted without a separate turn-taking module. Its authors report 160 ms theoretical and about 200 ms practical latency.

In between. Hosted realtime speech APIs from the major LLM providers expose speech-to-speech models with tool calling. A “half-cascade” takes audio into a speech-aware LLM but produces text, then uses a separate TTS: it hears tone and hesitation but keeps text-level control of the output.

When to choose which. Task-oriented agents for support, sales or healthcare, where entities must be exact, tools drive the outcome, transcripts must be audited, and Indian languages and code-switching matter, are mostly cascades today. Open-ended conversation, companionship and language practice, where naturalness and latency dominate, suit speech-to-speech. Many teams prototype on a realtime API and move hot paths to a cascade when they need control, or the reverse when naturalness is the bottleneck.

Trap

“Speech-to-speech is strictly better because it's lower latency.” Latency is one axis. The questions that decide production systems are usually whether the order number was captured exactly, whether the right tool was called once, whether the call can be audited, and whether the model handles the callers' languages. Say which of these your use case cares about before picking.

Say it in an interview: “A cascade is streaming ASR, an LLM and streaming TTS with an orchestrator for turn-taking and barge-in. Text in the middle gives control, tools, logs and per-stage metrics, at the cost of latency and lost tone. Speech-to-speech models run one transformer over codec tokens, about 100 audio tokens a second for a codec like Mimi, so they're faster and more natural but harder to steer and debug. For a support agent that must capture order numbers and call tools, I'd start with a cascade.”

03Speech recognition (ASR)

Automatic speech recognition (ASR, also called speech-to-text or STT) maps a sequence of audio features to a sequence of text tokens. This section builds the three model families from the one problem they all solve, then covers what a voice agent needs around them: streaming, vocabulary biasing, number formatting, code-switching and diarization.

The core problem: alignment

Say “cancel order four seven two nine” takes 2 seconds. That is 200 feature frames (50 after the encoder's first layers merge every 4 frames into one, the usual 4× subsampling; see Conformer below) for 6 words or 32 characters. Training data is audio plus its transcript, with no timestamps: nobody marked which frames are the “f” of “four”. People also speak at different speeds, stretch vowels and pause. So the model has to learn the alignment at the same time as the labels.

  • The old way: hidden Markov models with Gaussian mixtures, a pronunciation dictionary, a separately trained language model, and frame-level labels bootstrapped by forced alignment (lining up a known transcript with the audio to get each sound's timing). Many parts, each trained separately.
  • End-to-end models learn the alignment implicitly and output characters or subword tokens directly. There are three ways to do it: CTC, transducers and attention encoder–decoders. Most modern systems use the same kind of encoder (a Conformer or transformer over log-mel frames) and differ in the output layer and loss.
  • Output units: characters (small vocabulary, long outputs), or subword tokens from BPE or SentencePiece, typically a few hundred to a few thousand for CTC and transducer models and about 50,000 byte-level BPE tokens for Whisper.

CTC: a blank symbol and a sum over alignments

Connectionist temporal classification (Graves et al., 2006) keeps the model simple: the encoder emits, for every frame, a probability over the vocabulary plus one extra symbol, the blank (written _ here), meaning “nothing new at this frame”. A sequence of one symbol per frame is an alignment (or path). To read text off a path, apply the collapse rule: first merge consecutive repeats, then delete blanks.

The second row shows why the blank exists: without a blank between them, the two l's merge into one. A double letter needs a blank in between. Blanks also let the model say nothing during silence and between sounds, so most frames end up as blank: CTC outputs are “peaky”, a short spike per token in a sea of blanks.

The probability of a transcript is the sum over every path that collapses to it, and each path's probability is the product of its per-frame probabilities:

\[ \begin{aligned} P(y \mid x) &= \sum_{a \,\in\, \mathcal{B}^{-1}(y)} \ \prod_{t=1}^{T} p_t(a_t \mid x) \\ \mathcal{L}_{\text{CTC}} &= -\ln P(y \mid x) \end{aligned} \]

In words: \(\mathcal{B}\) is the collapse rule, so \(\mathcal{B}^{-1}(y)\) is every path that reads as \(y\). Training raises the total probability of all of them, without ever choosing one; the model is free to put the spikes wherever it likes.

The forward algorithm. Enumerating paths explodes: for 100 frames and a 10-token transcript with no repeated neighbours there are \(\binom{110}{20} \approx 4.4 \times 10^{21}\) alignments. Paths share prefixes, though, so dynamic programming works. Write the target, a transcript of \(L\) tokens, with blanks around and between its tokens, \(z = (\_, y_1, \_, y_2, \dots, \_)\), which has \(2L + 1\) states, and let \(\alpha_t(s)\) be the total probability of all path prefixes that are at state \(s\) after frame \(t\):

\[ \alpha_t(s) = \Big(\alpha_{t-1}(s) + \alpha_{t-1}(s-1) + \mathbb{1}[\text{skip allowed}]\ \alpha_{t-1}(s-2)\Big)\ p_t(z_s) \]

In words: you reach state \(s\) by staying (a repeat), by stepping from the previous state, or by jumping over a blank, which is allowed only when \(z_s\) is a real token different from \(z_{s-2}\) (jumping between two identical tokens would merge them). \(P(y \mid x)\) is the sum of the last two states at the last frame: ending on the final token or the final blank. Decoding. Greedy decoding takes the most likely symbol at each frame and collapses: here a, a, _ → “a”. Prefix beam search keeps the best few partial transcripts, merging paths that collapse to the same prefix, and is where an external language model and hotword boosts plug in (below).

Trap

The best path is not the best transcript. Two frames, each with \(P(\text{a}) = 0.4\): greedy decoding picks blank twice and outputs nothing (path probability 0.36), but “a” is reached by three paths (a_, _a, aa) totalling 0.64. Greedy is usually close enough because CTC outputs are so peaky, but prefix beam search, which adds up all the paths that share a prefix, is what searches (approximately) for the transcript with the highest \(P(y \mid x)\).

CTC's key assumption: conditional independence. Each frame's output depends on the audio but not on the other outputs. The model can't learn “after ‘order’, ‘four’ is more likely than ‘for’” inside the output layer; whatever context it uses must come through the encoder's view of the audio. So CTC models benefit a lot from an external language model, and their mistakes look like spelling (“four” → “for”). The other properties follow from the design:

  • Monotonic and length-limited: the output length, plus one blank for each pair of repeated neighbours, can't exceed the number of frames (fine at 25 frames a second after subsampling).
  • Fast: one parallel pass and a collapse, no autoregressive loop, so very cheap at inference.
  • Streams naturally if the encoder only looks at past and current audio: each frame's output can be read as soon as it's computed.

RNN-Transducer: adding a language model inside

The RNN-Transducer (RNN-T, Graves 2012) removes the independence assumption while keeping streaming. It has three parts:

  • The encoder turns audio frames into vectors \(e_t\), as before.
  • The prediction network reads the tokens emitted so far (not the audio) and produces a state \(g_u\). It is effectively a small language model over the output.
  • The joint network combines one encoder frame and one prediction state into a distribution over the vocabulary plus blank.
\[ \begin{aligned} h_{t,u} &= \tanh\big(W_e\, e_t + W_g\, g_u\big) \\ P(k \mid t, u) &= \operatorname{softmax}\big(W_o\, h_{t,u}\big)_k \end{aligned} \]

In words: at frame \(t\), having emitted \(u\) tokens, the model either emits a token \(k\) (then \(u\) advances, the prediction network updates, and it may emit again from the same frame) or emits blank, which means “move to the next frame”. Unlike CTC, several tokens can come out of one frame, and each choice depends on what was already emitted.

  • Training sums over every path through a \(T \times (U+1)\) lattice of (frame, tokens emitted so far) positions, again by dynamic programming (below).
  • Why it's the streaming default: decoding moves left to right, frame by frame, and emits tokens as soon as the encoder has seen enough audio. With a streaming encoder (below), this is the standard design for on-device and real-time production ASR.
  • The cost is training memory. The joint network's output is a 4-D tensor, batch × \(T\) × \((U+1)\) × (vocabulary + 1). For 10 s of audio at 40 ms frames (\(T = 250\)), a 60-token transcript and a 1,024-token vocabulary, that's \(250 \times 61 \times 1{,}025 \approx 15.6\) million logits, 62.5 MB in 32-bit floats for one utterance and 2 GB for a batch of 32. Pruned and fused transducer losses exist to avoid materialising all of it.
  • Variants: the token-and-duration transducer (TDT) also predicts how many frames to skip after each emission, which speeds up decoding.

The forward recursion. Let \(\alpha(t, u)\) be the probability of having emitted the first \(u\) tokens of the transcript by frame \(t\). Write \(\varnothing(t, u)\) for the joint network's blank probability at \((t, u)\), and \(y(t, u)\) for its probability of the next correct token \(y_{u+1}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \alpha(t, u) &= \alpha(t-1, u)\,\varnothing(t-1, u) + \alpha(t, u-1)\,y(t, u-1) \\ P(y \mid x) &= \alpha(T, U)\,\varnothing(T, U) \end{aligned} \]

In words: you reach \((t, u)\) either by a blank at the previous frame (move to the next frame) or by emitting token \(u\) without moving frame, starting from \(\alpha(1, 0) = 1\); every path ends with a final blank. With 3 frames and a one-token transcript there are 3 paths: emit the token at frame 1, 2 or 3, with blanks everywhere else.

Attention encoder–decoder: Whisper

The third family treats ASR like translation: a transformer encoder reads all the audio frames, and an autoregressive decoder generates the transcript one token at a time, attending to the encoder output through cross-attention (the mechanism is in the transformers note). The decoder is a full language model conditioned on the audio, so these models are usually the most accurate and handle punctuation, casing and written-form numbers naturally. Whisper is the best-known example.

  • Input: 16 kHz audio cut into 30-second windows, as log-mel features (80 bands, 128 in large-v3) with 25 ms windows and a 10 ms hop: 80 × 3,000 per window.
  • Encoder: two 1-D convolutions (kernel 3, GELU), the second with stride 2, so 3,000 frames become 1,500 positions of 20 ms each; sinusoidal positions; then transformer blocks.
  • Decoder: a transformer with learned positions that writes byte-level BPE tokens.
  • Multitask through special tokens: the decoder's prompt says what to do, for example <|startoftranscript|> <|hi|> <|transcribe|> <|notimestamps|>: language (or let the model predict it), transcribe or translate into English, with or without timestamp tokens. Previous text can be put before it as a prompt, which carries context and spellings across windows.
  • Training: 680,000 hours of weakly supervised audio with internet transcripts in the original release (later versions used more), which is why it is robust to accents and noise, and also why it sometimes writes text that is not in the audio.
  • Sizes: from tiny (39M parameters) to large (1,550M); “turbo” (809M) is large-v3 with the decoder cut from 32 layers to 4, much faster with a small accuracy loss.

Why vanilla Whisper doesn't stream.

  1. Fixed 30-second input. A 2-second utterance is padded to 30 seconds, and the encoder does the full 30 seconds of work.
  2. Whole-window attention. The encoder attends in both directions over the window and the decoder starts only after the encoder is done, so nothing can be emitted until the audio has ended.
  3. Unstable on partial audio. It was trained on complete segments; transcribe a growing buffer every half second and the last words keep changing.
  4. Hallucination on silence and noise. Given no speech, it can repeat phrases or produce subtitle-style text, so it must be gated by a VAD.

Workarounds exist: re-transcribe a growing buffer and commit only the prefix that two consecutive runs agree on (the LocalAgreement policy of the whisper_streaming project), or cut utterances with a VAD and transcribe each one after the caller stops. Both cost latency: the first repeats work and commits words late, the second adds the whole transcription time after the end of every turn. That's why live agents usually run streaming CTC or transducer models, and use Whisper-style models for offline transcription, analytics and labelling.

Conformer encoders

Most current ASR encoders are Conformers (Gulati et al., 2020): transformer blocks with a convolution module added, because speech needs both kinds of context. Self-attention captures long-range context (the sentence, the topic); convolution captures local patterns over tens of milliseconds (the transitions between sounds).

Each block runs, in order: a half-step feed-forward layer; multi-head self-attention with relative positions; a convolution module (pointwise convolution with a gated linear unit, a depthwise convolution, batch norm, Swish, another pointwise convolution); a second half-step feed-forward layer; and a final layer norm. The two half-step feed-forward layers sandwiching the middle are called the “macaron” layout.

A convolutional front end first subsamples the 10 ms frames by 4× (40 ms per position); FastConformer variants subsample 8× (80 ms), which cuts the quadratic attention cost by about 4×. The paper reported 2.1% / 4.3% WER on LibriSpeech test-clean / test-other without a language model. The same encoder can feed a CTC head, a transducer, an attention decoder, or a CTC and an attention head trained together.

Self-supervised pretraining: wav2vec 2.0 and HuBERT

Transcribed speech is expensive, and for most Indian languages there is little of it, while untranscribed audio is plentiful. Self-supervised models pretrain the encoder on raw audio alone, the way BERT pretrains on unlabelled text, and are then fine-tuned for ASR with a CTC head on a small labelled set.

  • wav2vec 2.0 (Baevski et al., 2020): a convolutional encoder turns the waveform into a latent vector every 20 ms, and spans of these are masked before a transformer builds context over them. At each masked position the model must pick the true latent (in a quantised form learned at the same time) out of distractors taken from other positions: a contrastive loss. Its authors report 4.8% / 8.2% WER on LibriSpeech test-clean / test-other after pretraining on 53,000 hours of unlabelled audio and fine-tuning on just ten minutes of labels (1.8% / 3.3% with all 960 labelled hours).
  • HuBERT (Hsu et al., 2021): cluster frames offline with k-means (on MFCCs at first) so every frame gets a pseudo-label, then train BERT-style to predict the cluster IDs of masked frames; re-cluster on the model's own features and train again. It works because the clusters are consistent, even though they aren't phonemes.
  • Why it matters for agents: multilingual versions pretrained on many languages are the usual starting point for low-resource and Indic ASR, and the same encoders give features for speaker verification, emotion and turn detection.

Streaming ASR: partials, finals and chunks

A streaming recogniser sends two kinds of result while the caller speaks:

  • Partial (interim) hypotheses every 100–300 ms or so. They change as more audio arrives: “can sell” becomes “cancel order” once the next word gives context. Useful for live captions, for starting the LLM early, and for spotting the caller starting to speak over the agent.
  • Final hypotheses, committed when the recogniser decides a segment is over (often on its own silence detection). They won't change. Tool arguments and the LLM's real input should come from finals.

How the encoder streams. Full self-attention needs the future, so streaming encoders use chunked attention: audio is processed in chunks, and each frame attends to its own chunk plus a cache of past chunks, and possibly a small right-context lookahead. The chunk size sets a floor on latency, because a word spoken at a random point in a chunk waits for the chunk to close:

Smaller chunks mean less future context and usually a higher WER, so streaming models are typically somewhat less accurate than the same model run on whole utterances. Models trained with randomly varied chunk sizes can run at several latency settings.

Language models and contextual biasing

Voice agents live or die on words the recogniser rarely saw in training: customers' names, product and plan names, medicine names, Indian place names, order IDs. Two tools help.

Shallow fusion with a language model. During beam search, add a text language model's opinion to the acoustic score:

\[ \text{score}(y) = \log P_{\text{ASR}}(y \mid x) + \lambda \log P_{\text{LM}}(y) + \beta \lvert y \rvert \]

In words: \(\lambda\) sets how much to trust the language model, and the length bonus \(\beta\) per token stops it from favouring short outputs (every extra token lowers \(\log P_{\text{LM}}\)). Both are tuned on a development set. A small n-gram model (see the n-gram language model section) trained on your own call transcripts is the classic choice for CTC beam search.

Contextual biasing (hotwords, keyword boosting, custom vocabulary). Give the recogniser a list of phrases to favour, and during beam search add a bonus to any hypothesis that is partway through one of them (the list is stored as a prefix tree, so partial matches can be tracked; the bonus is taken back if the phrase isn't completed). Hosted APIs expose this as keywords, phrase hints or custom vocabulary with boost weights; with Whisper, you put the terms in the prompt.

  • Make it per call: the caller's name and their open order IDs from the CRM are the most valuable list you have.
  • Don't over-boost: too much bonus makes the recogniser hear the boosted words everywhere (false insertions). Measure both recall of the boosted terms and WER on everything else, on test sets with and without the terms.
  • After recognition: match entities against the database with fuzzy or phonetic matching (an order ID one digit off, a name spelled differently), and confirm with the caller.
  • When hints aren't enough: fine-tune on in-domain audio.

Inverse text normalisation

Many recognisers, especially CTC and transducer models trained on normalised text, output the spoken form: “cancel order four seven two nine and refund twenty five hundred rupees”. Downstream code needs the written form: order 4729, ₹2,500. Converting is inverse text normalisation (ITN), done with hand-written grammars compiled to weighted finite-state transducers, or with a neural or LLM rewriter.

  • Categories: cardinals, ordinals, money, dates, times, phone numbers, emails (“ravi at gmail dot com”), units.
  • Ambiguity needs context: “one oh one” could be the number 101 or the code 1-0-1; “four seven two nine” is an identifier, written 4729 with no thousands separator, while “four thousand seven hundred twenty nine” is the quantity 4,729.
  • Indian numbers: lakh and crore grouping (“do lakh” = 2,00,000), and Hindi fractions such as “dhai sau” (250) or “saade teen” (3.5).
  • Where it matters: tool arguments (send 4729 to the order API), WER scoring (both sides must use the same form, section 04), and display. LLMs read either form fine.

Its mirror image, text normalisation for TTS, is in section 07.

Code-switching, accents and noisy calls

Code-switching is changing language mid-conversation or mid-sentence, as in Hinglish: “mera order cancel karna hai, number hai four seven two nine”. It is the norm in many Indian support calls, and it breaks several assumptions:

  • One language per utterance: language ID at the start of a segment, or a language chosen up front, can't follow a sentence that switches twice.
  • Script: the same speech can be written with Hindi words in Devanagari and English in Latin, all romanised, or with English words transliterated into Devanagari (ऑर्डर for “order”). If references and model outputs use different conventions, WER counts script choices as errors.
  • Numbers in either language: “chaar saat do nau” and “four seven two nine” are the same order.
  • Data: mixed speech is scarcer than either language alone.

What helps: models trained on code-mixed audio with a shared vocabulary over both scripts (Indic-specialised ASR models exist for this; detecting the language first and routing to a per-language model works for monolingual callers but not for mid-sentence switches), a fixed output convention, and scoring after transliterating reference and hypothesis into one script. Report errors on the English and Hindi parts separately, since one can hide the other.

Accents and channel noise. Measure WER per accent or region, per handset type and per noise level, not just overall. Train with augmentation: noise added at signal-to-noise ratios from about 0 to 20 dB, room reverberation (convolving with room impulse responses), speed perturbation (0.9×, 1.0×, 1.1×), simulated 8 kHz telephony, and SpecAugment, which masks random bands of frequencies and spans of time in the features. A noise-suppression front end makes audio nicer for humans but can add artifacts that raise WER, so test it rather than assume it helps.

Fine-tuning ASR on your own calls

When biasing and normalisation aren't enough, adapt the model to your channel, accents and vocabulary.

  1. Collect consented call audio, ideally the caller's channel on its own, cut into utterances with the VAD.
  2. Transcribe cheaply: pseudo-label with the largest model you have, then have people correct a subset, prioritising entity-heavy and low-confidence utterances. Write transcription guidelines first: how to write numbers, which script to use for code-switched words, what to do with fillers and noise.
  3. Split honestly: by caller and by call, never by utterance, or the same voices land in both train and test (see honest splits).
  4. Train gently: start from a pretrained checkpoint with a low learning rate; freeze the lower layers or train small adapters (LoRA); mix in general-domain data so the model doesn't forget everything else; add phone-channel and noise augmentation.
  5. Evaluate and ship carefully: WER and entity accuracy per slice against the current model on the same held-out calls, then run the new model in shadow mode on live traffic before switching (see shadow mode).

Diarization: who spoke when

In a live two-party call the agent knows which audio is its own, so the real-time loop doesn't need diarization. It matters for mono recordings (QA, analytics, mining old human-agent calls for training data) and for calls with more than one person on the caller's side.

  1. Find speech with a VAD.
  2. Embed: cut speech into short overlapping windows (around 1–2 s) and compute a speaker embedding for each (x-vector or ECAPA-TDNN style networks trained for speaker verification).
  3. Cluster the embeddings (agglomerative or spectral clustering; the number of speakers is often unknown, so a distance threshold or the eigenvalue gap decides it).
  4. Resegment boundaries and handle overlapping speech, which clustering alone can't. End-to-end neural diarization predicts per-frame activity for each speaker directly and handles overlap; pyannote is the common open-source toolkit.

The metric is the diarization error rate: (false-alarm speech + missed speech + speech attributed to the wrong speaker) ÷ total speech time. The cheapest fix of all: when you control the telephony, record each party on its own channel, and diarization becomes trivial.

Say it in an interview: “ASR has to align about 100 frames a second to a few words a second without labelled timing. CTC adds a blank, collapses repeats and sums over all alignments with a forward algorithm, but assumes outputs are independent given the audio. A transducer adds a prediction network over previous tokens and a joint network, which makes it the usual streaming model. Whisper is an encoder–decoder on 30-second windows: very accurate, but it needs the whole window, so it doesn't stream as built. For an agent I'd add per-call hotword biasing, inverse text normalisation for numbers, and evaluation on code-switched 8 kHz audio.”

04Measuring ASR: WER and what it misses

Word error rate counts the fewest word edits that turn the recogniser's output (the hypothesis) into the correct transcript (the reference): substitutions S (wrong word), deletions D (missed word) and insertions I (extra word), divided by the number of reference words N:

\[ \text{WER} = \frac{S + D + I}{N} \]

In words: errors per reference word. Lower is better. Because errors can outnumber the words, 1 − WER isn't an accuracy and can go negative.

\[ d_{i,j} = \min\big(d_{i-1,j-1} + \mathbb{1}[r_i \ne h_j],\ \ d_{i-1,j} + 1,\ \ d_{i,j-1} + 1\big) \]

In words: match or substitute (diagonal, free if the words agree), delete a reference word (down), or insert a hypothesis word (right). The first row and column count pure insertions and deletions.

In words: half an error per reference word. And the one thing the call needed, the order number 4729, came out as “for seven nine”.

  • Ties: several alignments can reach the same minimum; the total is fixed but the S/D/I split can differ between tools.
  • Cost: the table is \(O(N \times M)\) for N reference and M hypothesis words.

Why WER can exceed 100%

Substitutions and deletions are capped by N, but insertions are not. Reference “no”, hypothesis “uh no no thanks”: three insertions over one reference word is a WER of 300%. This happens in practice when a model hallucinates on silence or music, or a VAD lets line noise through. On segments whose reference is empty, WER is undefined, so count insertions there separately.

Normalise before you score

Reference “cancel order 4729”, hypothesis “cancel order four seven two nine”: the recognition is perfect, but raw WER is one substitution plus three insertions over three words, 133%. Formatting conventions differ between transcribers, vendors and models, so both sides must pass through the same normaliser first: lower-case, strip punctuation, write numbers one way, unify spelling variants (“colour” and “color”), expand contractions, and usually drop fillers (“uh”, “um”). Whisper's English text normaliser is a common reference. Always say which normaliser a number used; two vendors' WERs computed with different normalisers can't be compared. For Hinglish, transliterate both sides into one script first.

Character error rate

CER is the same formula over characters. Use it for languages written without spaces (Chinese, Japanese, Thai), where “words” depend on a segmenter; for morphologically rich languages (Tamil, Telugu, Malayalam), where one long word carries several meanings and a wrong suffix makes the whole word wrong; and when near misses should count as near. “four” → “for” is one wrong word (WER 100% on that word) but one missing character out of four (CER 25%).

Corpus WER, not the average of utterance WERs

Over a test set, add up all edits and divide by all reference words. Averaging per-utterance WERs over-weights short utterances: one error in a 2-word utterance (50%) and two in a 40-word one (5%) average to 27.5%, while the corpus WER is 3/42 = 7.1%.

  • Uncertainty: errors within an utterance (and a speaker) are correlated, so get a confidence interval by resampling whole utterances or speakers with the bootstrap (see the bootstrap section), and compare two systems on the same utterances, paired.
  • Slices: report WER per accent, channel, noise level and language mix; an overall improvement can hide a regression on the callers who matter.

Entity accuracy: why 5% WER can miss the phone number

WER weighs every word equally: “uh” counts as much as “not”, and one digit counts as much as “the”. Take “my number is nine eight seven six five four three two one zero please call me back after six today”: 20 words. Mishear one digit, and the WER is 1/20 = 5%, which sounds excellent, yet the phone number is wrong and the callback fails. Digit accuracy is 90%; whole-number accuracy is 0.

Long entities are fragile because every part must be right. If each digit is independently right with probability \(p\), a 10-digit number is fully right with probability \(p^{10}\):

Per-digit accuracyWhole 10-digit number right

Meaning flips are the same problem: “I do not want to cancel” heard as “I do want to cancel” is one deletion, a WER of 16.7%, and the opposite instruction. So alongside WER, track:

  • Entity exact-match rate per type: phone numbers, order IDs, amounts, dates, names, addresses.
  • Keyword recall and precision for the domain terms and boosted words.
  • Slot accuracy and task success downstream, which is what the business feels (see intents and slots and conversation metrics).

And design around it: read critical entities back to the caller, take long numbers by keypad when speech keeps failing (section 10), and validate against what the system knows (a check digit, an order ID that exists for this caller). Use the recogniser's word confidences and its N-best list of alternative transcripts: a low-confidence digit or name should trigger a read-back rather than a silent guess (when to confirm is a dialogue-policy decision).

Speed: real-time factor and latency of finals

  • Real-time factor (RTF) = processing time ÷ audio duration. RTF 0.1 means 10 s of audio takes 1 s. Some leaderboards (such as the Open ASR Leaderboard) report the inverse, RTFx, where bigger is faster. It measures throughput, not how quickly a streaming system responds.
  • Streaming latency: partial latency (from a word being spoken to it first appearing), final latency (from the end of speech to the committed transcript), and stability (how often partials get rewritten). Report p50 and p95 (or p99) under realistic load, not means.
  • Keep the endpointing wait separate: how long the system waits to decide the caller has finished is a turn-taking setting (section 05), not recogniser speed, though vendors sometimes bundle the two into one “latency” number.

Say it in an interview: “WER is substitutions plus deletions plus insertions over reference words, from an edit-distance alignment. It can pass 100% because insertions are unbounded. I'd normalise both sides the same way, compute it over the corpus with bootstrap intervals by utterance, and slice it by accent and channel. But WER treats every word equally, so one wrong digit in a 20-word sentence is 5% WER and a failed callback; for an agent I'd also track entity exact-match rates and task success.”

05Voice activity detection and endpointing

A voice activity detector (VAD) labels each short frame of audio, typically 10–30 ms, as speech or not. A voice agent uses it four ways: to detect that the caller has started speaking (including over the agent, which is barge-in), to detect that they've stopped (endpointing), to avoid sending silence to the recogniser (cost, and Whisper-style hallucinations), and to cut audio into utterances for models that need whole segments.

Energy-based VAD

The simplest detector measures each frame's energy, \(E = 10 \log_{10}\big(\tfrac{1}{n}\sum x_i^2\big)\) in decibels, and calls the frame speech if it is some margin (say 10–15 dB) above a running estimate of the noise floor. A zero-crossing rate (how often the waveform changes sign) helps catch hissy consonants like “s”, which are high-frequency but quiet.

It is nearly free, and it fails in exactly the conditions phone calls have: a TV or other people talking in the background, traffic, wind, breathing on the microphone, line clicks, music on hold, a quiet caller, or automatic gain control that keeps changing the levels. Loud non-speech becomes “speech”, and soft speech becomes “silence”.

Model-based VAD

  • WebRTC VAD: the classic detector from the WebRTC project, a Gaussian mixture model over energies in a few frequency bands. It takes 10, 20 or 30 ms frames of 16-bit audio at 8, 16, 32 or 48 kHz, with an aggressiveness setting from 0 to 3 (3 filters out the most non-speech). Very fast, but noise often fools it.
  • Silero VAD: a small neural network (about 2 MB) for 8 or 16 kHz audio, scoring chunks of 512 samples at 16 kHz (32 ms) with a speech probability, in under a millisecond per chunk on one CPU thread. A common default in agent frameworks.
  • Others: segmentation models from diarization toolkits, and the recogniser's own endpointer, which many streaming ASR APIs expose as an end-of-utterance flag.

Choose by measuring, on your own call audio, the two errors that matter: speech missed (the start of a caller's turn clipped, a barge-in not noticed) and noise accepted (a cough or a TV that stops the agent mid-sentence).

From frame scores to segments: smoothing

Raw frame decisions flicker. Four rules turn them into stable segments:

  • Hysteresis: two thresholds. Enter speech when the score rises above one (Silero's default is 0.5), and leave only when it falls below a lower one (its default is 0.15 lower, 0.35), so a score hovering near a single threshold doesn't toggle every frame.
  • Minimum speech duration: ignore bursts shorter than, say, 250 ms (Silero's default for its offline segmenter): clicks, pops, a cough.
  • Hangover (minimum silence duration): stay in speech until silence has lasted H ms. This bridges the short pauses inside a turn, and it is exactly the endpointing wait.
  • Padding: keep a few tens of milliseconds on each side of a segment so the recogniser doesn't lose the edges of the first and last words.

Endpointing: the silence-threshold trade-off

With VAD alone, the caller's turn ends when silence lasts a threshold \(T\). That one number sets two failures against each other:

  • Too long, and every reply is slow. All of \(T\) is added to every turn's latency, before any model starts.
  • Too short, and callers get cut off. People pause mid-turn to think, to breathe, to read a number off a card. The agent then replies to half a sentence, and the caller talks over the reply.

A model to see the shape (illustrative assumptions, not measured data). Say an ordinary turn contains on average 1.5 internal pauses (Poisson), each log-normal with a median of 300 ms (90th percentile about 650 ms). When reading out a phone number, there are on average 3 pauses with a median of 500 ms and the same spread (90th percentile about 1,080 ms). A turn is cut off if any pause reaches \(T\). With pauses arriving as a Poisson count with mean \(\lambda\):

\[ P(\text{cut off}) = 1 - e^{-\lambda\, P(\text{pause} \ge T)} \]

In words: each pause is a chance to be cut off; the expected number of pauses longer than \(T\) is \(\lambda P(\text{pause} \ge T)\), and the turn survives only if that count is zero.

Cut-offs fall steeply and then flatten while latency grows linearly, and no single threshold suits both chit-chat and dictation: one that is fine for ordinary turns cuts off half the phone numbers. VAD-only setups in agent frameworks typically wait around half a second to a second (Pipecat's VAD default stop time is 0.8 s). The fixes:

  • Make the wait depend on context: after asking for a phone number, wait longer, count digits, or switch to keypad entry.
  • Use a turn detector that looks at what was said, not just the silence (next section).
  • Recover from cut-offs: if the caller resumes within a second or so of the agent starting, stop, merge the two fragments into one turn and answer that.

Say it in an interview: “A VAD scores frames as speech or not; energy VADs break on noise, so I'd use a small neural one like Silero, with hysteresis, a minimum speech duration and a hangover. The hangover is the endpointing wait, and it's a straight trade: every millisecond is added to every reply, but short waits cut people off, especially mid-number. So I'd make the wait depend on context and use a turn detector rather than silence alone.”

06Turn-taking, barge-in and echo

In human conversation, gaps between turns are typically a few hundred milliseconds, and across languages speakers avoid both overlapping and long silences (Stivers et al., 2009). People manage this because they predict the end of the other person's turn from its words, grammar and intonation, and start planning a reply before it ends. A silence-only agent can't start until the silence has lasted \(T\). Turn-taking is the engineering of everything around that: when to speak, when to stop, and what to remember when interrupted.

End-of-turn detection beyond silence

Signals that a turn is over or not:

  • Words: “I want to cancel my order.” is complete. “My number is nine eight seven six five” is clearly not, and neither is a sentence ending in “and” or “because”.
  • Prosody: falling pitch and a lengthened last syllable mark an end; level or rising pitch and filled pauses (“uh”, “matlab”) mark a continuation.
  • Dialogue context: if the agent just asked for a 10-digit number, five digits is not an answer yet.

Turn detector models use these signals to predict \(P(\text{end of turn})\) whenever the VAD sees a short pause:

  • Text-based: a small language model fine-tuned on transcripts to predict whether the user is done, given the last few turns. LiveKit's turn detector is an example (its first version was a 135M-parameter transformer based on SmolLM v2).
  • Audio-based: a classifier over the last few seconds of waveform, which hears intonation as well as words. Pipecat's Smart Turn is an example (a Whisper-tiny encoder with a small classification head, about 8M parameters).
  • The LLM itself: ask it to output a special “wait” token when the user sounds unfinished. Accurate, but it spends a full LLM call and its latency on the decision.

A typical policy: on a short pause (around 200 ms of VAD silence), run the turn model. If it's confident the turn is over, reply now. If not, keep waiting, re-check as more audio or words arrive, and reply anyway after a maximum timeout (a few seconds), or gently prompt. The wait becomes short when the turn is clearly complete and long when it clearly isn't, so the detector cuts latency and cut-offs at the same time, which a fixed threshold can't.

Backchannels

Listeners make short noises that don't claim the turn: “mm-hmm”, “okay”, “right”, “haan”, “achha”. These are backchannels. If the caller says “haan” while the agent is explaining a refund, the agent should keep talking; stopping for every “mm-hmm” makes it feel brittle. Ways to tell a backchannel from a real interruption: duration (backchannels are short, under about half a second), the words (a per-language list of backchannel tokens), and whether more speech follows. Agents can also backchannel while the caller gives a long explanation, but in a half-duplex cascade that risks talking over them, so it's mostly limited to an acknowledgement once they finish.

Barge-in: when the caller interrupts

Barge-in is the caller speaking while the agent is still talking. Running example: the agent is saying “Your refund of two thousand rupees will reach your account in five to seven working days.” (16 words, about 6.4 s at 150 words a minute), and 2.4 s in, the caller says “wait, I paid by card”. Handling it takes five steps:

  1. Detect a real interruption. Run the VAD on the caller's echo-cancelled audio during playback, and require more than a blip: a minimum duration (often a few hundred milliseconds), or one or two recognised words, or an interruption classifier, so coughs, line noise and backchannels don't stop the agent.
  2. Stop the audio at once, everywhere. TTS generates faster than real time, so seconds of reply may already be queued: in the orchestrator, in the TTS stream, in the telephony provider's buffer, in the client's jitter buffer (the short playback queue that smooths out unevenly arriving packets, section 10). Flush them all; Twilio's Media Streams, for example, have a clear message that empties audio already sent. Stopping only the generator leaves the caller listening to the agent talk over them.
  3. Cancel work in flight: the LLM generation and pending TTS requests. Don't blindly cancel side-effecting tool calls (a payment halfway through); those need idempotency and a defined outcome.
  4. Trim the agent's message in the history to what was actually heard. Track playback position, from the transport's playback acknowledgements (Twilio's mark messages, for example) and the TTS's word timestamps. At 2.4 s the caller had heard “Your refund of two thousand rupees”, so that, marked as interrupted, is what goes into the history. Hosted realtime speech APIs offer the same operation (OpenAI's Realtime API has a truncate event that takes the played audio's end time).
  5. Handle the caller's turn normally. If it turns out to have been noise, continue or rephrase (“Sorry, go ahead?”) rather than restarting the whole reply.

Trap

Skipping step 4. If the history keeps the full reply, the LLM believes the caller heard “five to seven working days”, so it won't repeat it, and may later say “as I mentioned, five to seven days”. The caller never heard it. And measure the playback position at the caller's end, not the bytes you've sent: generation runs ahead of playback, so “sent” over-counts what was heard, often by seconds.

Some segments shouldn't be interruptible: a legally required disclosure, a consent statement, the confirmation of a payment. Keep listening during them, but finish the segment before responding.

Echo cancellation, and why barge-in fails without it

The agent's own voice comes back on the caller's channel: as acoustic echo when the caller uses a speakerphone or laptop speakers and the microphone picks up the playback, and as line echo from impedance mismatches in parts of the phone network. The echo is delayed (tens to hundreds of milliseconds) and filtered by the room or line.

  • What breaks: the VAD hears speech during playback, so the agent “barges in” on itself and stops mid-sentence; or the recogniser transcribes the agent's words as the caller's (“user: your refund of two thousand rupees”), and the LLM answers its own echo.
  • Acoustic echo cancellation (AEC): an adaptive filter (for example NLMS, normalised least mean squares) learns the echo path from the reference signal, the exact audio that was played, predicts the echo in the microphone signal and subtracts it. A nonlinear suppressor removes what remains, and double-talk detection freezes the filter's learning while both people speak, so the caller's voice doesn't corrupt it.
  • Where it runs: best on the device that has both the speaker and the microphone. Browsers and WebRTC stacks include AEC; phones and carriers cancel echo too, imperfectly. Server-side AEC is possible since you have the reference, but the echo path then includes variable network delay, which makes it harder.
  • Fallbacks when it isn't good enough: raise the barge-in threshold during playback, ignore “user” transcripts that match what the agent just said, or turn barge-in off for that call.

Half duplex and full duplex

A cascade with barge-in is half duplex: one side speaks at a time, and interruption is a rule in the orchestrator. A full-duplex model such as Moshi (section 02) takes in the caller's audio and produces its own at every step, so overlap, backchannels and yielding the floor are learned behaviour rather than rules. That is more natural, but harder to control (when should it stay silent, and can it be guaranteed to finish a disclosure?), and it still needs echo cancellation on the device.

Say it in an interview: “I'd detect end of turn with a VAD plus a turn model that looks at the words and intonation, so complete turns get a short wait and unfinished ones a long one. For barge-in: confirm it's a real interruption and not a backchannel, flush every audio buffer down to the phone, cancel the LLM and TTS, and trim the agent's message in the history to what was actually played, using playback marks and word timestamps. None of that works without echo cancellation, or the agent interrupts itself.”

07Speech synthesis (TTS)

Text-to-speech turns the agent's reply into audio. The classic pipeline has four steps (text → normalised text → phonemes → mel spectrogram → waveform), and newer models merge them. In a voice agent, TTS also has to stream, pronounce numbers and names correctly, and report what it has said so far.

Text normalisation and grapheme-to-phoneme

Text normalisation (TN) rewrites written text as words to be spoken, the reverse of ASR's inverse normalisation:

  • Money and numbers: “₹2,500” → “two thousand five hundred rupees” (or “do hazaar paanch sau rupaye” for a Hindi voice); “₹2,50,000” → “two lakh fifty thousand rupees”.
  • Identifiers vs amounts: order 4729 should be read “four seven two nine”, not “four thousand seven hundred and twenty-nine”; a phone number in groups with pauses (“nine eight seven six five, four three two one zero”).
  • Dates and abbreviations: “12/10” is 12 October in India and December 10 in the US; “Dr.” is doctor or drive; “EMI” and “KYC” are spelled out letter by letter; times, units, URLs and emails all need rules.

In an agent, the most reliable approach is to have the LLM write in spoken form (“four seven two nine”) and also run a deterministic normaliser you control and test, because a wrong reading of an amount is a real error, not a style issue.

Grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) converts words to phonemes: a pronunciation dictionary lookup (such as CMUdict for English), with a neural G2P model for words not in it. Heteronyms need context (“read” present or past, “lead” the metal or the verb). Names are the usual failure, especially Indian names in an English voice, so production systems keep a custom lexicon or phoneme overrides. Many end-to-end models read characters directly and learn pronunciation implicitly, which removes G2P errors on common words but makes a specific mispronunciation harder to fix.

Acoustic model and vocoder

Predicting 22,000–24,000 samples a second directly from text is hard, so the classic design splits it in two. An acoustic model predicts a mel spectrogram (about 80 numbers every ~10 ms, smooth and compact), and a vocoder turns the mel spectrogram into a waveform.

  • Tacotron 2 is a sequence-to-sequence model with attention that generates mel frames one at a time from characters, followed by a WaveNet vocoder. It sounds natural, but it is slow, and its attention can fail: skipping or repeating words, or mumbling on long or unusual inputs.
  • FastSpeech 2 is non-autoregressive. An encoder reads the phonemes; a variance adaptor predicts each phoneme's duration (in frames), pitch and energy; a length regulator repeats each phoneme's vector for its duration; and a decoder produces all frames in parallel. Duration targets come from a forced aligner during training. No attention means no skipped words; parallel output is fast; and durations and pitch are explicit, so speed and intonation are controllable.

  • Vocoders. WaveNet generated audio one sample at a time: excellent, far too slow. HiFi-GAN is a GAN: a generator upsamples each mel frame to its 256 samples with transposed convolutions, and discriminators that look at the waveform at several periods and several time scales push it towards realistic pitch periodicity and texture. Its authors report generating 22 kHz audio about 168× faster than real time on a V100 GPU, and a small version about 13× faster than real time on CPU.

End-to-end: VITS

VITS (Kim et al., 2021) goes from text to waveform in one model, trained jointly: a conditional variational autoencoder whose prior is made flexible with a normalising flow, a HiFi-GAN-style decoder trained adversarially, an alignment between text and frames found during training (monotonic alignment search, so no external aligner), and a stochastic duration predictor, so the same sentence can be spoken with different rhythms. Its authors report MOS comparable to the real recordings on a single-speaker dataset, and many open TTS voices are built on it.

Codec language-model TTS

The newest family treats TTS as language modelling over the neural codec tokens of section 02. VALL-E (Wang et al., 2023) conditions on the text's phonemes and the codec tokens of a 3-second recording of the target speaker; an autoregressive transformer predicts the first codebook's tokens, a non-autoregressive one fills in the remaining codebooks, and the codec decoder produces audio. It clones a voice zero-shot, including its emotion and acoustic environment.

  • Strengths: natural, expressive, few-second voice cloning, and it scales with data like an LLM.
  • Weaknesses: sampling makes output vary between runs, and like an LLM it can hallucinate: skip, repeat or garble words. It's also slower than parallel models. Many recent systems combine a token language model with a parallel (for example flow-matching) decoder to keep the expressiveness and reduce these problems.

Streaming TTS

An agent can't wait for the LLM's whole reply. The orchestrator collects tokens until a phrase boundary (end of sentence, or a comma once the phrase has a few words), sends that phrase to TTS, and streams the audio chunks to the caller as they're produced; meanwhile the next phrase is being generated and synthesised. The numbers:

  • Waiting for the whole LLM reply: 60 tokens at 100 tokens a second is 600 ms after the first token. The first 8-token phrase is ready after 80 ms, as in the budget of section 09.
  • Non-streaming synthesis: a 45-word reply is 18 s of audio; even at RTF 0.05 that's 900 ms before any sound. Streaming TTS typically produces its first audio in roughly 100–200 ms (rule of thumb).

The trade-off is prosody. A TTS model given a fragment doesn't know what comes next: it can't tell a question is coming until it sees the “?”, it tends to give each fragment a sentence-final fall, and joins between chunks can be audible. Common fixes: keep the first phrase short for latency and later ones sentence-sized for quality, and use TTS APIs that accept text incrementally over one connection with a context id, so they carry prosody across chunks. Ask the TTS for word-level timestamps too; barge-in needs them to know what was heard. And since spoken audio can't be taken back, run output checks on each phrase before it goes to TTS, not on the whole reply afterwards (see guardrails).

Voice cloning, consent and safety

A few seconds of someone's voice is now enough to clone it. That enables impersonation scams (a fake relative or bank officer), attacks on voice-biometric authentication, and voices used without permission. Practices for a voice-agent product:

  • Consent: clone a voice only with the speaker's recorded, verifiable consent, and keep the record; restrict who can create voices.
  • Watermarking: embed an inaudible, detectable mark in generated audio so it can be identified later.
  • Disclosure: say the caller is talking to an AI where law or policy requires it, and never instruct the agent to claim to be human.
  • Authentication: don't rely on voice alone to verify a customer when voices can be cloned.

Prosody, SSML and pronunciation

Prosody is the rhythm, stress, pitch and pausing of speech. SSML (Speech Synthesis Markup Language, a W3C standard) lets you control it with tags: <break time="300ms"/> for a pause, <say-as interpret-as="telephone"> or interpret-as="characters" for numbers and acronyms, <prosody rate="90%"> to slow down, <phoneme> to force a pronunciation, <sub alias="…"> to substitute a spoken form. Vendor support varies, and some newer TTS models take natural-language style instructions or inline tags instead. For an agent: slow down and group digits when reading numbers back, pause briefly before important information, and keep a lexicon so brand and product names are always said the same way.

Say it in an interview: “TTS is text normalisation, phonemes, an acoustic model to a mel spectrogram, and a vocoder to the waveform: FastSpeech 2 predicts durations and generates frames in parallel, HiFi-GAN is a fast GAN vocoder, VITS does it end to end, and codec language models like VALL-E clone voices from seconds of audio but can skip or repeat words. In an agent I'd stream it phrase by phrase, normalise numbers myself, get word timestamps for barge-in, and only clone voices with consent.”

08Measuring TTS: MOS and beyond

The mean opinion score (MOS) is the standard listening test: listeners hear each clip and rate it on a 1–5 scale (5 excellent, 4 good, 3 fair, 2 poor, 1 bad: the absolute category rating scale from ITU-T P.800), and MOS is the average. Variants ask about naturalness, overall quality, or similarity to a target speaker (SMOS).

How noisy a MOS is

Small test. Ten ratings: 4, 5, 4, 3, 4, 5, 4, 4, 3, 4. Their mean is 4.00 and their standard deviation 0.667. With so few ratings, use the t distribution (9 degrees of freedom, critical value 2.262; see z or t):

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{CI}_{95} &= \bar r \pm t_{0.975,\,n-1}\,\frac{s}{\sqrt n} \\ &= 4.00 \pm 2.262 \times \frac{0.667}{\sqrt{10}} \\ &= 4.00 \pm 0.48 = [3.52,\ 4.48] \end{aligned} \]

In words: with ten ratings the true MOS could be anywhere from about 3.5 (between fair and good) to about 4.5 (between good and excellent).

A realistic test, and why the naive interval is too narrow. 20 listeners each rate the same 10 sentences, 200 ratings. Treating them as 200 independent ratings gives a tight interval. But they aren't independent: some listeners are harsh and some lenient, and some sentences are harder to say well. Simulate 200 such tests, with listener offsets of standard deviation 0.5, sentence offsets of 0.3 and rating noise of 0.6 (assumptions for illustration), rounded to the 1–5 scale, and compare each method's interval with how much the test's MOS really varies from one test to the next:

Truth: 1.96 × the spread of MOS across the 200 tests±0.28

Only the two-way bootstrap matches the truth; the naive interval is about 2.5 times too narrow (2.3 to 2.8 times across the simulated tests), because the result has to generalise to new listeners and new sentences, not just new ratings. Three consequences:

  • Report intervals that resample listeners and sentences (or fit a mixed model with both as random effects).
  • Small differences need big tests. With a rating standard deviation of 0.9, telling 4.1 from 4.2 needs about \(16\sigma^2/\delta^2 = 16 \times 0.81 / 0.01 \approx 1{,}300\) ratings per system if they were independent (the sample-size rule from the power section), and more when listeners and sentences correlate them; a difference of 0.3 needs about 144. Paired comparisons (below) need far fewer.
  • MOS from different tests isn't comparable. Scores depend on the listener pool, the instructions and, strongly, on which other systems are in the same test. A paper's “4.3” and your vendor's “4.3” aren't the same number.

Comparative tests: CMOS, preference and MUSHRA

  • CMOS (comparative MOS): listeners hear A and B saying the same sentence and rate B relative to A from −3 to +3. Pairing removes the listener's and the sentence's offsets, so it detects much smaller differences.
  • AB preference: “which sounds better?” with a no-preference option, analysed with a sign or binomial test.
  • MUSHRA (ITU-R BS.1534): for each sentence the listener sees all systems side by side, plus a hidden reference (the real recording) and a deliberately degraded anchor, and rates each from 0 to 100 on sliders. It ranks several close systems well, but needs careful, preferably trained, listeners; it is common for codecs and high-quality TTS.

Intelligibility: transcribe it back

Run a strong ASR model over the TTS output and compute WER or CER against the input text. This catches skipped, repeated and garbled words, which is exactly where codec language models fail, and it runs on every build without listeners. Normalise both sides, and know the floor: the same ASR on real recordings of the same sentences isn't perfect either. Check entities in particular: do “₹2,500” and “4729” come out as intended?

Speaker similarity

For cloned or branded voices, compute the cosine similarity between speaker embeddings (from a speaker-verification model) of the generated audio and of a real recording of the target speaker. Track it with listener similarity scores, since embeddings can be fooled by matching channel characteristics rather than the voice.

Automatic MOS predictors

Neural networks trained to predict human MOS from audio (MOSNet, UTMOS, NISQA for transmitted speech, DNSMOS for noise suppression) give a cheap quality score for every build and every voice. They correlate reasonably with listeners on the kind of audio they were trained on, drift outside it, and can be gamed by optimising against them, so use them for regression alerts, not launch decisions.

Speed: time to first audio

  • Time to first audio (or first byte), TTFA/TTFB: from sending text to receiving the first playable chunk. This is the TTS's share of the latency budget.
  • Real-time factor: synthesis time ÷ audio duration. A stream must stay comfortably below 1, or playback runs out of audio and stutters (an underrun).
  • Under load: measure p50 and p95 at your expected number of concurrent calls; GPU-backed TTS that is fast for one stream can queue at 200.

09The latency budget

The number that matters is voice-to-voice latency: from the caller's last syllable to the first syllable of the agent's reply, as heard at the caller's end. Humans answer within a few hundred milliseconds. A common goal for agents is around 800 ms to 1 s, and about 1.5 s is still acceptable to many callers (a rule of thumb; one widely read practitioners' guide puts the comfortable limit at about 1,500 ms). Past 2 s or so, callers start saying “hello?” and talking over the reply, which then triggers barge-in and makes things worse.

Orchestration frameworks

  • Pipecat: an open-source Python framework that builds the agent as a pipeline of processors passing frames (audio, text, control): transport → VAD → STT → context → LLM → TTS → transport, with interruption handling, turn detection and plugins for most vendors.
  • LiveKit Agents: an open-source framework in which the agent joins a WebRTC room as a participant (Python or Node), with a turn-detector model, interruption handling and SIP for phone calls.
  • Hosted platforms (Vapi, Retell and similar): configure a prompt, tools, a voice and a phone number, and the platform runs the pipeline. Fastest to launch, but less control over turn-taking, latency internals and where data goes, and a per-minute fee on top of the model costs.

Build vs buy turns on how much you need to control: custom turn-taking, a specific Indic ASR, data residency and latency tuning push towards a framework you run yourself.

Prompting for voice

A chat prompt read aloud makes a bad voice agent. Voice-specific rules, each with its reason:

  • Short sentences, one question per turn. Listeners can't scroll back, and they hold only a few items in memory.
  • No markdown, lists, emojis, URLs or tables. TTS reads them literally, skips them or sounds robotic. Ask for a reply that sounds natural spoken.
  • Write numbers as they should be said: “four seven two nine”, “two thousand five hundred rupees”, “the twelfth of October”, and still run a deterministic normaliser after the LLM.
  • Read critical entities back, in groups, and confirm before any irreversible action: “That's nine eight seven six five, four three two one zero. Is that right?”
  • Tell the model its input is a speech transcript: it may contain misheard words (“for” for “four”), so ask a clarifying question rather than guess, and never act on a single hearing of a critical entity.
  • Match the caller's language and register: reply in Hinglish to Hinglish, use polite forms, don't switch language unprompted.
  • Lead with the answer, keep the first sentence short (latency, section 09).
  • Handle the voice-only situations: silence (“Are you still there?”), noise, repeated misunderstanding (after two failures offer the keypad or a human).

Keep the structured state (collected slots, verified identity, what has been confirmed) in the orchestrator, not only in the transcript; see dialogue state tracking.

Tool calls during a call

The mechanics are the same as for any LLM agent (see tool calling and structured output). What changes in voice is that silence is dead air:

  • Say something before a slow tool: prompt the LLM to emit a short sentence (“Let me check that order for you.”) before the tool call, or have the orchestrator play a filler if the tool hasn't returned within about a second; for long operations, give short updates every few seconds.
  • Arguments from confirmed entities: pass the normalised, read-back value (4729), not the raw transcript.
  • Idempotency: a barge-in, a retry or a duplicated final transcript must not cancel an order twice or charge twice; give side-effecting calls idempotency keys.
  • Timeouts and fallbacks: decide what the agent says when a tool fails or takes too long, and run independent tools in parallel.

DTMF: the keypad

Pressing a phone key sends dual-tone multi-frequency (DTMF) signalling: two sine tones at once, one from a row group (697, 770, 852, 941 Hz) and one from a column group (1,209, 1,336, 1,477 Hz, plus 1,633 Hz for the A–D keys that ordinary phones lack). “5” is 770 + 1,336 Hz. Providers deliver keypresses either as events (RTP telephone events or SIP INFO), or as tones in the audio, which you detect by measuring energy at the eight frequencies (the Goertzel algorithm does exactly that, cheaply).

  • Use it for: PINs, account and order numbers when speech keeps failing, menu choices (“press 1 for a human”), and consent capture. Keypad entry is exact, and it keeps sensitive numbers out of transcripts and the LLM's context.
  • Outbound agents may also need to send DTMF to navigate other companies' phone menus.

Voicemail and answering-machine detection

On outbound calls, the agent must know whether a person or a machine answered. People usually say something short (“Hello?”) and wait; machines play a long, unbroken greeting and then a beep. Answering-machine detection uses the length and cadence of the greeting, beep detection (a steady tone), classifiers over the first seconds of audio or transcript (“you've reached…”), and carrier tones for unreachable numbers.

The trade-off is time against accuracy. Waiting several seconds to decide means a real person hears silence and may hang up; deciding fast misclassifies, and the agent either leaves a voicemail on a live person or talks to a machine. A common compromise: greet immediately while classifying in parallel; if it's a machine, wait for the beep and leave a short message that meets the applicable rules.

Call transfer

When the agent should hand over (the policy is in human handoff), voice adds the mechanics. A cold transfer redirects the call to a number or queue with a SIP REFER or the provider's API; a warm transfer also hands the human the summary, transcript and collected details first (what to pass is in human handoff), via a screen, SIP headers or the CRM. Tell the caller what is happening, play hold audio, handle queue timeouts (what if no one picks up?), and keep the recording and consent consistent across the transfer.

Privacy, consent and compliance

Calls carry names, phone numbers, account details and, in healthcare, health information, and every stage keeps a copy: recordings, transcripts, LLM prompts, logs and vendors' systems.

  • Consent and disclosure: tell callers the call is recorded and that they are talking to an automated agent, and log that you did.
  • Minimise and redact: remove personal data from transcripts, logs and analytics copies (entity detection plus patterns for phone, card and ID numbers), set retention periods, and restrict who can listen to recordings.
  • Payments: take card numbers by keypad, mask them in recordings and transcripts, and keep them out of the LLM's context; this shrinks the system's card-security (PCI DSS) scope.
  • Health data: treat it as sensitive: tighter access, explicit consent, and vendors bound not to train on it.
  • India: the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 governs consent and the use of personal data; sector regulators can require data to stay in the country (payment data under RBI rules, for example), which limits where ASR, LLM and TTS can run; and TRAI's commercial-communication rules govern outbound calling (promotional calls from registered 140-series numbers that respect the do-not-disturb registry, service and transactional calls from regulated financial entities on 1600-series numbers). Check the current rules before relying on the details.
  • Vendors: know which providers see audio and text, where they process it, how long they keep it, and whether they train on it.

Serving many calls at once

  • Calls are long-lived, stateful sessions: each holds open audio streams and conversation state for minutes. Route a call to one worker for its whole life, scale on concurrent calls rather than requests per second, and drain workers on deploys instead of dropping live calls.
  • Capacity maths (illustrative): if a GPU running streaming ASR keeps up with audio at 50 times real time in aggregate (RTFx 50, with many streams batched together), it can in principle serve 50 concurrent calls. Run at 60–70% utilisation so queueing doesn't add latency, and plan for 30–35. A peak of 1,000 concurrent calls then needs about 30 GPUs for ASR alone; repeat the sum for TTS and any self-hosted LLM.
  • Batching across calls: GPU-served streaming ASR and TTS put chunks from many calls into one forward pass. Bigger batches raise throughput but make chunks wait, so cap the batching delay at a few tens of milliseconds.
  • Bursts and limits: keep warm pools with models already loaded, since a cold start takes far longer than a caller will wait; when at capacity, queue new calls or play a busy message rather than degrading every live call; respect vendors' rate limits and concurrency quotas.
  • Regions: deploy per region, near the callers and the telephony provider, for latency and data residency, with failover.

Say it in an interview: “For phone calls I'd connect through a telephony provider over SIP or its media stream, which gives 8 kHz μ-law; for apps, WebRTC, which brings echo cancellation and copes with packet loss. I'd orchestrate with a framework like Pipecat or LiveKit Agents unless a hosted platform is enough. Voice prompts need short spoken sentences, spoken-form numbers and read-backs of entities; tools need a filler before slow calls and idempotency; and DTMF, voicemail detection and warm transfers handle what speech alone can't.”

11Evaluating, monitoring and costing a voice agent

Component metrics and conversation metrics

  • Component metrics localise problems: WER and entity accuracy for ASR (section 04); turn-taking errors (callers cut off, replies that came too late, false and missed barge-ins); MOS, re-transcription WER and time to first audio for TTS (section 08); and per-stage latency percentiles (section 09).
  • Conversation metrics decide: task success, containment, transfer rate, handling time, satisfaction (see conversation metrics).

Better components don't guarantee better conversations. A new ASR model can lower WER on general speech and raise errors on digits; a shorter endpointing threshold improves latency and lowers task success by cutting people off. Ship on conversation metrics, and use component metrics to explain them.

Simulated callers

Before launch, and on every change, call the agent with LLM-driven test callers. The general method (a persona, a goal with hidden facts, several runs per scenario, scoring on the final state plus a judge) is in evaluating agents. What voice adds is the audio path and timing:

  1. Voice: the caller LLM's text goes through TTS in varied voices and accents, then through the real channel: downsampled to 8 kHz, μ-law coded, with background noise at chosen signal-to-noise ratios and some packet loss.
  2. Timing behaviours: pausing mid-number, interrupting the agent, backchannelling, long silences, talking over the greeting, keypad input.
  3. The call: over the real transport (a phone number or WebRTC), so the agent's ASR, turn-taking and latency are exercised for real. The caller side transcribes the agent and continues the conversation.
  4. Scoring: deterministic checks first (was the cancel tool called exactly once, with order_id 4729? was the number read back?), an LLM judge on the transcript (see LLM-as-judge), and voice-specific statistics: latency per turn, cut-offs, false barge-ins.

Run a matrix of intents × personas × noise levels × languages, including attempts at prompt injection by voice. Synthetic voices are cleaner and more regular than real callers, so calibrate pass rates against outcomes on real calls.

Regression suites of recorded calls

Keep a golden set (see golden sets) of real, consented and redacted calls with verified transcripts, entities and outcomes, rich in the hard cases: accents, Hinglish, noise, digits, names, interruptions, long silences.

  • Component regressions replay cleanly: re-run ASR on the stored caller audio for WER and entity accuracy; re-run turn detection on the stored audio and check where turns were declared over.
  • Whole conversations don't replay: as soon as the new agent says something different, the recorded caller's next words no longer fit. Replay only up to the first divergence, or turn a recorded call into a simulated caller with the same goal, facts and audio conditions.

Monitoring production calls

  • Latency: per-turn voice-to-voice p50 and p95, broken down by stage.
  • Turn-taking health: interruptions per call, how often the agent stops for noise, how often callers say “hello?” or “are you there?” (dead air), and how often they talk over a reply within a second of it starting (cut-offs).
  • Understanding: “sorry, can you repeat that” from either side, ASR confidence, entity re-confirmations, keypad fallbacks.
  • Outcomes: containment, transfers, early hang-ups, tool error rates.
  • Drift: new product names, new accents, a new campaign's callers; update the hotword lists and the test sets (see drift and online monitoring).
  • Humans in the loop: listen to a sample of calls every week; dashboards miss how a call feels.
  • Vendors: alert on each provider's error rate and latency, with a fallback ASR, LLM and TTS ready.

WER by edit distance

def wer(ref, hyp):
    r, h = ref.split(), hyp.split()
    R, H = len(r), len(h)
    d = [[0] * (H + 1) for _ in range(R + 1)]
    for i in range(R + 1): d[i][0] = i  # deletions
    for j in range(H + 1): d[0][j] = j  # insertions
    for i in range(1, R + 1):
        for j in range(1, H + 1):
            cost = 0 if r[i-1] == h[j-1] else 1
            d[i][j] = min(d[i-1][j-1] + cost,  # sub
                          d[i-1][j] + 1,       # del
                          d[i][j-1] + 1)       # ins
    # walk back to split the total into S, D, I
    i, j, S, D, I = R, H, 0, 0, 0
    while i > 0 or j > 0:
        if (i > 0 and j > 0 and d[i][j] ==
                d[i-1][j-1] + (r[i-1] != h[j-1])):
            S += r[i-1] != h[j-1]
            i, j = i - 1, j - 1
        elif i > 0 and d[i][j] == d[i-1][j] + 1:
            D, i = D + 1, i - 1
        else:
            I, j = I + 1, j - 1
    return (S + D + I) / R, S, D, I

BLANK = "_"

def collapse(path):
    out, prev = [], None
    for s in path:
        if s != prev and s != BLANK:   # new symbol
            out.append(s)
        prev = s                       # blank resets
    return "".join(out)

def ctc_greedy(frame_probs, vocab):
    best = [vocab[argmax(p)] for p in frame_probs]
    return collapse(best)

def ctc_prob(frame_probs, vocab, label):
    idx = {s: k for k, s in enumerate(vocab)}
    z = [BLANK]
    for c in label:
        z += [c, BLANK]                # _ c1 _ c2 _
    S, T = len(z), len(frame_probs)
    a = [[0.0] * S for _ in range(T)]
    a[0][0] = frame_probs[0][idx[z[0]]]
    if S > 1:
        a[0][1] = frame_probs[0][idx[z[1]]]
    for t in range(1, T):
        for s in range(S):
            x = a[t-1][s] + (a[t-1][s-1] if s else 0)
            if s >= 2 and z[s] != BLANK \
                    and z[s] != z[s-2]:
                x += a[t-1][s-2]       # skip a blank
            a[t][s] = x * frame_probs[t][idx[z[s]]]
    return a[-1][-1] + (a[-1][-2] if S > 1 else 0)

Energy VAD with a hangover

def energy_vad(frames_db, thresh_db=-40,
               min_speech=3, hangover=30):
    # frames_db: energy of each 10 ms frame, in dB
    segs, in_speech, run, quiet = [], False, 0, 0
    for t, e in enumerate(frames_db):
        loud = e > thresh_db
        if not in_speech:
            run = run + 1 if loud else 0
            if run >= min_speech:        # not a click
                in_speech, quiet = True, 0
                start = t - min_speech + 1
        else:
            quiet = 0 if loud else quiet + 1
            if quiet >= hangover:        # turn over
                segs.append((start, t - hangover + 1))
                in_speech, run = False, 0
    if in_speech:
        segs.append((start, len(frames_db)))
    return segs

async def call_loop(call, llm, tts, turn_model):
    history = [SYSTEM_PROMPT]
    reply = None                     # agent turn in flight
    async for ev in call.events():   # VAD, ASR, playback
        if ev.type == "speech_start" and reply:
            if await is_interruption(ev):  # not a cough
                reply.task.cancel()        # LLM + TTS
                call.clear_playback()      # all buffers
                ms = call.played_ms(reply) # heard, not sent
                reply.msg.text = heard_text(reply.words, ms)
                reply.msg.interrupted = True
                reply = None
        elif ev.type == "playback_done":
            reply = None
        elif ev.type == "pause" and reply is None:
            text = call.transcript_so_far()
            if turn_model.is_end(text, history):
                history.append(user_msg(call.take_turn()))
                reply = new_reply(history)  # adds msg
                job = speak(call, llm, tts, reply)
                reply.task = spawn(job)

async def speak(call, llm, tts, reply):
    tokens = llm.stream(reply.history)
    async for phrase in phrases(tokens):
        async for chunk in tts.stream(phrase):
            # chunk times start at 0: shift them so
            # they count from the start of the reply
            reply.words += shift(chunk.word_times,
                                 reply.sent_ms)
            reply.sent_ms += chunk.ms
            await call.send_audio(chunk.audio)
        reply.msg.text += phrase + " "

def phrases(tokens, min_words=4):     # async in real use
    buf = ""
    for tok in tokens:
        buf += tok
        end = buf.rstrip()
        if end.endswith((".", "?", "!")) or (
                end.endswith((",", ";"))
                and len(buf.split()) >= min_words):
            yield buf.strip()
            buf = ""
    if buf.strip():
        yield buf.strip()

def heard_text(words, played_ms):
    # words: [(word, start_ms, end_ms)] from TTS
    return " ".join(w for w, s, e in words
                    if e <= played_ms)

1Walk me through what happens in the second after the caller stops speaking.

The audio reaches the server, the system decides the turn is over, the transcript is finalised, the LLM starts its reply, the first phrase goes to TTS, and the first audio travels back: about 1 s when everything streams.

  • 0–100 ms, inbound: the last audio crosses the phone network and the provider's media stream (8 kHz μ-law, 20 ms packets).
  • ~100–400 ms, end of turn: the VAD sees silence; after a short pause a turn detector (text or audio model) judges the turn complete. VAD alone would wait a fixed 500–800 ms.
  • ~50 ms, final transcript: the streaming recogniser has been decoding all along and commits the final.
  • ~300 ms, LLM first token (prompt cached), then ~80 ms until the first phrase ends at a punctuation mark.
  • ~120 ms, TTS first audio, then ~100 ms outbound transport and jitter buffer: about 1,050 ms in total.
  • Meanwhile: the VAD keeps listening for barge-in, and every stage is timestamped.

Follow-up: starting the LLM speculatively on the partial transcript overlaps it with the end-of-turn wait, saving ~150 ms. (09)

2Cascade or speech-to-speech for a customer-support agent? Why?

Usually a cascade: support calls need exact entities, reliable tool calls, auditable transcripts and coverage of the callers' languages, and text in the middle gives all four.

  • Cascade gains: inspect and guard the text, normalise numbers, use the best tool-calling LLM, pick Indic-specialised ASR and TTS, and measure each stage (WER, time to first token, MOS).
  • Cascade losses: tone and emotion are discarded at ASR, latencies add up, ASR errors flow into the LLM.
  • Speech-to-speech gains: one model over codec tokens: lower latency (a few hundred ms), hears tone, full-duplex models handle overlap and backchannels natively.
  • Speech-to-speech losses: harder to steer and debug, weaker on complex tool chains, many audio tokens per second, one vendor.

Follow-up: for companionship or language practice, where naturalness dominates, I'd lean speech-to-speech. A half-cascade (audio-in LLM, text out, separate TTS) is a middle ground. (02)

3How does CTC work?

The encoder outputs a distribution over tokens plus a blank at every frame; a path collapses to text by merging repeats then deleting blanks; the loss is minus the log of the total probability of all paths that collapse to the transcript, computed by dynamic programming.

  • Why the blank: it lets frames emit nothing (silence, between sounds) and separates double letters: “ll” needs “l _ l”.
  • The forward algorithm: interleave blanks into the label (2L + 1 states); at each frame a state is reached by staying, stepping, or skipping a blank between different tokens. Cost \(O(T \cdot L)\) instead of an exponential number of paths.
  • Conditional independence: each frame's output depends only on the audio, so there's no language model inside; external LMs help and errors look like spelling (“for” for “four”).
  • Properties: monotonic, output no longer than the frames, peaky (mostly blanks), fast one-pass decoding, streams with a causal encoder.

Follow-up: the transducer fixes the independence assumption with a prediction network. (03)

5Compare CTC, RNN-T and attention encoder–decoder models. Which would you use for streaming, and why?

A transducer (RNN-T) for streaming: monotonic, frame-synchronous decoding like CTC, but conditioned on previous outputs.

  • CTC: per-frame outputs, sum over alignments, outputs independent given audio. Cheapest, streams, needs an external LM for the best accuracy.
  • RNN-T: encoder + prediction network (an LM over emitted tokens) + joint network; blank means “next frame”, and several tokens can come from one frame. Streams naturally; the training cost is the T × U × V joint tensor (15.6M logits for 10 s of audio, 60 tokens, 1,024-token vocabulary).
  • Attention encoder–decoder (Whisper): a full autoregressive decoder with cross-attention; most accurate and formats text well, but needs the whole input and can hallucinate.
  • Shared part: all three usually sit on a Conformer or transformer encoder; streaming comes from chunked attention in the encoder.

Follow-up: chunk size sets a latency floor: a 320 ms chunk adds 160 ms on average. (03)

6Why isn't Whisper a streaming model? How would you use it in a live agent?

It encodes fixed 30-second windows with attention over the whole window, and decodes only after the encoder has seen everything; so it can't emit words while the caller is still talking.

  • Fixed input: short audio is padded to 30 s (3,000 log-mel frames → 1,500 encoder positions); a 2 s turn costs a 30 s encode.
  • Unstable partials: trained on complete segments, so the last words change as audio grows.
  • Hallucination: on silence or noise it can invent or repeat text; gate it with a VAD.
  • Workarounds: transcribe each VAD-cut utterance after the caller stops (adds the whole transcription time to every turn); or re-decode a growing buffer and commit the prefix two runs agree on (LocalAgreement), which repeats compute and commits late.

Follow-up: for live turns I'd use a streaming transducer or CTC model, and keep Whisper-class models for offline transcripts, QA and labelling. (03)

8Why log-mel features? What goes wrong when phone audio meets a model trained on 16 kHz audio?

Mel spacing matches how pitch is perceived and keeps the spectral envelope while dropping detail; the log compresses loudness and turns channel effects into offsets. Phone audio has nothing above 4 kHz, so about a quarter of a 16 kHz model's mel bands are empty.

  • Mel: roughly linear below 1 kHz, logarithmic above (1,000 Hz = 1,000 mel, 8,000 Hz = 2,840 mel); 80 triangular filters, narrow at low frequencies and wide at high ones.
  • Log: quiet consonants and loud vowels both register; a microphone's or line's frequency response becomes an additive offset that mean normalisation removes.
  • Phone audio: 18–19 of 80 filters over 0–8 kHz sit above 4 kHz and are empty after upsampling; fricatives (“s”, “f”) lose cues; μ-law noise and packet loss add more.
  • Fixes: fine-tune on real 8 kHz calls or wideband data passed through a simulated phone channel; or use an 8 kHz model; always evaluate on your channel.

9Drill: reference “cancel order four seven two nine”, hypothesis “cancel the order for seven nine”. Compute WER by hand. Can WER exceed 100%, and why normalise first?

3 edits over 6 reference words: WER = 50% (S = 1, D = 1, I = 1). WER can exceed 100% because insertions are unbounded, and unnormalised formatting differences count as errors.

  • Recurrence: \(d_{i,j} = \min(d_{i-1,j-1} + [r_i \ne h_j],\ d_{i-1,j} + 1,\ d_{i,j-1} + 1)\), first row and first column 0 to 6; the bottom-right cell is 3.
  • Backtrace: cancel = cancel; insert “the”; order = order; four → for (substitution); seven = seven; delete “two”; nine = nine. So \((1 + 1 + 1)/6 = 0.5\).
  • Over 100%: “no” vs “uh no no thanks” is 3 insertions over 1 word, 300%; typical when a model hallucinates on silence or noise.
  • Normalisation: “cancel order 4729” vs “cancel order four seven two nine” scores 1 substitution + 3 insertions over 3 words, 133%, for a perfect transcript. Use one normaliser for both sides and report it.
  • Corpus WER: total edits over total words, not the average of utterance WERs, which over-weights short utterances (27.5% vs 7.1% in the note's example).

Follow-up: the order number 4729 is lost entirely, which a 50% WER doesn't tell you; entity accuracy does. (04, 04, 04)

10Why does WER understate errors on phone numbers? What would you measure instead?

WER weights every word equally, but a phone number is only useful if every digit is right: one wrong digit in a 20-word sentence is 5% WER and a 100% failed callback.

  • Compounding: with per-digit accuracy \(p\), a 10-digit number is right with probability \(p^{10}\): 95% → 59.9%, 98% → 81.7%, 99% → 90.4%.
  • Meaning flips: “I do not want to cancel” → “I do want to cancel” is one deletion (16.7% WER) and the opposite instruction.
  • Measure: exact-match rate per entity type (phone, order ID, amount, date, name), keyword recall and precision, slot accuracy and task success.
  • Design around it: read entities back in groups, offer keypad entry, validate against known data (the caller's open orders).

(04)

11A vendor's ASR reports 6% WER on a benchmark, but on your calls the agent keeps failing. How do you debug?

Measure on your own audio and your own success criteria: the benchmark differs from your calls in channel, speakers, language mix, vocabulary and what counts as an error.

  • Build a test set of real (consented, redacted) calls with careful transcripts, and compute WER with one normaliser, plus entity exact-match.
  • Slice: 8 kHz vs app audio, accent or region, noise level, Hinglish vs monolingual, digits, names. One slice usually explains most failures.
  • Check the plumbing: sample rate and μ-law decoding (a wrong rate or codec ruins everything quietly), resampling filters, clipping, VAD cutting word edges.
  • Check turn-taking: “ASR failures” are often cut-off turns (the endpointer fired mid-number) or barge-ins on echo.
  • Then fix: hotword biasing, ITN rules, a domain LM, a different or fine-tuned model, keypad fallback for digits.

(01, 04)

12The recogniser keeps getting customer names and product names wrong. What do you do?

Bias recognition towards the expected words, per call, then correct and confirm downstream.

  • Contextual biasing: pass a phrase list (the caller's name, their order IDs, the product catalogue) as hotwords; beam search adds a bonus to hypotheses partway through a listed phrase (a prefix tree), or with Whisper put terms in the prompt.
  • Language model fusion: \(\log P_{\text{ASR}} + \lambda \log P_{\text{LM}} + \beta \lvert y\rvert\) with an LM trained on your transcripts.
  • Downstream: fuzzy or phonetic matching against the database, LLM correction given the candidate list, read-back confirmation.
  • Risk: over-boosting creates false insertions; measure boosted-term recall and general WER, on sets with and without the terms.
  • If still bad: fine-tune on in-domain audio.

(03)

13What's hard about Hinglish (code-switched) speech recognition, and how do you evaluate it fairly?

Language changes mid-sentence, the same speech can be written in several scripts, numbers come in either language, and mixed training data is scarce.

  • Language ID per utterance can't follow “mera order cancel karna hai”.
  • Script ambiguity: Devanagari, romanised, or English words transliterated (ऑर्डर); mismatched conventions inflate WER without any recognition error.
  • Numbers: “chaar saat do nau” and “four seven two nine” must both become 4729; Indian grouping and fractions (“dhai sau” = 250) in ITN.
  • Models: trained on code-mixed audio with a shared vocabulary over both scripts.
  • Little labelled data: start from a self-supervised multilingual encoder (wav2vec 2.0 or HuBERT style) and fine-tune with CTC; wav2vec 2.0 reached 4.8% / 8.2% WER on LibriSpeech with ten minutes of labels.
  • Evaluation: transliterate reference and hypothesis to one script before scoring; consider CER; report errors on the Hindi and English parts separately; entity accuracy on numbers.

(03, 03, 03)

14How does a VAD work? Which settings matter?

It scores each 10–30 ms frame as speech or not, then smooths the scores into segments; the smoothing settings decide the behaviour.

  • Energy VAD: frame energy in dB vs an adaptive noise floor (+ zero-crossing rate); free, but fooled by TV, traffic, breath and quiet callers.
  • Model VADs: WebRTC VAD (GMM over band energies, aggressiveness 0–3), Silero (small neural net, 32 ms chunks at 16 kHz, a probability per chunk).
  • Settings: hysteresis (start above 0.5, stop below 0.35), minimum speech duration (ignore clicks), hangover or minimum silence (bridges pauses; this is the endpointing wait), padding (keep word edges).
  • Example: speech 100–400 and 520–800 ms with a 300 ms hangover is one turn, declared over at 1,100 ms; with 100 ms it's two turns.

(05)

15How would you choose the endpointing silence threshold?

It's a trade between latency and cutting callers off, and the right value depends on what the caller is doing, so I'd make it adaptive rather than pick one number.

  • The cost: the whole threshold is added to every reply's latency.
  • The risk: callers pause mid-turn. In an illustrative model (1.5 pauses per turn, median 300 ms), 300 ms cuts off 53% of ordinary turns, 800 ms 7%, 1,200 ms 2%; for digit dictation (3 pauses, median 500 ms) 800 ms still cuts off 48%.
  • Formula: \(P(\text{cut}) = 1 - e^{-\lambda P(\text{pause} \ge T)}\) for a Poisson number of pauses.
  • Better: context-dependent waits (longer after asking for a number), a turn detector, keypad for long numbers, and merging a resumed turn with the cut-off one.
  • Tune on data: measure cut-off rate (callers talking over a reply within a second) against latency on real calls.

(05)

16How do you detect the end of a turn better than with silence alone?

Predict whether the turn is complete from the words, the intonation and the dialogue context, and wait short when it is, long when it isn't.

  • Signals: syntactic and semantic completeness (“I want to cancel my order.” vs “my number is nine eight seven…”), falling pitch and final lengthening vs filled pauses, what the agent just asked for.
  • Models: text-based turn detectors (a small LM on the transcript, e.g. LiveKit's), audio-based ones (e.g. Pipecat's Smart Turn on the waveform), or the LLM emitting a “wait” token.
  • Policy: on ~200 ms of VAD silence, run the model; reply if confident, otherwise keep waiting with a maximum timeout.
  • Why it wins: it lowers latency and cut-offs together, which a fixed threshold can't.

Follow-up: evaluate it per language on real calls: false end-of-turn (cut-offs) vs late responses. (06)

17How do you handle barge-in, and why does it fail without echo cancellation?

Confirm it's a real interruption (not a backchannel or noise), stop audio everywhere, cancel generation, and trim the agent's message to what the caller heard. Without echo cancellation the agent hears its own voice and interrupts itself.

  • Detect: VAD on the echo-cancelled caller channel during playback, plus a minimum duration, a word or two of ASR, or an interruption classifier. Backchannels (“mm-hmm”, “haan”) are short, from a known list and not followed by more speech: keep talking through them.
  • Stop: flush the orchestrator queue, the TTS stream, the provider's buffer (e.g. Twilio's clear) and the client's jitter buffer; TTS runs ahead of playback, so seconds may be queued.
  • Cancel: LLM and TTS; side-effecting tools need idempotency, not blind cancellation.
  • Trim the history: from playback marks and TTS word timestamps; interrupted at 2.4 s, it keeps “Your refund of two thousand rupees”, marked interrupted. Use the played position, not bytes sent; otherwise the LLM thinks the caller heard “five to seven working days”.
  • Echo: the agent's audio leaks back (speakerphone, line echo), so the VAD fires on it or ASR transcribes it as the caller. Acoustic echo cancellation learns the echo path from the reference signal (the audio that was played) with an adaptive filter such as NLMS and subtracts the predicted echo; double-talk detection freezes learning while both speak. Best on the device (WebRTC includes it); fallbacks are higher barge-in thresholds during playback or ignoring transcripts that match the agent's own recent words.

(06, 06)

18What audio arrives from a phone call, and when would you use WebRTC, WebSockets, SIP or DTMF?

Phone calls arrive as 8 kHz, 8-bit G.711 (μ-law or A-law) in 20 ms packets; WebRTC is for apps, WebSockets for server-to-server streams, SIP for the phone network, and DTMF for keypad input.

  • Phone path: PSTN → telephony provider → SIP trunk (RTP audio) or the provider's media-stream WebSocket (base64 μ-law) → decode, resample to 16 kHz for ASR; TTS back down to 8 kHz μ-law.
  • WebRTC: UDP, Opus, jitter buffer, on-device echo cancellation; best for browser and mobile agents and lossy networks.
  • WebSocket: TCP and simple, but head-of-line blocking causes latency spikes on lossy links; fine between data-centre services.
  • SIP: call setup and control: INVITE, BYE, REFER for transfers.
  • DTMF: two tones per key (“5” = 770 + 1,336 Hz), as events or in-band (Goertzel); exact and private for PINs and long numbers.

(01, 10, 10)

19Walk through a TTS pipeline. Where do FastSpeech 2, HiFi-GAN, VITS and VALL-E fit?

Text normalisation → grapheme-to-phoneme → acoustic model (text to mel spectrogram) → vocoder (mel to waveform); VITS merges the last two stages, and codec language models replace them with token prediction.

  • TN and G2P: “₹2,500” → “two thousand five hundred rupees”, order 4729 → “four seven two nine”; lexicon plus neural G2P; heteronyms and names are the failures.
  • FastSpeech 2: non-autoregressive; predicts each phoneme's duration, pitch and energy; the length regulator repeats phoneme vectors (durations 3, 5, 2, 4, 3, 6 → 23 frames ≈ 267 ms); parallel, robust, controllable. Tacotron 2 was autoregressive with attention, which can skip or repeat words.
  • HiFi-GAN: a GAN vocoder upsampling mel frames to samples, with multi-period and multi-scale discriminators; far faster than real time.
  • VITS: end to end (VAE + flow + adversarial training, alignment learned during training, stochastic durations).
  • VALL-E: language modelling over codec tokens, conditioned on a 3-second prompt; zero-shot cloning, but can hallucinate.

(07)

20How does streaming TTS work in an agent, and what is the trade-off in chunk size?

Cut the LLM's token stream into phrases at punctuation, synthesise each as soon as it's complete, and stream audio chunks while the next phrase is generated; smaller chunks are faster but sound worse.

  • Why: waiting for a 60-token reply at 100 tokens/s costs 600 ms after the first token; the first 8-token phrase is ready in 80 ms. Non-streaming synthesis of 18 s of audio at RTF 0.05 is 900 ms before any sound.
  • The cost: the TTS doesn't see what comes next: questions get the wrong intonation, every chunk falls like a sentence end, joins can be audible.
  • Mitigations: a short first phrase, sentence-sized later ones; APIs that take incremental text with a context id and keep prosody across chunks.
  • Also needed: word timestamps from the TTS, so barge-in can trim to what was heard.

(07)

21How do you evaluate a TTS voice? Drill: ten ratings 4, 5, 4, 3, 4, 5, 4, 4, 3, 4. Give the MOS with a 95% interval.

MOS 4.00, 95% interval [3.52, 4.48]; and for real decisions, comparative tests plus automatic checks.

  • Drill: mean 4.00; standard deviation 0.667; standard error \(0.667/\sqrt{10} = 0.211\); t with 9 degrees of freedom is 2.262; half-width 0.48.
  • Correlated ratings: with 20 listeners × 10 sentences, the naive interval (±0.10) is about 2.5× too narrow; bootstrapping listeners and sentences gives ±0.25 in the note's simulation.
  • Sensitivity: CMOS or AB preference for comparing two voices; MUSHRA for ranking several close ones; 4.1 vs 4.2 at sd 0.9 needs about 1,300 independent ratings per system.
  • Automatic: re-transcription WER/CER (skipped words, wrong numbers), speaker-embedding similarity for cloned voices, automatic MOS predictors for regressions, time to first audio p50/p95 under load.

(08)

22Drill: inbound 100 ms, silence threshold 800, batch ASR 400, LLM first token 500 then 50 tokens at 60/s before TTS starts, TTS 500 for the whole reply, outbound 100. Total? And with streaming: turn detector 300, ASR final 50, first token 300, first phrase 8 tokens at 100/s, TTS first audio 120?

3,233 ms naive; 1,050 ms streaming.

  • Naive: 100 + 800 + 400 + (500 + 50/60 × 1,000 = 1,333) + 500 + 100 = 3,233 ms.
  • Streaming: 100 + 300 + 50 + 300 + 80 + 120 + 100 = 1,050 ms.
  • Speculative start: begin the LLM after 200 ms of silence on the partial transcript: about 900 ms.
  • Percentiles: don't add per-stage p95s. In the note's simulation the sum of stage p95s is 1,887 ms but the p95 of the total is 1,500 ms, and the LLM's first token causes 77% of the variance.

(09, 09)

23Your agent's p50 voice-to-voice latency is 1.4 s. How would you cut 300 ms?

Measure the per-stage breakdown on real turns first, then attack the biggest items, which are usually the end-of-turn wait and the LLM's first token.

  • End of turn: replace a fixed 800 ms silence threshold with a turn detector (300 ms on complete turns): up to ~500 ms on its own.
  • Overlap: start the LLM speculatively on the partial transcript during the wait (~150 ms).
  • LLM first token: prompt caching, a shorter prompt, a faster model for routine turns, provisioned capacity (also cuts the p95).
  • Network: co-locate media, ASR, LLM and TTS near the callers; warm connections. A cross-continent round trip per hop is often 150–250 ms.
  • TTS: stream it, a short first phrase, cached audio for fixed phrases.

Follow-up: fillers reduce perceived latency only; check cut-off rate and task success after any endpointing change. (09)

24Drill: a codec runs at 12.5 frames a second with 8 codebooks of 2,048 entries. Tokens per second, bitrate, and how does that compare with text?

100 tokens a second and 1.1 kbit/s, about 30 times as many tokens as the transcript of the same speech.

  • Tokens: 12.5 × 8 = 100 per second; 2,048 entries is 11 bits, so 100 × 11 = 1,100 bit/s (Mimi, used by Moshi).
  • Compression: raw 24 kHz 16-bit audio is 384 kbit/s, about 350× more.
  • Text: 150 words a minute at ~1.3 tokens a word is ~3.3 tokens a second.
  • Why it matters: context length and cost grow with audio tokens; a 5-minute call is 30,000 tokens per stream, or 3,750 steps of Moshi's main transformer (12.5 a second; a small depth transformer predicts each step's 8 codebook tokens). EnCodec at 6 kbit/s (75 Hz × 8) is 600 tokens a second, hence the push to low frame rates.
  • RVQ: each codebook quantises the residual left by the previous ones, coarse to fine.

(02)

25How would you serve 1,000 concurrent calls?

Treat calls as long-lived stateful sessions, size GPU capacity from throughput at a safe utilisation, batch across calls, and keep warm capacity in each region.

  • Sessions: each call stays on one worker for its whole life; scale on concurrent calls, not requests per second; drain workers on deploys instead of dropping calls.
  • Capacity (illustrative): a GPU running streaming ASR at RTFx 50 serves about 50 streams in principle; at 60–70% utilisation plan for 30–35, so about 30 GPUs for ASR at 1,000 calls. Repeat for TTS and any self-hosted LLM, or check vendors' concurrency quotas.
  • Batching: chunks from many calls in one forward pass, with the batching delay capped at a few tens of milliseconds so latency holds.
  • Bursts: warm pools with models loaded; at capacity, queue new calls or play a busy message rather than degrading every live call.
  • Regions: near callers and the telephony provider, for latency and data residency, with failover.

(10)

26How is prompting a voice agent different from prompting a chat agent?

The output is heard once, in real time, through TTS, and the input is a noisy transcript; the prompt has to account for both.

  • Output: short sentences, one question per turn, no markdown, lists, emojis or URLs; numbers in spoken form (“four seven two nine”, “two thousand five hundred rupees”); answer first, short first sentence for latency.
  • Input: say it's a speech transcript with possible misrecognitions; clarify instead of guessing; never act on one hearing of a critical entity.
  • Confirmation: read entities back in groups; confirm before irreversible actions.
  • Language: match the caller's language mix and register.
  • Voice situations: silence, noise, repeated failures (offer keypad or a human), interruptions.

Follow-up: keep state such as collected and confirmed slots in the orchestrator, not just the transcript. (10)

27A tool call takes 3 seconds in the middle of a call. What should the agent do?

Never leave dead air: say what it's doing before the call, give a short update if it runs long, and make the call safe to retry.

  • Before: have the LLM emit “Let me check that order for you.” before the tool call, or have the orchestrator play a filler if no result arrives within about a second.
  • During: a brief update every few seconds on long operations; keep listening for barge-in.
  • Correctness: arguments from confirmed, normalised entities (4729); idempotency keys so a retry or a barge-in can't cancel or charge twice; timeouts with a spoken fallback.
  • Speed: run independent tools in parallel; prefetch likely data (the caller's orders) when the call starts.

(10)

28What privacy and compliance issues does a voice agent for support or healthcare calls in India raise?

Consent and AI disclosure, personal and health data copied into recordings, transcripts, prompts and vendors' systems, payment data, data residency, and the rules for outbound calling.

  • Disclosure: say the call is recorded and that the caller is talking to an automated agent; log it.
  • Data: redact personal data from transcripts and logs, set retention periods, restrict access to recordings; treat health data as sensitive.
  • Payments: card numbers by keypad, masked in recordings and transcripts, never in the LLM's context (smaller PCI DSS scope).
  • India: the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 for consent and use of personal data; sector rules that keep some data in the country (payment data under RBI rules); TRAI's commercial-communication rules for outbound calls (140-series numbers and the do-not-disturb registry for promotional calls, 1600-series for regulated financial entities' service calls).
  • Vendors and voices: where audio and text are processed and kept, and whether they're used for training; clone voices only with consent.

(10, 07)

29How do you evaluate a voice bot before launch, and monitor it after?

Before: simulated callers over the real channel plus a regression set of recorded calls, scored on both component and conversation metrics. After: per-turn latency, turn-taking health, understanding and outcomes, with humans listening to samples.

  • Simulated callers: an LLM with a goal, hidden facts and a persona; TTS in varied voices; degraded to 8 kHz μ-law with noise; called over the real transport; scored by deterministic checks (tool called once with order_id 4729) and an LLM judge.
  • Recorded calls: re-run ASR and turn detection on stored audio; conversations diverge after the first different reply, so replay only up to there or seed a simulated caller from the call.
  • Component metrics: WER and entity accuracy, cut-offs and false barge-ins, TTS checks, latency p50/p95.
  • Conversation metrics: task success, containment, transfers.
  • In production: “hello?” rate, talk-over rate, repeats, early hang-ups, tool errors, drift in vocabulary and callers; vendor health with fallbacks.

30Drill: telephony $0.010/min, ASR $0.008/min, TTS $30 per million characters at 360 characters a minute, and an LLM at 4 turns a minute with a 2,000-token prompt, history growing 100 tokens a turn, 20 output tokens a turn. Cost per minute of a 5-minute call with a model at $2.50 in and $10 out per million? What happens at 20 minutes?

About $0.059 a minute, of which the LLM is $0.030; at 20 minutes the LLM part roughly doubles per minute, to $0.060.

  • TTS: 360 × $30/1M = $0.0108 a minute.
  • LLM input, 20 turns: \(\sum_{k=0}^{19}(2{,}000 + 100k) = 59{,}000\) tokens; output 400. Cost: 59,000 × $2.50/1M + 400 × $10/1M = $0.1515 per call, $0.0303 a minute.
  • Total: 0.010 + 0.008 + 0.0108 + 0.0303 = $0.059 a minute.
  • 20 minutes: 80 turns, 476,000 input tokens (23,800 a minute vs 11,800), because each turn resends the whole history: $0.060 a minute for the LLM.
  • Levers: prefix caching at 10% of the input price brings the 5-minute LLM cost to $0.0055 a minute; trimming or summarising history; a small model ($0.0018 a minute at $0.15/$0.60).

15Cheat sheet

Audio

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{bitrate} &= f_s \times \text{bits} \times \text{channels} \\ \text{frames} &= 1 + \lfloor (N - W)/H \rfloor \\ m &= 2595 \log_{10}(1 + f/700) \\ F_\mu(x) &= \operatorname{sgn}(x)\ln(1 + \mu\lvert x\rvert)/\ln(1 + \mu) \end{aligned} \]

Phone: 8 kHz, 8-bit μ-law/A-law, 64 kbit/s, nothing above 4 kHz, 20 ms packets. ASR: 16 kHz, 25 ms window (400 samples), 10 ms hop (160), 100 frames/s, 80 mels; Whisper 80 × 3,000 per 30 s → 1,500 encoder positions. Upsampling doesn't restore 4–8 kHz.

Architectures

Cascade: VAD/turn detector → streaming ASR → LLM + tools → streaming TTS; control, tools, logs, per-stage metrics; loses tone; ~1 s. Speech-to-speech: codec tokens (RVQ) in one model; Mimi 12.5 Hz × 8 = 100 tokens/s, 1.1 kbit/s; Moshi full duplex, 160–200 ms.

ASR

\[ \begin{aligned} P_{\text{CTC}}(y \mid x) &= \sum_{a \in \mathcal{B}^{-1}(y)} \prod_t p_t(a_t) \\ \alpha_t(s) &= (\alpha_{t-1}(s) + \alpha_{t-1}(s-1) + [\text{skip}]\,\alpha_{t-1}(s-2))\,p_t(z_s) \\ h_{t,u} &= \tanh(W_e e_t + W_g g_u) \\ \text{score} &= \log P_{\text{ASR}} + \lambda \log P_{\text{LM}} + \beta\lvert y\rvert \end{aligned} \]

CTC: blank, merge repeats then drop blanks, independent outputs, fast. RNN-T: prediction + joint networks, streams. Whisper: 30 s windows, not streaming, hallucinates on silence. Conformer: attention + convolution, 4× (or 8×) subsampling. Self-supervised encoders (wav2vec 2.0: masked contrastive; HuBERT: masked k-means targets) + CTC fine-tuning for low-resource languages. Chunk C adds ~C/2 latency. Biasing per call; ITN for numbers; transliterate Hinglish before scoring; diarization: embed + cluster, DER.

WER

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{WER} &= (S + D + I)/N \\ d_{i,j} &= \min(d_{i-1,j-1} + [r_i \ne h_j],\ d_{i-1,j} + 1,\ d_{i,j-1} + 1) \\ P(\text{10 digits right}) &= p^{10} \end{aligned} \]

Running example 3/6 = 50%. Can exceed 100% (insertions). Normalise both sides. Corpus WER, bootstrap by utterance. CER for unsegmented or agglutinative languages. One digit wrong in 20 words = 5% WER, failed task: track entity exact match. RTF = processing time / audio time.

VAD and turn-taking

\[ P(\text{cut off}) = 1 - e^{-\lambda\,P(\text{pause} \ge T)} \]

Hysteresis 0.5/0.35, minimum speech, hangover = endpointing wait, padding. 300 ms cuts off 53% of ordinary turns in the illustrative model, 800 ms 7%, but 48% of digit dictation. Turn detectors (text or audio) wait short on complete turns. Barge-in: confirm, flush all buffers, cancel, trim history to what was played. AEC or the agent interrupts itself.

TTS and MOS

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{CI} &= \bar r \pm t_{0.975,\,n-1}\, s/\sqrt n \\ n_{\text{per system}} &\approx 16\sigma^2/\delta^2 \end{aligned} \]

TN → G2P → acoustic model (FastSpeech 2: durations, length regulator) → vocoder (HiFi-GAN); VITS end to end; VALL-E codec LM, 3 s cloning. Stream by phrase; word timestamps. Ten ratings: 4.00 ± 0.48. Bootstrap listeners and sentences (±0.25 vs naive ±0.10). CMOS, MUSHRA, re-transcription WER, speaker similarity, MOS predictors, TTFA p95.

Latency and cost

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{naive} &= 100 + 800 + 400 + 1{,}333 + 500 + 100 = 3{,}233 \text{ ms} \\ \text{tuned} &= 100 + 300 + 50 + 300 + 80 + 120 + 100 = 1{,}050 \text{ ms} \\ \text{LLM input} &= \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(\text{prompt} + k \times \text{per turn}) \end{aligned} \]

Goal about 1 s (rule of thumb). p95 of a sum is less than the sum of p95s; the LLM owns the tail. Levers: turn detector, speculative start, streaming, prompt caching, co-location, short first phrase, fillers. Cost per minute = telephony + ASR + TTS + LLM; with the assumed prices, $0.031 (small model) to $0.059 (large); the LLM part grows with call length. Scale on concurrent calls (sticky sessions, warm pools, about 60–70% utilisation). Compliance: consent and AI disclosure, redaction, cards by keypad, DPDP Act, TRAI calling rules.
