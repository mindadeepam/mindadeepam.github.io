Activation functions

Why a network needs a bend between its layers, how each activation shapes the gradient that flows back through it, and how to choose one: sigmoid and tanh, ReLU and its variants, GELU and SiLU, gated units like SwiGLU, and the output layer.

About 35 minutes · formulas, derivations, figures and 20 questions · pairs with Deep learning basics

In short

  1. An activation is the element-wise bend in \(a = f(z)\). Without it, any stack of layers is one affine map. With it, one wide hidden layer can approximate any continuous function, and depth multiplies the number of linear pieces a ReLU network can draw.
  2. Backprop multiplies the error by \(f'(z)\) at every layer, so an activation is judged by its slope. The sigmoid's slope is at most 1/4, and both sigmoid and tanh saturate (slope near 0 in the tails), so gradients vanish with depth.
  3. ReLU's slope is exactly 1 when on and 0 when off: fast, sparse and cheap, but a unit whose input is always negative gets no gradient and dies. Leaky ReLU, ELU, SELU, GELU and SiLU each fix part of that.
  4. GELU, \(z\Phi(z)\), and SiLU, \(z\sigma(z)\), are smooth, slightly non-monotonic ReLUs; GELU is the BERT and GPT default. SwiGLU, \((\text{SiLU}(xW) \odot xV)W_2\), is the feed-forward block of most modern LLMs, with the hidden width cut to \(\tfrac23 \cdot 4d\) to keep the parameter count.
  5. Output activations follow the target and pair with a likelihood loss, so the gradient at the logits is prediction minus target: identity with MSE, sigmoid with BCE, softmax with cross-entropy, softplus for positive values. Pass logits, not probabilities, to the loss.
  6. Initialization must match the activation (LeCun or Xavier for tanh, LeCun normal for SELU, He's \(2/n\) for ReLU). A softmax saturates just like a sigmoid, which is why attention divides its scores by \(\sqrt{d_k}\).

01Why a non-linearity at all

A layer computes a weighted sum, then bends it:

\[ z = W x + b \qquad a = f(z) \]

The activation \(f\) acts on each unit's own pre-activation, separately. (Softmax is the one exception, and it lives at the output.) It's the only part of a layer that isn't a straight line. Three facts explain why it's needed and what it buys.

Stacked linear layers collapse

Remove \(f\) and compose two layers:

\[ \begin{aligned} y &= W_2\,(W_1 x + b_1) + b_2 \\ &= (W_2 W_1)\,x + (W_2 b_1 + b_2) \\ &= W' x + b' \end{aligned} \]

In words: a linear map of a linear map is linear. A 100-layer network without activations is linear regression; put a sigmoid on the end and it's logistic regression. It can only draw flat decision boundaries, so it can't even solve XOR.

  • The product \(W_2 W_1\) has rank at most the narrowest layer's width, so a deep linear network with a bottleneck is a low-rank linear map, never more.
  • Its loss is still non-convex in the weights, with saddle points. Deep linear networks are a useful model of training dynamics, even though they represent nothing new.

Universal approximation: what it promises and what it doesn't

The theorem (Cybenko 1989 for sigmoids; Hornik 1991; Leshno et al. 1993 in general): one hidden layer, with any continuous activation that is not a polynomial, can approximate any continuous function \(g\) on a closed, bounded region \(K\) as closely as you like, given enough units \(N\):

\[ \sup_{x \in K}\, \Big\lvert\, g(x) - \sum_{i=1}^{N} c_i\, f\big(w_i^\top x + b_i\big) \Big\rvert < \varepsilon \]

Why "not a polynomial": with a polynomial activation of degree \(k\), the whole network is a polynomial of degree at most \(k\), however wide it is, and those can't approximate everything. ReLU, sigmoid, tanh and GELU all qualify.

What it does not promise:

  • Size. \(N\) can be enormous; for some functions it grows exponentially with the input dimension.
  • Learnability. The weights exist; nothing says gradient descent finds them.
  • Generalization. Approximating a function is not learning it from finite data.
  • Extrapolation. It holds only on \(K\). Outside the data, a ReLU net continues linearly and a sigmoid net goes flat.

ReLU networks are piecewise linear

Fix an input. Every ReLU is either on (it acts as the identity) or off (it outputs 0). For that on/off pattern, the whole network is a product of masked matrices: an affine map. Inputs that share a pattern form a convex region. So a ReLU network is a continuous, piecewise-linear function: flat facets glued along the hyperplanes where some unit's pre-activation crosses 0.

\[ \begin{aligned} f(x) &= A_r\, x + c_r \qquad \text{on region } r \\ A_r &= W_L\, D^{(r)}_{L-1}\, W_{L-1} \cdots D^{(r)}_1\, W_1 \end{aligned} \]

Here \(D^{(r)}_l\) is the diagonal 0/1 mask of which units in layer \(l\) are on in region \(r\).

How the number of regions grows:

  • Width grows the count polynomially. One hidden layer of \(n\) units cuts \(d\)-dimensional input space with \(n\) hyperplanes, giving at most \(\sum_{i=0}^{d} \binom{n}{i}\) regions, on the order of \(n^d\). On a 1-D input, that's just \(n + 1\) pieces.
  • Depth multiplies. Each layer can fold the space handed to it by the layer below, so every later piece is reused in every fold. The maximum count grows exponentially with depth (Montúfar et al. 2014).

The cleanest example is the tent map, two ReLUs that map \([0, 1]\) up to 1 and back down:

\[ t(x) = 2\,\text{ReLU}(x) - 4\,\text{ReLU}\big(x - \tfrac12\big) \]
  • The maximum isn't typical. Trained networks have far fewer regions than the bound: the count grows with the total number of units, about as it would for a shallow network of the same size, not exponentially with depth (Hanin and Rolnick 2019). Depth's advantage is real but more modest in practice.
  • Piecewise linear is still universal for smooth targets. Sawtooths let a deep ReLU net approximate \(x^2\) with error shrinking exponentially in depth (Yarotsky 2017). Products follow from \(xy = \tfrac12\big[(x + y)^2 - x^2 - y^2\big]\), and polynomials and smooth functions from products.

Trap

“ReLU is linear on each side, so a ReLU network is linear.” Only inside one region. Each unit bends along its own hyperplane, and a sum of functions bent in different places is not a line. The network would be linear only if every unit were on for every input.

02Sigmoid and tanh

\[ \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} \qquad \tanh z = \frac{e^{z} - e^{-z}}{e^{z} + e^{-z}} \]

The sigmoid squashes any score into \((0, 1)\), and tanh into \((-1, 1)\). Their slopes:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sigma'(z) &= \frac{e^{-z}}{(1 + e^{-z})^2} \\ &= \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} \cdot \frac{e^{-z}}{1 + e^{-z}} \\ &= \sigma(z)\,\big(1 - \sigma(z)\big) \end{aligned} \]

A product \(p(1 - p)\) peaks at \(p = \tfrac12\), so the sigmoid's slope is at most 1/4, reached at \(z = 0\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \tanh' z &= \frac{\cosh^2 z - \sinh^2 z}{\cosh^2 z} \\ &= 1 - \tanh^2 z \end{aligned} \]

tanh's slope peaks at 1, at \(z = 0\). Both slopes are written in terms of the output, so backprop reuses the stored activation instead of recomputing an exponential.

tanh is a rescaled sigmoid. Multiply top and bottom by \(e^{z}\) in the third line:

\[ \begin{aligned} 2\sigma(2z) - 1 &= \frac{2}{1 + e^{-2z}} - 1 \\ &= \frac{1 - e^{-2z}}{1 + e^{-2z}} \\ &= \frac{e^{z} - e^{-z}}{e^{z} + e^{-z}} \\ &= \tanh z \end{aligned} \]
  • The slope: differentiating gives \(\tanh'(z) = 4\sigma'(2z)\), so tanh's peak slope is four times the sigmoid's.
  • The same function class: the 2 inside folds into this layer's weights and bias, and the \(\times 2\) and \(-1\) fold into the next layer's. A tanh network is a sigmoid network with rescaled parameters. Every difference between them is about optimization: slope and centring.

Saturation

Far from 0 both curves go flat. For large \(\lvert z \rvert\), their slopes decay exponentially:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sigma'(z) &\approx e^{-\lvert z \rvert} \\ \tanh'(z) &\approx 4\, e^{-2\lvert z \rvert} \end{aligned} \]

A unit whose pre-activation sits out there is saturated: its output barely moves when its weights move, so it gets almost no gradient, even in a shallow network. tanh saturates sooner (the 2 in the exponent). Typical causes are unscaled inputs, weights initialized too large, and weights that grew during training.

Vanishing gradients through depth

Backprop passes the error \(\delta_l = \partial L / \partial z_l\) down one layer at a time: through the weights, then through the activation's slope. Unrolled from the top layer \(L\) down to layer 1 (the product is ordered, \(l = 1\) on the left):

\[ \begin{aligned} \delta_l &= \operatorname{diag}\big(f'(z_l)\big)\, W_{l+1}^\top\, \delta_{l+1} \\ \delta_1 &= \Big[\prod_{l=1}^{L-1} \operatorname{diag}\big(f'(z_l)\big)\, W_{l+1}^\top\Big]\, \delta_L \end{aligned} \]

Taking norms, each layer contributes its largest slope times its weight matrix's norm:

\[ \lVert \delta_1 \rVert \le \lVert \delta_L \rVert\, \prod_{l=1}^{L-1} \Big(\max_i \big\lvert f'(z_{l,i}) \big\rvert\Big)\, \lVert W_{l+1} \rVert \]
  • For sigmoids every slope factor is at most 1/4, so the bound shrinks like \(4^{-(L-1)}\) unless the weight matrices' norms average (as a geometric mean) more than 4.
  • Bigger weights don't rescue it. They spread the pre-activations into the tails, where the slope is far below 1/4.
  • The result: the error reaching the early layers shrinks exponentially with depth. That's the vanishing gradient. The early layers barely move, and the network trains like a shallow model sitting on random features.
  • A second sigmoid problem: its outputs average about 0.5, not 0. Glorot and Bengio (2010) saw the top hidden layer of deep sigmoid nets drive itself into saturation near 0 early in training, and recover only slowly.

tanh helps (peak slope 1) but still saturates. The mirror image, factors above 1 at every layer, is the exploding gradient. That's more a weights problem than an activation problem, handled by initialization (section 08), normalization and clipping.

Zero-centring and the zig-zag

The gradient for a weight into unit \(i\) is that unit's error times the input the weight saw:

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial W_{l,ij}} = \delta_{l,i}\; a_{l-1,j} \]

If every input \(a_{l-1,j}\) is positive, as sigmoid outputs always are, every entry of row \(i\) has the sign of \(\delta_{l,i}\). On one example, all the weights into a unit must go up together or down together. If the best move is “this weight up, that one down”, the updates can only get there by zig-zagging.

  • Mini-batches soften it. Averaging examples whose errors have different signs lets a batch update point anywhere. That's why it's a secondary effect next to saturation.
  • The same issue, seen through curvature. For a linear unit with squared loss, the Hessian is the inputs' second-moment matrix. With input mean \(\mu\):
\[ \mathbb E\big[a\, a^\top\big] = \operatorname{Cov}(a) + \mu\, \mu^\top \]

The \(\mu\mu^\top\) term adds one large eigenvalue along \(\mu\), which stretches the loss bowl and forces a small learning rate (LeCun et al. 1998). Centring removes it. It's the same reason input features are standardized, one layer deeper.

Where they're still used

  • Gates (sigmoid): LSTM and GRU gates, squeeze-and-excitation, GLU. A gate means “what fraction to let through”, which needs \((0, 1)\). Saturation is even useful here: a gate can be fully open or fully shut.
  • Bounded, signed states (tanh): the LSTM candidate and cell output, the vanilla RNN state. A recurrence applies the same weights at every step, and tanh's bound stops the state blowing up. The LSTM escapes vanishing gradients through its additive cell path, not through its activations.
  • Output layers: sigmoid for a probability (binary, or each label of a multi-label problem); tanh for targets in \([-1, 1]\), such as a GAN generator's pixels.
  • Inside other functions: SiLU is \(z\sigma(z)\), GELU's fast approximation uses tanh, and Gemma 2 caps attention and output logits smoothly with \(c \tanh(s / c)\).
  • Not as the hidden activation of a deep feed-forward stack. With 2–3 layers either trains fine; the problem compounds with depth.

03ReLU

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{ReLU}(z) &= \max(0, z) \\ \text{ReLU}'(z) &= \begin{cases} 1 & z > 0 \\ 0 & z < 0 \end{cases} \end{aligned} \]

Why it trains fast

  • Slope exactly 1 when on. In the backward product above, \(\operatorname{diag}(f')\) becomes a 0/1 mask. Along active paths the activation multiplies the error by exactly 1, so it never shrinks it; only the weights scale the signal, and initialization controls those. AlexNet (2012) reported that a ReLU network reached 25% training error on CIFAR-10 about six times faster than the same network with tanh.
  • No saturation for \(z > 0\). A large positive input still has slope 1.
  • Sparsity. With pre-activations symmetric around 0, about half the units output exactly 0 for any input. An off unit passes nothing forward and gets no gradient, so each input runs through, and trains, only the units it switched on. Sparse codes are easy for the next layer to read, and the backward pass needs only a 0/1 mask. The zeros rarely save compute on GPUs, which multiply densely anyway; real savings need very high sparsity, as in some LLM inference tricks.
  • Cheap. One comparison forward; a stored mask backward. No exponentials.

One more property: ReLU is positively homogeneous, \(\text{ReLU}(cz) = c\,\text{ReLU}(z)\) for \(c > 0\). Scaling one layer's weights by \(c\) and the next layer's by \(1/c\) leaves the function unchanged. Weight decay breaks this symmetry (it prefers the balanced scaling), and a normalization layer after the weights makes their scale irrelevant altogether.

The derivative at 0

ReLU has a corner at 0, so it has no derivative there. Any value in \([0, 1]\) is a valid subgradient: the slope of a line that touches the function at the corner and stays below it. PyTorch, TensorFlow and JAX all use 0. In float32 it rarely matters, because a pre-activation is seldom exactly 0. Exact zeros are commoner than they look, though (zero padding, sparse inputs, dead units upstream, rounding), and in 16-bit training the choice can measurably change results (Bertoin et al. 2021). Above all, pick one value and use it consistently.

Dead ReLUs

A unit is dead when its pre-activation is negative for every input in the data. Then its slope is 0 on every example, so every gradient reaching its weights is exactly 0. With \(g_i(x)\) the gradient arriving at unit \(i\)'s output and \(a(x)\) its input vector:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_i} &= \sum_{x} g_i(x)\; \mathbf 1\big[z_i(x) > 0\big]\; a(x) \\ &= 0 \qquad \text{if } z_i(x) \le 0 \text{ for every } x \end{aligned} \]

Nothing moves the weights, so it stays dead. It revives only if the layers below change its inputs enough to push some \(z_i\) back above 0.

Why it happens:

  • One large update. A learning rate that's too high, or an outlier batch, pushes the bias far negative or swings the weights away from all the data.
  • Non-negative inputs make it sticky. A hidden ReLU's inputs are other ReLUs' outputs, all \(\ge 0\). A unit whose incoming weights are all negative, with a negative bias, is off for every possible input, not just the training data.
  • Bad initialization: large negative biases, or weights so large that one step overshoots.

How to detect it: over many batches, record for each unit (each channel, in a CNN) whether it was ever active. Being 0 on one example is normal; being 0 on every example of a large sample is dead. Track the dead fraction per layer over training. A jump right after a learning-rate spike is the signature. The monitor is in section 10.

How to fix it: a lower learning rate or warmup; He initialization; normalization before the activation, which re-centres each unit's pre-activations every batch so it fires on about half the batch; gradient clipping; or an activation with a non-zero negative side (Leaky ReLU, ELU, GELU, SiLU). Some practitioners reinitialize dead units in place.

Not zero-centred, and unbounded

ReLU outputs are never negative. For a Gaussian pre-activation with mean 0 and standard deviation \(s\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E\big[\text{ReLU}(z)\big] &= \frac{s}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \\ \mathbb E\big[\text{ReLU}(z)^2\big] &= \frac{s^2}{2} \end{aligned} \]
  • The positive mean (about \(0.4\,s\)) gives the next layer a milder version of the sigmoid's zig-zag. The next layer's bias and normalization layers absorb it.
  • The second moment is what sets He initialization, below.
  • Unbounded above. That's why it doesn't saturate, but activations can grow without limit: in a recurrence they can explode, and in low-precision arithmetic their range isn't known in advance (hence ReLU6, section 04).

He initialization: why ReLU needs the factor 2

To keep a signal's size steady through depth, each layer must preserve the variance of its pre-activations. Take a unit with \(n_{\text{in}}\) inputs, coming from a previous layer whose pre-activations \(z_{\text{prev}}\) are symmetric around 0. Section 08 derives the first line below; the second uses the fact that ReLU zeroes the negative half:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(z) &= n_{\text{in}}\, \operatorname{Var}(w)\, \mathbb E[a^2] \\ &= n_{\text{in}}\, \operatorname{Var}(w) \cdot \tfrac12 \operatorname{Var}(z_{\text{prev}}) \end{aligned} \]

Setting \(\operatorname{Var}(z)\) equal to \(\operatorname{Var}(z_{\text{prev}})\):

\[ \operatorname{Var}(w) = \frac{2}{n_{\text{in}}} \]

A nearly linear activation keeps the whole variance, which gives \(1/n_{\text{in}}\). ReLU keeps half, so the weights must make up the lost half. Without the 2, the variance halves at every layer and the signal vanishes in a deep network. With Leaky ReLU or PReLU of slope \(\alpha\), the 2 becomes \(2/(1 + \alpha^2)\).

04ReLU variants

Each variant keeps ReLU's slope of 1 for positive inputs and changes the negative side, to stop units dying, to centre the outputs, or to bound them.

Leaky ReLU, PReLU and RReLU

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{LeakyReLU}(z) &= \begin{cases} z & z > 0 \\ \alpha z & z \le 0 \end{cases} \\ \text{LeakyReLU}'(z) &= \begin{cases} 1 & z > 0 \\ \alpha & z \le 0 \end{cases} \end{aligned} \]
  • Leaky ReLU (\(\alpha = 0.01\) typically; 0.2 in GAN discriminators): an off unit still passes a fraction \(\alpha\) of the gradient, so it can climb back. It can't die.
  • PReLU learns \(\alpha\), usually one per channel (He et al. 2015 started it at 0.25). Its gradient is \(\partial f / \partial \alpha = \min(0, z)\). Don't apply weight decay to \(\alpha\): it would pull \(\alpha\) toward 0, back to ReLU.
  • RReLU samples \(\alpha\) uniformly from a range, such as \([1/8, 1/3]\), during training and uses the range's mean at test time: a small regularizer in the spirit of dropout.
  • Why not always use Leaky? Once initialization and normalization are right, the gains over ReLU are small and inconsistent, and ReLU's exact zeros give sparsity.

ELU: smooth, with a negative floor

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{ELU}(z) &= \begin{cases} z & z > 0 \\ \alpha\,(e^{z} - 1) & z \le 0 \end{cases} \\ \text{ELU}'(z) &= \begin{cases} 1 & z > 0 \\ \text{ELU}(z) + \alpha & z \le 0 \end{cases} \end{aligned} \]
  • Negative outputs pull the mean toward 0. For a standard-normal input the mean output is about 0.16, against ReLU's 0.40, so the next layer's inputs are closer to centred. Clevert et al. (2015) argue this speeds learning in the same way centring does.
  • Saturates on the negative side, at \(-\alpha\). That's deliberate: a strongly negative input gives a fixed “off” value that's robust to noise, instead of one that keeps changing like Leaky ReLU's.
  • Differentiable at 0 when \(\alpha = 1\): the slope from the left, \(\alpha e^{0}\), then equals the slope from the right, 1. (The second derivative still jumps.)
  • Cost: an exponential on the negative side.

SELU and self-normalizing networks

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{SELU}(z) &= \lambda \begin{cases} z & z > 0 \\ \alpha\,(e^{z} - 1) & z \le 0 \end{cases} \\ \lambda &\approx 1.0507, \qquad \alpha \approx 1.6733 \end{aligned} \]

SELU is a scaled ELU whose two constants make activations normalize themselves, layer after layer, without BatchNorm (Klambauer et al. 2017).

The fixed point. Track the mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\nu\) of a layer's activations. The paper's analysis assumes each unit's weights are normalized:

\[ \begin{aligned} \textstyle\sum_j w_j &= 0 \\ \textstyle\sum_j w_j^2 &= 1 \end{aligned} \]

Then a unit's pre-activation is roughly Gaussian with mean 0 and variance \(\nu\), and the layer maps \((\mu, \nu)\) to a new pair \((\tilde\mu, \tilde\nu)\). \(\lambda\) and \(\alpha\) are the unique pair that makes \((0, 1)\) a fixed point of that map: two equations, two unknowns.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E_{z \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)}\big[\text{SELU}(z)\big] &= 0 \\ \mathbb E_{z \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)}\big[\text{SELU}(z)^2\big] &= 1 \end{aligned} \]
  • It's attracting. The paper proves the map is a contraction near \((0, 1)\), so deviations shrink with every layer: activations settle at mean 0 and variance 1, and gradients can neither vanish nor explode.
  • Why it works: \(\lambda > 1\) makes the positive side stretch the variance when it's too small, and the negative side, saturating at \(-\lambda\alpha \approx -1.76\), damps it when it's too large.

What it requires (break any one and the guarantee is gone):

  • LeCun normal initialization, \(w \sim \mathcal N(0, 1/n_{\text{in}})\). It makes \(\sum_j w_j^2\) close to 1 for every unit. \(\sum_j w_j\) is 0 only on average (it varies by about \(\pm 1\) from unit to unit), but it multiplies the input mean, which standardized inputs keep near 0.
  • Standardized inputs, mean 0 and variance 1.
  • AlphaDropout instead of dropout. Ordinary dropout sets units to 0, which isn't SELU's resting value, and it shifts the mean and variance. AlphaDropout sets dropped units to the negative saturation value \(\alpha' = -\lambda\alpha\), then applies an affine correction that restores mean 0 and variance 1.
  • A plain feed-forward stack. Residual additions and normalization layers change the variance between layers and break the fixed-point argument, one reason SELU never caught on in CNNs or transformers.

AlphaDropout, with a random keep-mask \(m\) and keep probability \(r\); \(a\) and \(b\) are exactly the corrections that restore mean 0 and variance 1 when \(x\) has them:

\[ \begin{aligned} y &= a\,\big(x\, m + \alpha'(1 - m)\big) + b \\ m &\sim \text{Bernoulli}(r) \\ a &= \big(r + \alpha'^2\, r\,(1 - r)\big)^{-1/2} \\ b &= -a\, \alpha'\, (1 - r) \end{aligned} \]

ReLU6, hard-sigmoid and hard-swish for mobile

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{ReLU6}(z) &= \min\big(\max(0, z),\, 6\big) \\ \text{hardsigmoid}(z) &= \text{ReLU6}(z + 3) / 6 \\ \text{hardswish}(z) &= z \cdot \text{ReLU6}(z + 3) / 6 \end{aligned} \]
  • ReLU6 (used in MobileNetV1 and V2) caps outputs at 6, so the activation range is known in advance, which keeps 8-bit fixed-point quantization accurate.
  • Hard-sigmoid and hard-swish (MobileNetV3) are piecewise-linear stand-ins for \(\sigma\) and SiLU: no exponential, cheap on phone CPUs, and quantization-friendly. Hard-swish's slope is 0 below \(-3\), \((2z + 3)/6\) between \(-3\) and 3, and 1 above 3.

Maxout

\[ h(x) = \max_{j = 1, \dots, k}\, \big(w_j^\top x + b_j\big) \]

A maxout unit takes the largest of \(k\) linear functions of its input (Goodfellow et al. 2013), so it learns its own convex, piecewise-linear activation. ReLU is the case \(k = 2\) with one piece fixed at 0, and Leaky ReLU is \(\max(\alpha z, z)\). The gradient flows to whichever piece wins, and some piece always wins, so a maxout unit can't die. The price is \(k\) times the parameters per unit. It was designed to pair with dropout and is now rare.

Hard thresholds: the straight-through estimator

A step function or sign, used to binarize weights or activations, has slope 0 almost everywhere, so backprop gets nothing through it. The straight-through estimator uses the hard function forward and pretends it was the identity backward, usually only where \(\lvert z \rvert \le 1\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{forward: } y &= \operatorname{sign}(z) \\ \text{backward: } \frac{\partial L}{\partial z} &\approx \frac{\partial L}{\partial y}\; \mathbf 1\big[\lvert z \rvert \le 1\big] \end{aligned} \]

Quantization-aware training treats rounding the same way. For sampling a discrete category, Gumbel-softmax adds Gumbel noise to the logits and takes a softmax at temperature \(T\): a differentiable stand-in for a one-hot sample, which approaches a true one-hot sample as \(T \to 0\) (temperature is covered in section 07).

05Smooth activations: GELU, SiLU and Mish

Write ReLU as \(z\) times a hard switch. The smooth versions replace the switch with a soft one between 0 and 1:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{ReLU}(z) &= z \cdot \mathbf 1[z > 0] \\ \text{GELU}(z) &= z \cdot \Phi(z) \\ \text{SiLU}(z) &= z \cdot \sigma(z) \end{aligned} \]

GELU

Here \(\Phi\) is the standard normal CDF and \(\varphi\) is its density:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Phi(z) &= \tfrac12\big[1 + \operatorname{erf}(z/\sqrt2)\big] \\ \varphi'(z) &= -z\, \varphi(z) \end{aligned} \]

By the product rule:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{GELU}'(z) &= \Phi(z) + z\,\varphi(z) \\ \text{GELU}''(z) &= 2\,\varphi(z) + z\,\varphi'(z) \\ &= \varphi(z)\,\big(2 - z^2\big) \end{aligned} \]
  • The slope peaks at about 1.13 at \(z = \sqrt2\) and bottoms out at about \(-0.13\) at \(z = -\sqrt2\).
  • The function bottoms out at about \(-0.17\), near \(z = -0.75\).
  • The tails: far right it's \(z\); far left it goes to 0 at a Gaussian rate, faster than any exponential.

The stochastic-regularizer reading. Hendrycks and Gimpel (2016) derived GELU as the expected value of a random mask whose keep-probability depends on the input:

\[ \begin{aligned} m &\sim \text{Bernoulli}\big(\Phi(z)\big) \\ \mathbb E[\, z\, m \,] &= z\, \Phi(z) \\ &= \text{GELU}(z) \end{aligned} \]
  • Dropout keeps a unit with a fixed probability, whatever its value.
  • ReLU keeps it with probability 1 if it's positive and 0 if not.
  • GELU keeps it with probability \(\Phi(z)\): the larger \(z\) is compared with a standard normal, which normalized pre-activations roughly are, the more likely it's kept. Taking the expectation makes it deterministic. There's no noise in practice; the reading explains the shape.

The tanh approximation. Early implementations, including OpenAI's GPT and GPT-2, used a cheaper tanh form:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{GELU}(z) &\approx \tfrac12\, z\, \big(1 + \tanh u\big) \\ u &= \sqrt{2/\pi}\, \big(z + 0.044715\, z^3\big) \end{aligned} \]
  • Where it comes from: it replaces \(\operatorname{erf}(z/\sqrt2)\) with a tanh. The \(\sqrt{2/\pi}\) matches erf's slope at 0, and the cubic term bends the tails. Matching the Taylor series at 0 would give a cubic coefficient of \(2/(3\pi) - 1/6 \approx 0.0455\); the standard 0.044715 is tuned for accuracy over a wider range.
  • Accuracy: its largest error is about \(5 \times 10^{-4}\). A cruder version, \(z\,\sigma(1.702z)\), is off by up to about 0.02.
  • In PyTorch: nn.GELU(approximate="tanh"). Use whichever form a pretrained model was trained with.

Why BERT and GPT use it. The honest answer is empirical and historical:

  • The GELU paper reported small, consistent gains over ReLU and ELU on vision, speech and language tasks. GPT (2018) adopted it, BERT (2018) followed GPT, and later models (GPT-2 and 3, ViT) kept it. The original 2017 transformer used ReLU.
  • Controlled comparisons in transformer feed-forward blocks later found GELU roughly level with ReLU (within noise on perplexity, a little ahead on some downstream tasks), and gated variants clearly ahead of both (Shazeer 2020; Narang et al. 2021).
  • The mechanisms usually offered: it's smooth, so the gradient changes continuously instead of jumping at 0; moderately negative inputs still get gradient, so units rarely die; the small dip lets a unit say “slightly no” instead of only “no”; and with LayerNorm keeping pre-activations near unit scale, the inputs land in exactly the curved region where these differences matter.
  • Any gain over ReLU is small. “It works at least as well, and its smoothness may help optimization” is accurate; “it's fundamentally better” isn't.

SiLU, also called Swish

\[ \text{Swish}_\beta(z) = z\, \sigma(\beta z) \qquad \text{SiLU}(z) = z\, \sigma(z) \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{SiLU}'(z) &= \sigma(z) + z\, \sigma(z)\, \big(1 - \sigma(z)\big) \\ &= \sigma(z)\, \big(1 + z\, (1 - \sigma(z))\big) \end{aligned} \]
  • Found by search. Ramachandran, Zoph and Le (2017) searched automatically over small compositions of unary and binary functions. \(z\sigma(\beta z)\) came out on top and gave small gains over ReLU on ImageNet. The same function had already been named SiLU in the GELU paper (Hendrycks and Gimpel 2016) and used by Elfwing et al. (2017) in reinforcement learning.
  • \(\beta\) interpolates: \(\beta = 0\) gives the linear \(z/2\), and \(\beta \to \infty\) gives ReLU. GELU is close to \(\beta \approx 1.7\), which is why GELU hugs ReLU more tightly than SiLU does.
  • Self-gated: the input gates itself through \(\sigma\). Gated linear units (section 06) give the gate its own projection instead.
  • The non-monotonic bump: the minimum is about \(-0.28\) at \(z \approx -1.28\), and the slope ranges from about \(-0.10\) to 1.10. Left of the minimum the slope is negative: raising \(z\) lowers the output. That's harmless for gradient descent, and the bump keeps gradients alive for slightly negative inputs.
  • The left tail decays like \(z e^{z}\), slower than GELU's, so SiLU passes more gradient to strongly negative inputs.
  • Used in EfficientNet, the YOLOv5 family of detectors, and inside SwiGLU.

Mish

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Mish}(z) &= z \tanh\big(\text{softplus}(z)\big) \\ \text{softplus}(z) &= \log\big(1 + e^{z}\big) \end{aligned} \]

The same family: smooth, non-monotonic (minimum about \(-0.31\) near \(z = -1.19\)), ReLU-like far from 0. YOLOv4 used it. It costs an exp, a log and a tanh, and its gains over SiLU are small, so it's rarely the default.

Trap

“GELU and SiLU units can't die.” Their slope is 0 only at a single point, so a unit can't be stuck over a whole region the way a ReLU can. But far to the left their slopes are tiny (GELU's falls off like a Gaussian), so a unit pushed far negative is nearly stuck in practice.

06Gated linear units: GLU, GEGLU and SwiGLU

A gated linear unit makes two projections of the same input and multiplies them element by element. One goes through an activation and acts as the gate; the other is a plain linear “value”:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{GLU}(x) &= \sigma(xW + b) \odot (xV + c) \\ \text{ReGLU}(x) &= \text{ReLU}(xW) \odot xV \\ \text{GEGLU}(x) &= \text{GELU}(xW) \odot xV \\ \text{SwiGLU}(x) &= \text{SiLU}(xW) \odot xV \end{aligned} \]

GLU comes from gated convolutional language models (Dauphin et al. 2017). Shazeer (2020) swapped the sigmoid for other activations and used the result as the transformer's feed-forward block. This section uses the convention of code: tokens are rows, so a layer is written \(xW\) rather than \(Wx\).

The SwiGLU feed-forward block

The plain transformer feed-forward network (FFN), and the SwiGLU version, with biases dropped as in most LLMs:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{FFN}(x) &= \text{GELU}(x W_1)\, W_2 \\ \text{FFN}_{\text{SwiGLU}}(x) &= \big(\text{SiLU}(xW) \odot xV\big)\, W_2 \end{aligned} \]
TensorShapeNote
\(x\)\(N \times d\)\(N\) = batch × sequence length; each token is handled separately
\(W\) (gate), \(V\) (value, or “up”)\(d \times d_{ff}\)Two separate projections of the same \(x\)
\(xW\), \(xV\), \(\text{SiLU}(xW) \odot xV\)\(N \times d_{ff}\)The element-wise product keeps the shape
\(W_2\) (“down”)\(d_{ff} \times d\)Back to the model width
Output\(N \times d\)Added to the residual stream

Why the hidden size is two-thirds of 4d

The plain block has two \(d \times d_{ff}\) matrices, with \(d_{ff} = 4d\); SwiGLU has three. To keep the parameter count, and with it the compute per token, the same:

\[ \begin{aligned} P_{\text{plain}} &= 2\, d\, d_{ff} \\ &= 8\, d^2 \qquad (d_{ff} = 4d) \\ P_{\text{SwiGLU}} &= 3\, d\, d_{ff}' \\ 3\, d\, d_{ff}' &= 8\, d^2 \\ d_{ff}' &= \tfrac{8}{3}\, d \\ &= \tfrac{2}{3} \cdot 4d \end{aligned} \]
  • Equal parameters mean equal FLOPs. A matrix multiply costs about two FLOPs per parameter per token; the extra element-wise product is negligible. Shazeer's comparisons were made this way, at equal compute, which is why the gains count.
  • In practice: LLaMA rounds \(\tfrac83 d\) up to a multiple of 256 (its 7B model has \(d = 4096\) and \(d_{ff} = 11008\)). PaLM kept \(d_{ff} = 4d\) and simply paid for the third matrix. Later models such as Mistral 7B and Llama 3 8B use \(3.5d\).

Why gating helps

  • Multiplicative interactions. A plain FFN unit is a function of one projection, \(f(w \cdot x)\). A gated unit is a product of two, \(\text{SiLU}(w \cdot x)\,(v \cdot x)\). Near the origin that's a quadratic form in \(x\), so each unit directly computes a second-order interaction that a plain FFN has to assemble from many units. Even with no activation at all, the bilinear block \((xW \odot xV)W_2\) is non-linear, and in Shazeer's comparisons it beat the plain ReLU FFN.
  • Input-dependent feature selection. The gate decides, per token, how much of each value feature passes, like an LSTM gate or an attention weight, instead of applying one fixed bend.
  • A clean gradient path. The value branch is linear, so its gradient is just the gate's value, not multiplied by an activation's slope. This was Dauphin et al.'s argument for GLU over tanh-gated units. With \(h = \text{SiLU}(xW) \odot xV\), element by element:
\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial h}{\partial (xV)} &= \text{SiLU}(xW) \\ \frac{\partial h}{\partial (xW)} &= \text{SiLU}'(xW) \odot xV \end{aligned} \]

None of this is a proof. Shazeer credited the gains, “as all else, to divine benevolence”. The evidence is empirical, and it has held up at scale.

Where it's used

  • SwiGLU: PaLM, LLaMA 1–3, Mistral, Qwen and most open LLMs since 2023.
  • GEGLU: T5 v1.1 and Gemma.
  • Plain GELU FFN: encoders such as BERT and ViT, and GPT-2 and 3.

What if the FFN had no activation?

The plain block collapses to \(x W_1 W_2\), a single \(d \times d\) linear map, with \(8d^2\) parameters doing the work of \(d^2\). Each token's processing becomes linear, and the FFN loses its role as a per-token store of learned features. The transformer as a whole stays non-linear, because attention's softmax, the product of attention weights with values, and LayerNorm are all non-linear in \(x\), but it's much weaker. For SwiGLU, removing SiLU leaves the bilinear block, which is still non-linear and still trains reasonably.

ReLU's comeback: activation sparsity

Smooth activations and SwiGLU almost never output exactly 0, so every row of \(W_2\) is used for every token. ReLU's exact zeros let inference skip the rows of \(W_2\) whose activations are 0. Two lines of work bring ReLU back for that reason:

  • Squared ReLU, \(\text{ReLU}(z)^2\), came out of an architecture search for transformers (Primer, So et al. 2021) and improved on ReLU and GELU in that setting.
  • “ReLUfication” swaps a pretrained LLM's GELU or SiLU for ReLU and fine-tunes briefly. Most FFN activations then become exactly 0, which speeds up inference with little loss in quality (Mirzadeh et al. 2023).

07Output activations and losses

Hidden activations are about gradient flow. The output activation is about the range of the target, and it comes paired with a loss: the negative log-likelihood of the distribution the output describes. For the standard pairs, the gradient at the logits is simply prediction minus target.

TargetOutput and lossGradient at the logit

Sigmoid with binary cross-entropy

Two log identities do the work:

\[ \begin{aligned} \log \sigma(z) &= z - \log\big(1 + e^{z}\big) \\ \log\big(1 - \sigma(z)\big) &= -\log\big(1 + e^{z}\big) \end{aligned} \]

Substituting them:

\[ \begin{aligned} L &= -y \log \sigma(z) - (1 - y) \log\big(1 - \sigma(z)\big) \\ &= \log\big(1 + e^{z}\big) - y\, z \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial z} &= \sigma(z) - y \end{aligned} \]

The log's steep slope near 0 exactly cancels the sigmoid's flat tail, so a confidently wrong prediction still gets a gradient of nearly \(\pm 1\).

Trap: a sigmoid output with MSE

Here the slope doesn't cancel:

\[ \begin{aligned} L &= \tfrac12\big(\sigma(z) - y\big)^2 \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial z} &= \big(\sigma(z) - y\big)\, \sigma'(z) \\ &= \big(\sigma(z) - y\big)\, \sigma(z)\, \big(1 - \sigma(z)\big) \end{aligned} \]

The extra \(\sigma(1 - \sigma)\) is near 0 whenever the output is saturated, including saturated at the wrong answer, so learning stalls exactly when the model is most wrong. The complaint that sigmoids make gradients vanish applies to hidden layers and to MSE outputs, not to sigmoid with cross-entropy.

Softmax with cross-entropy

\[ p_i = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_k e^{z_k}} \]

Softmax is the one activation that isn't element-wise: each output depends on every logit, because the classes compete for one unit of probability. Its Jacobian is easiest through the log:

\[ \begin{aligned} \log p_i &= z_i - \log \textstyle\sum_k e^{z_k} \\ \frac{\partial \log p_i}{\partial z_j} &= \delta_{ij} - p_j \\ \frac{\partial p_i}{\partial z_j} &= p_i\, \big(\delta_{ij} - p_j\big) \end{aligned} \]

In matrix form:

\[ J = \operatorname{diag}(p) - p\, p^\top \]
  • Symmetric and positive semi-definite, with rank \(K - 1\).
  • Every row sums to 0. Adding the same constant to every logit changes nothing: \(\operatorname{softmax}(z + c\mathbf 1) = \operatorname{softmax}(z)\). That's why implementations subtract \(\max z\) before exponentiating: no overflow, same answer.
  • \(J \to 0\) as \(p\) becomes one-hot. A saturated softmax passes no gradient through itself, just like a saturated sigmoid. This matters in attention (section 08).
  • The sigmoid is the two-class case. With logits \((z, 0)\):
\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{softmax}\big((z, 0)\big)_1 &= \frac{e^{z}}{e^{z} + 1} \\ &= \sigma(z) \end{aligned} \]

With cross-entropy, the Jacobian collapses to prediction minus target. The last step uses \(\sum_i y_i = 1\), which also holds with label smoothing:

\[ \begin{aligned} L &= -\textstyle\sum_i y_i \log p_i \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_j} &= -\textstyle\sum_i y_i\, \big(\delta_{ij} - p_j\big) \\ &= -y_j + p_j \textstyle\sum_i y_i \\ &= p_j - y_j \end{aligned} \]

Temperature

\[ p_i(T) = \frac{e^{z_i / T}}{\sum_k e^{z_k / T}} \]
  • \(T \to 0\): differences between logits are magnified, and \(p\) becomes one-hot on the largest logit (split evenly among ties): the hard argmax.
  • \(T \to \infty\): differences vanish, and \(p\) becomes uniform, \(1/K\) each.
  • The ranking never changes. \(T\) changes confidence, not the argmax. That's why temperature scaling, fitting one \(T\) on validation data, calibrates a classifier without touching its accuracy.
  • Gradient: with a temperature, the cross-entropy gradient at the logits is \((p - y)/T\), and a small \(T\) also saturates \(p\), so very low temperatures make training brittle.
  • Distillation trains a student on the teacher's softmax at \(T > 1\), which exposes the relative sizes of the wrong-class probabilities. The soft loss is multiplied by \(T^2\), because its gradients shrink like \(1/T^2\) (Hinton et al. 2015).
  • Sampling from a language model at \(T < 1\) is more conservative; at \(T > 1\), more diverse.

Why “softmax”: log-sum-exp is a smooth maximum, and the softmax is its gradient. The bounds below squeeze it onto the max as \(T \to 0\), so its gradient becomes the one-hot argmax. A more accurate name would be “soft argmax”.

\[ \begin{aligned} T \log \textstyle\sum_k e^{z_k / T} &\ge \max_k z_k \\ T \log \textstyle\sum_k e^{z_k / T} &\le \max_k z_k + T \log K \end{aligned} \]

Softplus for positive outputs

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{softplus}(z) &= \log\big(1 + e^{z}\big) \\ \text{softplus}'(z) &= \sigma(z) \end{aligned} \]

Softplus is strictly positive and smooth: close to \(z\) for large \(z\), and close to \(e^{z}\) for very negative \(z\). Use it when an output must be positive: a standard deviation or variance, a rate, a scale. For example, a network that predicts a mean \(\mu\) and a variance \(v\), trained with the Gaussian negative log-likelihood:

\[ \begin{aligned} L &= \tfrac12 \log v + \frac{(y - \mu)^2}{2\, v} \\ v &= \text{softplus}(z_v) + \epsilon \end{aligned} \]
  • Why not exp: exp grows exponentially, so one large logit gives a huge variance and huge gradients. Softplus grows linearly.
  • Why the \(\epsilon\): without a floor the variance can collapse toward 0 on a point the mean fits exactly, sending the loss toward \(-\infty\).
  • The alternative: predict \(\log v\) directly with an identity output. Also common, and fine with care.

Multi-class or multi-label

  • Multi-class (exactly one true class): softmax with cross-entropy. Raising one class's probability necessarily lowers the others'.
  • Multi-label (any subset can be true, like tags or diagnoses): one sigmoid per label, each with its own BCE. The labels don't compete, and the probabilities needn't sum to 1. A softmax would force true labels to split one unit of probability between them.

Why you pass logits to the loss

  • Numerical stability. In float32, \(\sigma(z)\) rounds to exactly 1 once \(z\) is above about 17, so \(\log(1 - \sigma(z))\) becomes \(\log 0\). The logit form computes softplus stably as \(\max(z, 0) + \log(1 + e^{-\lvert z \rvert})\), and cross-entropy uses log-sum-exp with the max subtracted. Nothing overflows, and nothing hits \(\log 0\).
  • An exact gradient. The fused loss returns \(p - y\) directly, instead of multiplying a huge \(1/p\) by a tiny \(\sigma'\) that may already have underflowed.
  • No double activation. PyTorch's CrossEntropyLoss and BCEWithLogitsLoss apply log-softmax or log-sigmoid inside. Feeding them probabilities applies the activation twice: there's no error, the model just trains badly. (NLLLoss expects log-probabilities from log_softmax.)
  • Low precision. In float16, \(e^{z}\) overflows once \(z\) is above about 11 (the largest float16 is 65504), so mixed-precision training computes softmax, log-softmax and the loss in float32; PyTorch's autocast does this for you. bfloat16 has float32's range but much less precision.
  • Apply the sigmoid or softmax only when you need probabilities, at inference.

08Activations and training dynamics

Initialization from variance propagation

Take one unit, \(z = \sum_{j=1}^{n} w_j\, a_j\), with \(n\) inputs. Assume the weights are independent, have mean 0, and are independent of the inputs. Then the terms are uncorrelated with mean 0, and their variances add:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(z) &= \sum_{j=1}^{n} \operatorname{Var}(w_j\, a_j) \\ &= n\, \Big(\mathbb E[w^2]\, \mathbb E[a^2] - \big(\mathbb E[w]\, \mathbb E[a]\big)^2\Big) \\ &= n\, \operatorname{Var}(w)\, \mathbb E[a^2] \end{aligned} \]

Note \(\mathbb E[a^2]\), the second moment, not \(\operatorname{Var}(a)\): ReLU and sigmoid outputs don't have mean 0. The signal keeps its size when this equals the previous layer's pre-activation variance:

\[ n\, \operatorname{Var}(w)\, \frac{\mathbb E[a^2]}{\operatorname{Var}(z_{\text{prev}})} = 1 \]

The ratio \(\mathbb E[a^2] / \operatorname{Var}(z_{\text{prev}})\) depends only on the activation, so each activation gets its own weight variance. For ReLU, with \(z\) symmetric around 0 (second line) and mean 0 (third line):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E\big[\text{ReLU}(z)^2\big] &= \mathbb E\big[z^2\, \mathbf 1[z > 0]\big] \\ &= \tfrac12\, \mathbb E\big[z^2\big] \\ &= \tfrac12\, \operatorname{Var}(z) \end{aligned} \]
Activation\(\mathbb E[a^2] / \operatorname{Var}(z)\)\(\operatorname{Var}(w)\)

tanh, balancing both passesAbout 1\(2/(n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}})\) (Xavier, or Glorot)

  • Why it matters so much: over \(L\) layers the variance is multiplied by that factor \(L\) times. Anything other than 1 shrinks or grows exponentially before training has even started.
  • Xavier's \(2/(n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}})\): the backward pass sends the error through \(W^\top\), which sums over \(n_{\text{out}}\) terms, so preserving the gradient's variance wants \(1/n_{\text{out}}\) (for tanh near 0, where the slope is about 1). Glorot and Bengio averaged the two. As a uniform distribution the limits are \(\pm\sqrt{6/(n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}})}\), since a uniform on \([-a, a]\) has variance \(a^2/3\).
  • He, fan-in or fan-out: for ReLU the backward condition is the same with \(n_{\text{out}}\) (the mean squared slope is also 1/2). He et al. showed either one suffices: the other pass is then off by a constant factor overall, not an exponential one.
  • Sigmoid fits none of these. Its output mean is 1/2, so \(\mathbb E[a^2]\) is at least 1/4 even when \(z = 0\), and its slope at 0 is only 1/4. One more reason it's kept for gates and outputs.
  • Defaults: PyTorch's nn.Linear starts with \(\operatorname{Var}(w) = 1/(3 n_{\text{in}})\), smaller than He. ReLU codebases often call kaiming_normal_ explicitly; calculate_gain gives \(\sqrt2\) for ReLU and 5/3 for tanh.

Where normalization goes

  • Linear or conv → BatchNorm → ReLU is the standard order. The BatchNorm paper normalized pre-activations, which are closer to Gaussian than half-rectified outputs. Normalizing \(z\) every batch keeps each unit centred on ReLU's corner, so it fires on about half the batch, and keeps tanh and sigmoid inputs out of their flat tails. BatchNorm's learned shift \(\beta\) then sets each channel's firing threshold. A bias before BatchNorm is redundant: BatchNorm subtracts it.
  • Pre-activation ResNets (He et al. 2016) order each residual branch BatchNorm → ReLU → conv and leave the skip path untouched. The original ResNet applied a ReLU after the addition, putting a bend on the identity path; removing it gave a clean gradient highway and trained deeper networks better.
  • Transformers put the activation only inside the FFN branch: \(x + W_2\, f\big(W_1\, \text{LN}(x)\big)\) in the pre-LN layout. The residual stream itself never passes through an activation. A ReLU on the stream would zero part of the representation and mask the identity path's gradient.
  • LayerNorm or RMSNorm before the FFN keeps its pre-activations near unit scale, which is where GELU's and SiLU's curved region lies.

What the backward pass must store

  • ReLU needs only which units were on, and its output already says that, so it can run in place.
  • Sigmoid and tanh need only their output, which is kept anyway (their slopes are written in terms of it).
  • GELU and SiLU need their input, a full extra tensor per layer.
  • A SwiGLU block keeps its input and both \(N \times d_{ff}\) projections, and often the gated product too, so FFN activations are a large share of training memory in LLMs.
  • The remedies: activation checkpointing (store less, recompute during the backward pass) and fused kernels that compute an activation and its slope without materializing intermediates.

Activation statistics as a debugging tool

Log these per layer every few hundred steps (forward hooks in PyTorch):

  • the mean and standard deviation of the pre-activations;
  • for ReLU, the fraction of units that never fired over a window of batches (dead), and the fraction of zeros per batch (sparsity);
  • for tanh, the fraction of outputs beyond \(\pm 0.99\); for sigmoid, below 0.01 or above 0.99 (saturated);
  • the gradient norm per layer, and the ratio of update size to weight size (around \(10^{-3}\) is a common healthy range).

Attention's softmax saturates too: the square root of dk

Attention scores are dot products of queries and keys. If their components are independent with mean 0 and variance 1:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(q \cdot k) &= \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} \operatorname{Var}(q_i\, k_i) \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} \mathbb E\big[q_i^2\big]\, \mathbb E\big[k_i^2\big] \\ &= d_k \end{aligned} \]

So raw scores have a standard deviation of \(\sqrt{d_k}\), which grows with the head size. Large scores push each row's softmax toward one-hot, its Jacobian \(\operatorname{diag}(p) - pp^\top\) goes to 0, and the queries and keys stop getting gradient. It's the sigmoid's saturation problem again, inside attention. Dividing by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) restores unit variance, the same variance-preserving logic as initialization:

\[ \text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \operatorname{softmax}\Big(\frac{Q K^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\Big)\, V \]

Equivalently, \(\sqrt{d_k}\) is a fixed softmax temperature. During training, query and key norms can still grow and re-saturate attention in large models. The fixes are normalizing queries and keys (QK-norm, used in ViT-22B and several recent LLMs) and tanh soft-capping of the scores.

def relu_fwd(z):
    mask = z > 0                 # cache: which units were on
    return z * mask, mask

def relu_bwd(dy, mask):
    return dy * mask             # slope 1 where on, 0 where off

def gelu_fwd(z):
    u = C * (z + 0.044715 * z**3)
    t = tanh(u)
    return 0.5 * z * (1 + t), (z, t)

def gelu_bwd(dy, cache):
    z, t = cache
    du = C * (1 + 3 * 0.044715 * z**2)     # du/dz
    dz = 0.5 * (1 + t) + 0.5 * z * (1 - t**2) * du
    return dy * dz                         # product rule, tanh' = 1 - t^2

SiLU

def silu_fwd(z):
    s = sigmoid(z)

def silu_bwd(dy, cache):
    z, s = cache
    return dy * s * (1 + z * (1 - s))      # sigma (1 + z (1 - sigma))

SwiGLU feed-forward block, with shapes

# X: (N, d), N = batch * seq_len
# W, V: (d, f)   W2: (f, d)   f = d_ff, about 8d/3
def swiglu_fwd(X, W, V, W2):
    G = X @ W                    # (N, f) gate pre-activation
    U = X @ V                    # (N, f) value branch
    S = sigmoid(G)
    A = G * S                    # (N, f) SiLU(gate)
    H = A * U                    # (N, f) gated hidden
    Y = H @ W2                   # (N, d)
    return Y, (X, G, S, A, U, H)

def swiglu_bwd(dY, cache, W, V, W2):
    X, G, S, A, U, H = cache
    dW2 = H.T @ dY               # (f, d)
    dH = dY @ W2.T               # (N, f)
    dA = dH * U                  # product rule: each factor
    dU = dH * A                  #   gets the other one
    dG = dA * S * (1 + G * (1 - S))   # SiLU slope
    dW = X.T @ dG                # (d, f)
    dV = X.T @ dU                # (d, f)
    dX = dG @ W.T + dU @ V.T     # (N, d): X feeds both branches
    return dX, dW, dV, dW2

The forward and backward passes above were checked against finite differences. Note the two terms in dX: \(X\) is used twice, so its gradient is the sum over both paths.

A dead-unit and saturation monitor (PyTorch)

alive, sat = {}, {}              # layer name -> stats

def watch(name):
    def hook(module, inp, out):  # out: (..., units)
        flat = (out > 0).reshape(-1, out.shape[-1])
        on = flat.any(dim=0)               # fired on any row
        alive[name] = alive.get(name, on) | on
    return hook

def watch_tanh(name):
    def hook(module, inp, out):  # saturated share
        frac = (out.abs() > 0.99).float().mean().item()
        sat.setdefault(name, []).append(frac)
    return hook

for name, m in model.named_modules():
    if isinstance(m, nn.ReLU):
        m.register_forward_hook(watch(name))
    if isinstance(m, nn.Tanh):
        m.register_forward_hook(watch_tanh(name))

# run ~50 batches under torch.no_grad(), then:
for name, a in alive.items():
    print(name, "dead:", 1 - a.float().mean().item())
for name, f in sat.items():
    print(name, "saturated:", sum(f) / len(f))
alive.clear(); sat.clear()       # start a new window
  • Dead means never fired over the whole window, not zero on one batch.
  • Units on the last axis: the reshape handles both \(B \times \text{units}\) and transformer-style \(B \times T \times \text{units}\).
  • For conv layers (out of shape \(B \times C \times H \times W\)), the units are channels on axis 1, so reduce over every other axis: (out > 0).any(dim=(0, 2, 3)).
  • Hooks see modules, not calls. A single nn.ReLU reused in several places mixes their statistics, and functional F.relu calls aren't hooked at all. In those cases, hook the preceding linear layer and test its output, the pre-activation, for \(> 0\).

11Interview questions

Answer out loud first, then open the answer.

1Why do networks need non-linear activations? What does a deep network without them compute?

Each layer would be affine, and a composition of affine maps is affine:

\[ \begin{aligned} y &= W_2(W_1 x + b_1) + b_2 \\ &= (W_2 W_1)\, x + (W_2 b_1 + b_2) \\ &= W' x + b' \end{aligned} \]

So any depth is one linear layer: linear or logistic regression, with flat decision boundaries and no XOR. Training is still non-convex, and a narrow layer limits the rank, but the set of functions never grows. The activation is what lets each layer build new features from the last layer's. (01)

2What does the universal approximation theorem promise, and what doesn't it?

One hidden layer with any continuous, non-polynomial activation can approximate any continuous function on a closed, bounded region as closely as you like, given enough units. It doesn't bound the number of units (exponential in the input dimension for some functions), doesn't say gradient descent will find the weights, says nothing about generalizing from finite data, and nothing outside that region. It's an existence result; the practical case for depth is efficiency. (01)

3If one hidden layer is enough, why go deep?

Efficiency. A ReLU network is piecewise linear. On a 1-D input, one hidden layer of \(n\) units makes at most \(n + 1\) pieces, but each extra layer can fold the input and double the pieces: the tent map composed \(L\) times gives \(2^L\) pieces from \(2L\) units. Some functions need exponentially many units in a shallow network and only polynomially many in a deep one. Depth also reuses features hierarchically. The caveat: trained networks use far fewer regions than the theoretical maximum. (01)

4Can a network with only ReLUs approximate a smooth function?

Yes, as closely as you like on a bounded region. ReLU isn't a polynomial, so the universal approximation theorem applies, and deep ReLU nets approximate \(x^2\), and from it products and polynomials, with error falling exponentially in depth. But the result is always piecewise linear: its slope is piecewise constant and its second derivative is 0 almost everywhere. If you need smooth derivatives of the output with respect to the input (physics-informed losses, forces as gradients of an energy), use a smooth activation such as tanh, SiLU or softplus. (01)

5Why not use sigmoid in hidden layers?
  • Small slope: \(\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))\), at most 1/4, so backprop shrinks the error by at least 4× per layer from the slopes alone, about \(4^{-L}\) over \(L\) layers.
  • Saturation: in the tails the slope is about \(e^{-\lvert z\rvert}\), so saturated units get no gradient.
  • Not zero-centred: outputs are all positive (mean about 0.5), so a unit's weight gradients share one sign per example (zig-zag), and the next layer's loss surface is badly conditioned.
  • Cost: an exponential per unit.

It's fine at the output with BCE, and as a gate. (02)

6Derive tanh′, and show tanh(z) = 2σ(2z) − 1. Why is tanh better than sigmoid in hidden layers?
\[ \begin{aligned} \tanh' z &= 1 - \tanh^2 z \\ 2\sigma(2z) - 1 &= \frac{1 - e^{-2z}}{1 + e^{-2z}} \\ &= \tanh z \end{aligned} \]

So tanh is a sigmoid stretched and centred: the same function class, since the rescaling folds into weights and biases. It's better for optimization: four times the peak slope (1 against 1/4), and zero-centred outputs, so the weights into a unit can move in different directions on one example. It still saturates, with a slope of about \(4e^{-2\lvert z\rvert}\) in the tails. (02)

7Why does ReLU train faster than tanh? What is its derivative at 0?

On active units the slope is exactly 1, so the activation never shrinks the backpropagated error; it doesn't saturate for positive inputs; about half the units are off, so representations are sparse; and it's a comparison forward and a mask backward. AlexNet reported about 6× faster training to the same error. Why so late, then? Before about 2010 networks were shallow, where vanishing gradients barely matter, and smooth bounded activations seemed safer; ReLU paid off once networks got deep. At 0 there's a corner: any value in \([0, 1]\) is a subgradient. Frameworks use 0. It rarely matters in float32, but in low precision exact zeros are common enough that the choice can change training. (03)

8What is a dead ReLU? How do you detect and fix it?

A unit whose pre-activation is negative on every input: its output and slope are 0 everywhere, so all its weight and bias gradients are exactly 0, and it never recovers unless the layers below shift its inputs. The usual cause is a too-large update (high learning rate, outlier batch) driving the bias negative; non-negative inputs from earlier ReLUs make all-negative weights fatal. Detect: per unit, track whether it fired at least once over many batches, and plot the dead fraction per layer over training. Fix: lower learning rate or warmup, He initialization, BatchNorm before the ReLU, gradient clipping, or Leaky ReLU, ELU or GELU. (03)

9What happens if all of a layer's pre-activations are negative?

For one example: a ReLU layer outputs all zeros, the next layer sees only its biases, and no gradient flows below this layer for that example. For every example: the network's output is constant, every layer below gets zero gradient for good, and the loss sits at the best constant prediction. With Leaky ReLU the layer still passes \(\alpha\) times the gradient; with GELU or SiLU small negative outputs and gradients survive, though they're tiny far from 0. With sigmoid or tanh, strongly negative pre-activations give the same failure as saturation: outputs pinned near 0 or \(-1\), with slopes near 0. (03)

10Derive He initialization. Why the factor 2?

For \(z = \sum_j w_j a_j\) with \(n\) independent, mean-zero weights, the variances of the terms add (first line). For ReLU and a symmetric previous pre-activation, half the mass is zeroed (second line):

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(z) &= n\, \operatorname{Var}(w)\, \mathbb E[a^2] \\ &= n\, \operatorname{Var}(w) \cdot \tfrac12 \operatorname{Var}(z_{\text{prev}}) \end{aligned} \]

Setting \(\operatorname{Var}(z)\) equal to \(\operatorname{Var}(z_{\text{prev}})\):

\[ \operatorname{Var}(w) = \frac{2}{n} \]

The 2 puts back the half that ReLU removes; without it the variance halves at every layer. tanh, nearly linear near 0, gets \(1/n\) (or Xavier's \(2/(n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}})\) to balance the backward pass), and SELU gets LeCun normal. (08)

11Leaky ReLU, PReLU, ELU and SELU: what does each fix? What does SELU need?
  • Leaky ReLU: a fixed small negative slope, so units can't die.
  • PReLU: the negative slope is learned, usually per channel.
  • ELU: a smooth negative side that saturates at \(-\alpha\), pulling mean activations toward 0 and giving a noise-robust “off” value.
  • SELU: a scaled ELU whose constants (\(\lambda \approx 1.0507\), \(\alpha \approx 1.6733\)) make mean 0 and variance 1 an attracting fixed point, so activations self-normalize without BatchNorm.

SELU needs LeCun normal initialization, standardized inputs, AlphaDropout instead of dropout, and a plain feed-forward stack: residual additions and normalization layers break the argument. (04)

12What is GELU, and why do BERT and GPT use it?

\(\text{GELU}(z) = z\Phi(z)\): the expected value of \(z\) times a Bernoulli mask with keep-probability \(\Phi(z)\), an input-dependent blend of ReLU and dropout. It's smooth, ReLU-like far from 0, dips slightly negative, and keeps some gradient for moderately negative inputs. GPT adopted it after the GELU paper showed small gains over ReLU and ELU, BERT followed GPT, and later controlled comparisons in transformer FFNs found it roughly level with ReLU, with gated variants clearly ahead. Smoothness and fewer dead units are the usual explanations, but any gain over ReLU is small. The 2017 transformer used ReLU, and most recent LLMs moved on to SwiGLU. (05)

13GELU or SiLU? Is a non-monotonic activation a problem?

Both are \(z\) times a soft switch: \(\Phi(z)\) for GELU, \(\sigma(z)\) for SiLU. GELU is close to \(z\sigma(1.702z)\), so its switch is steeper and it hugs ReLU more closely; its left tail dies off at a Gaussian rate, while SiLU's decays like \(ze^{z}\). Both have a small dip (minimum about \(-0.17\) for GELU, \(-0.28\) for SiLU). Non-monotonicity isn't a problem: gradient descent doesn't need it, the negative-slope region is small, and the bump keeps gradients alive for slightly negative inputs. (05)

14What is SwiGLU, and why does it use 2/3 of the usual hidden size?

\(\text{FFN}(x) = (\text{SiLU}(xW) \odot xV)\, W_2\): a SiLU gate multiplies a linear value projection, element by element. That's three matrices instead of two. To match a plain FFN with \(d_{ff} = 4d\), which has \(8d^2\) parameters and the same FLOPs per token:

\[ \begin{aligned} 3\, d\, d_{ff}' &= 2\, d \cdot 4d \\ d_{ff}' &= \tfrac83\, d \end{aligned} \]

That's two-thirds of \(4d\). LLaMA rounds it up to a multiple of 256 (11008 for \(d = 4096\)). Shazeer's comparisons were at equal compute, so the gain isn't just extra parameters; PaLM kept \(4d\) and paid for the third matrix. (06)

15What happens if you remove the activation from a transformer's FFN?

The plain FFN becomes \(xW_1W_2\), one \(d \times d\) linear map, so each token's processing is linear and the FFN stops working as a per-token store of learned features. The model isn't linear overall, because attention (the softmax, and attention weights multiplying values) and LayerNorm are non-linear in \(x\), but it's much weaker. In SwiGLU, removing SiLU leaves the bilinear \((xW \odot xV)W_2\), which is still non-linear (quadratic) and trains reasonably. (06)

16Softmax temperature: what happens as T → 0 and T → ∞? What's the softmax Jacobian?

With \(p = \operatorname{softmax}(z/T)\): as \(T \to 0\), \(p\) becomes one-hot on the argmax (a hard max); as \(T \to \infty\), it becomes uniform, \(1/K\). The ranking never changes, only the confidence, so temperature scaling calibrates without changing accuracy, and LLM sampling uses it to trade predictability for diversity. The Jacobian is \(\operatorname{diag}(p) - pp^\top\): symmetric, rows summing to 0 (shift invariance), and going to 0 as \(p\) becomes one-hot, which is why a saturated softmax passes no gradient. With cross-entropy the gradient at the logits is \(p - y\), divided by \(T\) when there's a temperature. (07)

17Why pass logits rather than probabilities to the loss? Why not MSE on a sigmoid output?

Stability and an exact gradient. In float32, \(\sigma(z)\) rounds to 1 above \(z \approx 17\), so \(\log(1 - \sigma)\) becomes \(\log 0\); the logit form uses softplus and log-sum-exp with the max subtracted, and returns \(p - y\) directly. Passing probabilities to CrossEntropyLoss or BCEWithLogitsLoss applies the activation twice and silently trains badly. MSE on a sigmoid output has gradient \((\sigma - y)\,\sigma(1 - \sigma)\), which vanishes whenever the output is saturated, even at the wrong answer; cross-entropy's log cancels that factor. (07)

18Multi-class or multi-label output? And how would you output a variance?

Multi-class (exactly one label): softmax with cross-entropy; the classes compete. Multi-label (any subset): an independent sigmoid with BCE per label; the probabilities needn't sum to 1, and a softmax would force true labels to split probability. A variance, or any positive quantity: \(\text{softplus}(z) + \epsilon\) (linear growth, stable gradients, and a floor so the variance can't collapse), trained with the Gaussian NLL; or predict \(\log \sigma^2\) with an identity output. Counts: exp with a Poisson loss. (07)

19Why is attention scaled by the square root of dk?

With independent mean-0, variance-1 components, \(\operatorname{Var}(q \cdot k) = d_k\), so raw scores have standard deviation \(\sqrt{d_k}\). Large scores saturate the softmax toward one-hot, its Jacobian \(\operatorname{diag}(p) - pp^\top\) goes to 0, and the queries and keys stop learning: the same saturation that stalls sigmoid units. Dividing by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) restores unit variance. Not \(d_k\), because it's the variance that grows with \(d_k\), so the standard deviation grows with \(\sqrt{d_k}\). QK-norm and logit soft-capping handle the same problem when norms grow during training. (08)

20Why do LSTM gates use sigmoid and the candidate tanh? Could an RNN use ReLU?

A gate is a fraction to let through, so it needs \((0, 1)\), and saturation is useful for a gate (fully open or shut). The candidate and the cell output are signed values that must stay bounded in a loop applied hundreds of times, so they use tanh's \((-1, 1)\). The LSTM resists vanishing gradients through its additive cell update, not through its activations. A vanilla RNN can use ReLU only with care: it's unbounded, and with the same weights reused every step the state and gradients can explode; identity initialization of the recurrent weights (IRNN) plus gradient clipping makes it trainable. (02)

21Why is the standard block conv → BatchNorm → ReLU? What does BatchNorm do for ReLU?

BatchNorm re-standardizes each channel's pre-activations right before the ReLU at every step, so ReLU's on/off threshold stays inside the data.

  • No dead or purely linear units: without it, weight updates can drift a channel all negative (dead) or all positive (just linear). With it, \(\text{ReLU}(\gamma\hat z + \beta)\) fires when \(\hat z > -\beta/\gamma\): the network learns what fraction fires.
  • Steady scale through depth: He initialization fixes the scale only at step 0; BatchNorm keeps it fixed throughout training, so ReLU's unbounded outputs can't compound.
  • Higher learning rates: the output ignores the weights' scale, so the training is smoother and more tolerant.

Without it: shallow CNNs train fine with care; deep plain CNNs train slowly, need small learning rates, and lose more units to dying. Conv layers before BatchNorm drop their bias, since \(\beta\) replaces it. Transformers use LayerNorm instead, because BatchNorm depends on the batch. (08)
